\title{Fluctuating active nematics account for layer formation in \textit{Myxococcus xanthus} colonies}

\author{Ashot Matevosyan}
\author{Ricard Alert}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
In colonies of the bacterium \textit{Myxococcus xanthus}, cells leave the monolayer and
form new layers, the first step toward the fruiting bodies that starving colonies build.
Experiments show that these layers form preferentially at $+1/2$ topological defects of
the cell orientation. However, the mean pressure at a defect falls three to four orders of
magnitude short of the pressure needed to extrude a cell, whereas the traction
measured around defects has a standard deviation about ten times its mean. We therefore propose that
layers form during large, and hence rare, pressure fluctuations, and we compute the statistics of these
fluctuations. Treating layer formation as nucleation gives a critical pressure, so the
layering probability is the probability that the pressure exceeds it. Pressure fluctuations depend on the active noise in the system, and in an active nematic the
fluctuation-dissipation theorem does not fix that noise. We derive it instead by
coarse-graining self-propelled rods that reverse their direction of motion, and we keep
the noise through Dean's equation. As a result, the reversals set the active stress and
the noise amplitude together. The pressure fluctuates more strongly close to a $+1/2$ defect, which enhances the layering probability there. The enhancement
peaks off the defect core and, once the stiffness of the monolayer is fitted, matches
the measured one.
\end{abstract}

\textit{Myxococcus xanthus} is a soil bacterium that lives and hunts in groups
\cite{munoz2016}. While nutrients last, the cells glide together across the substrate as
a swarm, a thin film that feeds on prey bacteria (\cref{fig:myxo_life}(A)). When the
nutrients run out, the swarm instead aggregates into mounds, and these grow into
fruiting bodies of $10^5$ to $10^6$ cells, in which some of the cells become spores
(\cref{fig:myxo_life}(B)). A fruiting body is built by stacking layers of cells on top of
one another, so its first step is the formation of a second layer on top of the
monolayer (red arrow in \cref{fig:myxo_life}). We ask how this first new layer forms.

The cells are rods $5$ to $7~\micron$ long and about $0.5~\micron$ wide that glide along their
long axis and reverse their direction every few minutes
\cite{wu2009periodic,copenhagen2021}. Although each cell moves in one direction at a
time, it keeps reversing, and neighboring rods interact in the same way whichever end
leads. At long time scales the monolayer is therefore an active nematic
\cite{marchetti2013hydrodynamics,doostmohammadi2018active}, and since the substrate takes
up the momentum of the cells through friction, it belongs to dry active matter, in which
momentum is not conserved \cite{marchetti2013hydrodynamics}. The local average axis of the cells, the
director, is undefined at isolated points called topological defects. Because the
director has no head or tail, it can rotate by any integer multiple of $\pi$ along a loop
around a defect, so nematic defects carry integer or half-integer charge
\cite{degennes1993physics,mermin1979topological}. The elastic energy of a defect grows as
the square of its charge, so a $+1$ defect lowers its energy by splitting into two $+1/2$
defects \cite{degennes1993physics,duclos2017topological}. Half-integer defects therefore
dominate in two-dimensional nematics
\cite{doostmohammadi2018active,giomi2013defect}, and \textit{M.\ xanthus} monolayers show
them too \cite{copenhagen2021}: the
comet-shaped $+1/2$ defect and the three-armed $-1/2$ defect (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a,b)).
In other living active nematics, defects are where cells leave the plane:
epithelial cells are extruded at $+1/2$ defects \cite{saw2017topological}, neural
progenitor cells accumulate there into three-dimensional mounds
\cite{kawaguchi2017topological}, and in colonies of \textit{Pseudomonas aeruginosa},
cells of a fast-moving mutant rotate upright and become trapped where two $+1/2$ defects collide
\cite{meacock2021bacteria}. In chaining \textit{Bacillus subtilis} biofilms, cells
instead lift off near $-1/2$ defects, where simulations show stress accumulating
\cite{yaman2019emergence}.

Copenhagen \textit{et al.}~imaged \textit{M.\ xanthus} monolayers on a nutrient-rich
substrate, where no fruiting bodies form, and still saw new layers and holes appear
throughout the colony \cite{copenhagen2021}. Under starvation, fruiting bodies develop so
fast that the emergence of new layers is hard to follow, whereas on nutrients the colony
stays a thin sheet in which single layer-formation events can be tracked. We expect the same layering mechanism to act when nutrients are scarce. Unlike in colonies that grow by cell division
\cite{grant2014,you2019mono}, these layers need no critical colony size; they are induced by
the migration of the cells \cite{copenhagen2021}. They form, however, about
$200$ times more often near $+1/2$ defects than far from them (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(c),
red), whereas holes open about $80$ times more often near $-1/2$ defects. Here we address
the layers at $+1/2$ defects.
Copenhagen \textit{et al.}~explained the preference for $+1/2$ defects with an active
nematic model in
which the friction with the substrate is weaker along the cells than across them
\cite{copenhagen2021}. In this model the active flow around a $+1/2$ defect runs along its
axis, from the tail through the core toward the head (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)). The
cells enter at the tail, where they lie along the flow, and leave at the head, where they
lie across it and feel a stronger friction, so the inflow toward the core is stronger than
the outflow. The model assumes that cells are readily extruded, so that the net inflow
becomes a new layer. The measured flows~\cite{copenhagen2021,han2025local} also
converge at $+1/2$ defects, but a converging
flow is what layer formation itself requires, since the cells that leave the monolayer must
be replaced \cite{han2025local}. The measured flows therefore cannot tell whether the inflow
causes the layers or follows from them. Moreover, extrusion has an energy cost, and the mean
pressure at a defect is of the order of
the active stress, three to four orders of magnitude below the pressure needed to
extrude a cell (\cref{sec:nucleation-1} and \cref{app:parameters-choice}).

The stress in these monolayers, however, fluctuates strongly. The divergence of this stress
is the traction that the cells exert on the substrate. Han \textit{et al.}~measured this
traction around $\pm1/2$ defects and found that its standard
deviation is about ten times its mean \cite{han2025local}. The fluctuations stay strong in
aligned regions away from defects, where the mean traction nearly vanishes. Han \textit{et
al.}~attributed these fluctuations
to local polar order, whose magnitude the cells tune through their reversal rate, and found
them stronger at $+1/2$ than at $-1/2$ defects. They proposed that the fluctuating polar
forces build up local pressure, which can trigger new layers, and noted that active nematic
theory, which explains the mean flows, must be extended to capture these fluctuations and
the stochasticity of layer formation. Here we provide such an extension. We propose that layers form
during large, and hence rare, pressure fluctuations that originate in the self-propulsion and reversals of
single cells: the mean pressure stays of the order of the active stress, whereas the
fluctuations of the pressure grow with the stiffness of the monolayer against compression,
so that during such a fluctuation the pressure can exceed the pressure needed to extrude a
cell (\cref{sec:nucleation-2}). We compute the statistics of these fluctuations around a
$+1/2$
defect.

To connect layer formation to the pressure, we treat it as nucleation. The pressure needed
to extrude a cell is then a critical pressure in the monolayer: above it, it becomes
energetically favorable to grow a new layer from a single extruded cell. This critical
pressure is set by cell-cell and cell-substrate adhesion, the surface tension of the water
film that wets the colony, and cell
size, so it does not depend on position. A defect can therefore favor layers only through
the statistics of the pressure around it, and the layering probability is the probability
that the local pressure exceeds the critical one. 
% Because the fluctuations decorrelate at
% essentially the same rate everywhere, the ratio of this probability near a defect to that
% far from it gives the ratio of the layering rates (\cref{sec:nucleation-2}).


This probability is set by the fluctuations of the pressure, and hence by the noise
that drives them. In equilibrium, the fluctuation-dissipation theorem fixes that noise by
tying it to the dissipative terms in the equations of motion \cite{kubo1966fluctuation}. An active nematic
is out of equilibrium, so the theorem does not apply, and phenomenological theories add the noise
to the continuum equations by hand, with a free amplitude and a chosen tensor structure
\cite{ramaswamy2003active}. We therefore derive the
noise from the dynamics of the cells. 

Kinetic theories derive continuum equations of
active matter from particle models, including self-propelled rods that reverse, but
they average the noise out on the way
\cite{bertin2006boltzmann,baskaran2008hydrodynamics,grossmann2016mesoscale,peshkov2012nonlinear}.
% We employ a similar approach to Dean's equation.
The Dean--Kawasaki
equation, an exact stochastic equation for the density of interacting Langevin particles,
keeps it instead \cite{dean1996,kawasaki1994stochastic,illien2025dean}. It has been
extended to run-and-tumble and
active Brownian particles \cite{tailleur2008statistical,solon2015active} and to apolar
active nematics of particles that step back and forth at random and align with their
neighbors as in the Vicsek model \cite{bertin2013}. 
% There, the noise is derived from the motion between collisions, and the deterministic part of the equation for the nematic order from a Boltzmann equation \cite{bertin2013}. 
We extend the Dean--Kawasaki equation
to rods that interact through a pair potential and reverse at a rate $\frev$, as
\textit{M.\ xanthus} does (\cref{fig:model-micro}), and then take moments of their
distribution over orientations, so that the noise and the deterministic terms follow from the same
coarse-graining.

The result is a pair of stochastic equations for the density and the nematic order
parameter. Self-propulsion and reversals together drive an extensile active stress. The active stress
coefficient grows with the distance a cell glides between reversals, and the same
coefficient sets the amplitude of the density noise. Neither can therefore be tuned
without the other. The coarse-graining also fixes
how this noise depends on the local nematic order.
Fluctuating cell forces are also known to produce extensile stress
in cell monolayers~\cite{vafa2021fluctuations,killeen2022polar}, and for reversing rods
Banerjee \textit{et al.}~\cite{banerjee2026active} derived the active stress in the same
form as ours.

Solved around a $+1/2$ defect, these equations give a density variance, and hence, through
the stiffness of the monolayer, a pressure variance. The defect changes this variance only
slightly, but since layers form
in the far tail of the pressure distribution, this small change is enough to make layers
form much more often near the defect. The variance is largest just
off the core, at the head of the defect, on the side where the director bends around it (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)). 
In unpublished observations, the authors of Refs.~\cite{copenhagen2021,han2025local} also
find that layering starts more often at the head of the defect (private communication),
as in the example of \cref{fig:defects-layering}(b). 
Besides the radial profile of
\cref{fig:defects-layering}(c), the predicted layering probability therefore has an angular
profile around the defect, but there is no quantitative measurement of it.
Fluctuations of
microscopic origin thus suffice to explain where layers form, although the calculation
does not exclude other mechanisms.

This paper is organized as follows. The next section describes layer formation as
nucleation, which sets a critical pressure, and reduces the
layering probability to the statistics of the pressure.
\Cref{sec:model} then derives
the stochastic equations from the microscopic model, and \cref{sec:defect} solves them
at a $+1/2$ defect, first for the steady state and then for the fluctuations. With the
pressure variance in hand, \cref{sec:nucleation-2} computes the layering probability and
compares it with the measurements of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}. 
Finally,
\cref{sec:discussion} summarizes what is fitted and what is predicted, lists the
simplifications of the model, discusses the reversal rate as a control parameter, and proposes experimental tests.
The appendices give all derivations in detail, so that the paper is self-contained.

\section{Layer formation as a nucleation event}
\label{sec:nucleation-1}
% Mechanism and motivation. The calculation resumes in \cref{sec:nucleation-2} once
% \cref{sec:model,sec:defect} supply the pressure variance. Assembled 2026-09-25 from
% Ashot's draft, the energetics and threshold parts of the former sec:nucleation-old
% (dean.tex 1284-1390), and Report 9 (R. Alert, 2020).
% Rewritten 2026-09-28 as one narrative without subsections: defect compression ->

We model layer formation with nucleation theory: new layers are formed when growing one from a single cell no longer costs energy. First, we calculate the energy cost of a new layer.

We assume that cells adhere both to the substrate and
to other cells, and that water completely wets the colony. Let $\Wc$ and $\Ws$ be the
cell-cell and cell-substrate adhesion energies per unit area, and $\gammaw$ the surface
tension of water. Suppose a patch of cells of area $A$ and perimeter $\ell$ forms a new
layer (\cref{fig:nucleation}(a)). The cells of the patch need to (\textit{i}) detach from the
substrate, (\textit{ii}) detach from their neighbors, and (\textit{iii}) stretch the
surface of the water film over the edge of the patch. This costs $\Ws A + \Wc\ell h +
\gammaw\ell h$, where $h$ is the height of the
monolayer. At the same time, the bottom layer relaxes to fill the space the patch left;
cells move in from the outer boundary of the monolayer, which retracts, so the substrate
contact
that the patch lost is not regained (\cref{fig:nucleation}(a)).
The energy gain comes from (\textit{iv}) the adhesion of the patch to the cells beneath it and (\textit{v})
the work done by the pressure, $\Wc A + pA$. Here $p$ is the two-dimensional pressure of
the monolayer, a force per unit length. \Cref{app:extrusion} derives $pA$ from the
elastic energy the bottom layer releases.

The cell-cell adhesion $\Wc$ enters twice with opposite signs: as a cost on
the side faces and as a gain on the bottom face. For a circular patch, $\ell=\sqrt{4\pi A}$, and the energy cost of the new layer is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dE-of-A}
    \Delta E(A) = (\Ws-\Wc-p)\,A + (\Wc+\gammaw)\,h\sqrt{4\pi A},
\end{equation}
which is a function of the patch area $A$ and monolayer pressure $p$.

The perimeter term is a cost and grows as $\sqrt A$. The area term grows as $A$ and is a
gain once $p>\Ws-\Wc$. At such pressures, $\Delta E(A)$ rises at small $A$, reaches a
maximum and then falls. This is the barrier of classical nucleation theory (\cref{fig:nucleation}(b)). Its maximum
is the critical nucleus, of area $A^*=\pi h^2(\Wc+\gammaw)^2/(p+\Wc-\Ws)^2$. A larger patch
lowers its energy by growing, a smaller one by shrinking.

In classical nucleation theory, thermal fluctuations or impurities in the system move $A$ along a fixed curve $\Delta E(A)$.
In our system, because of active fluctuations, the pressure fluctuates. The barrier and the critical nucleus area $A^*$ move with it (\cref{fig:nucleation}(b)).

Thermal energy is
negligible against the adhesion and surface energies of even one cell. Cells glide in the
plane of the substrate, and nothing lifts a group of them off it together. A new layer
must therefore start from a single cell, as already observed in the experiments~\cite{copenhagen2021,han2025local}. If the
critical nucleus is smaller than one cell, $A^*<\Acell$, growth from that cell is downhill
in energy. Single-cell perturbations, such as substrate roughness or a division event,
then trigger the extrusion of that first cell. We do not model them and assume that they
act as soon as $A^*<\Acell$. The discreteness of 
the system thus sets the nucleation condition,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:nucleation-condition}
    \Delta E'(A)\big|_{A=\Acell} = 0,
\end{equation}
or $A^*=\Acell$. Solved for the pressure, it gives the critical pressure
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pcrit}
    \pcrit = (\Ws-\Wc) + \sqrt{\frac{\pi h^2}{\Acell}}\,(\Wc+\gammaw).
\end{equation}
So layers are formed only if the pressure in the monolayer exceeds $\pcrit$
(\cref{fig:nucleation}(b)), and the probability of layer formation at a point $\r$ is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:p-layer-def}
 \calP(\r) \propto \mathbb P\bigl(p(\r)>\pcrit\bigr).
\end{equation}
The probability on the right is the fraction of time during which $p(\r)>\pcrit$;
\cref{sec:nucleation-2} turns it into a rate.
\Cref{eq:p-layer-def} treats $\pcrit$ as a trigger: once the pressure exceeds it, a cell
leaves the monolayer. This requires the pressure to change slowly compared with the time a
cell takes to leave, because escape over a fluctuating barrier follows the instantaneous
barrier only in this slow limit~\cite{reimann1995thermally}. When the barrier changes fast, the crossing sees the
average barrier instead~\cite{doering1992resonant}. We assume the slow
limit throughout and compare it with the time scales of the model in
\cref{sec:defect-fluct}.

With the parameter values of \cref{app:parameters-extrusion},
$\pcrit=35~\kPa\tcdot\micron$. The surface tension of water dominates it. With
$\gammaw=65~\kPa\tcdot\micron$, the measured surface tension of the growth
medium~\cite{bourque2025active}, the second term of \cref{eq:pcrit} contributes
$33~\kPa\tcdot\micron$. The mean pressure in the monolayer is far below $\pcrit$: at a
defect it is of the order of the active stress, three to four orders of magnitude below
$\pcrit$ (\cref{app:parameters-choice}). Layers therefore form predominantly during large, and
hence rare, fluctuations of the pressure.

The nucleus is idealized as a disc of one cell area, which keeps the nucleation
condition independent of the orientation of the rod-shaped cells, and the pressure is taken
at a point rather than averaged over a cell. Other choices would mainly shift $\pcrit$ or the
width of the pressure fluctuations. The layering profile depends on the two only through
their ratio (\cref{sec:nucleation-2}), so such shifts are absorbed by the one parameter that
we fit, the stiffness of the monolayer against compression.
% 2026-11-01, author's prompt ("forward reference I would avoid"): the bulk modulus is named in
% words, without the forward reference to eq:free-energy-choice.

\Cref{eq:pcrit} contains the cell size and the adhesion and surface energies. It
contains no property of the fluctuations and does not depend on position. A defect can
therefore favor layers only by changing the distribution of the pressure and its fluctuations around it, which
makes the layering probability inhomogeneous.

In the following sections we set up a microscopic model of the monolayer and calculate the pressure fluctuations around a defect. Then, in \cref{sec:nucleation-2}, we compute the spatial profile of the layering probability \eqref{eq:p-layer-def}.

\subsection{The microscopic model}
\label{sec:model-micro}
% dean.tex 109-173 ("Microscopic equations of motion").
We start from Langevin equations for $N$ overdamped, self-propelled rods
\cite{baskaran2008hydrodynamics,grossmann2016mesoscale}, each with position $\r_i$ and orientation $\theta_i$ (\cref{fig:model-micro}):
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:model}
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:langevin-position}
    \frac{\d\r_i}{\d t} &= \frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F_i + v_0\,\p(\theta_i) k_i,
    \\
    \label{eq:langevin-angle}
    \frac{\d\theta_i}{\d t} &= \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma_i + \lambda_i,
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
with $\p(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ the unit vector along the rod axis. Here $\xi_0/\rho_0$ and $\xir/\rho_0$ are
translational and rotational friction coefficients, respectively ($\rho_0$ a reference
density
carried along for later convenience), and $\lambda_i$ is Gaussian white
noise, $\la\lambda_i(t)\lambda_j(t')\ra=2\Lambda\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t')$, with $\Lambda$
the rotational diffusion coefficient. The rods
interact through a pairwise potential $V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')$, so that
$V_\mathrm{tot}=\tfrac12\sum_{i,j=1}^N V(\r_i-\r_j,\theta_i,\theta_j)$. The force and
torque on rod $i$ are the values at $(\r_i,\theta_i)$ of the fields
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:force-torque}
    \F(\r,\theta) &= -\pd{}{\r}\sum_{j=1}^N V(\r-\r_j,\theta,\theta_j),
    \nonumber\\
    \Gamma(\r,\theta) &= -\pd{}{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^N V(\r-\r_j,\theta,\theta_j).
\end{align}
The sums run over all $N$ rods, including $j=i$,\footnote{Including $j=i$ lets us write the
force and torque in \cref{sec:model-coarse} as gradients of a single functional of the density
of all rods, \cref{eq:pair-free-energy}.} so that the same two fields act on every
rod. The self term adds no force or torque: a pair potential between identical rods
satisfies
$V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')=V(\r'-\r,\theta',\theta)$, and at $\r=\r'$ rotational
invariance leaves it a function of $\theta-\theta'$ alone. Both derivatives in
\cref{eq:force-torque} therefore vanish on the $j=i$ term.

The second term of \cref{eq:langevin-position} is self-propulsion at speed $v_0$ along
the rod axis. Here $k_i$ is a dichotomous noise: $k_i=\pm1$ with
equal probability, switching at rate $\frev$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:k-dichotomous}
    \la k_i(t)k_j(t')\ra = \delta_{ij}\,\ee^{-2\frev|t-t'|}.
\end{equation}
This models the reversals of \textit{Myxococcus xanthus} gliding motility
\cite{blackhart1985frizzy,guzzo2018gated}. At time scales long compared with $\frev^{-1}$,
we approximate $k_i$ by Gaussian white noise,
$\la k_i(t)k_j(t')\ra \approx \frev^{-1}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t')$. The coarse-graining of
\cref{sec:model-coarse} uses It\^o calculus and therefore requires this approximation.
Refs.~\cite{bressloff2024global,spinney2025dean} derive
density equations for particles that switch between discrete states, as our rods do at
each reversal, without taking this limit.

The dichotomous noise also simplifies the reversals themselves, because it makes them a
Poisson process. The measured intervals between reversals are more
regular~\cite{wu2009periodic}, since they come from a gated oscillator with a refractory
period, a time after each reversal during which the cell cannot reverse
again~\cite{guzzo2018gated}. Both simplifications could be lifted by keeping the direction of
motion of each cell as a dynamical variable~\cite{spinney2025dean}, together with the
time since its last reversal. We keep both as deliberate simplifications, because the
white-noise limit closes the theory on the density and the nematic order
(\cref{sec:model-closure}).


Self-propulsion is a multiplicative noise. The noise is $k_i$, and the amplitude it
multiplies, $v_0\p(\theta_i)$, is set by the orientation of the rod. This amplitude depends
only on $\theta_i$, which $k_i$ does not drive, so the It\^o and Stratonovich readings of
\cref{eq:model} coincide (\cref{app:dean-approach}). The term $v_0\p(\theta_i)k_i$ is also
the only noise in \cref{eq:langevin-position}, since the passive transport by the pair
force has no noise of its own. 
% Coarse-graining with It\^o calculus
% leaves a deterministic second-order term, proportional to $\p\p$, alongside the
% fluctuation, and both are set by the same combination $v_0^2/\frev$. The anisotropic part
% of $\p\p$ yields the active force density of \cref{eq:model-rho}; the isotropic part yields
% a diffusion, negligible next to the pressure-driven relaxation of the density
% (\cref{app:dean-angle}). The fluctuation enters \cref{eq:model-rho} as the density noise.

\subsection{Coarse-graining that preserves fluctuations}
\label{sec:model-coarse}
% dean.tex 174-359 ("Dean's approach").
We take Dean's approach. Dean's equation \cite{dean1996} is an \emph{exact} field-theoretic Langevin equation for
the density of interacting Langevin processes. Our model \eqref{eq:model} adds an
orientation, a self-propulsion and a reversal noise. The same derivation then gives an
exact equation for the joint density over the state space $(\r,\theta)$; the only
approximation it relies on is the Gaussian white-noise limit taken in the previous subsection.

Write the single-particle density $\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta) =
\delta(\r-\r_i(t))\,\delta(\theta-\theta_i(t))$, and let
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:joint-density-definition}
  \varrho_t(\r,\theta)=\rho_0^{-1}\sum_i\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta)
\end{align}
be the joint density
over $(\r,\theta)$, made dimensionless by $\rho_0$. The interaction is
pairwise, so the force and torque fields of \cref{eq:force-torque} are gradients of a
single
functional of $\varrho_t$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pair-free-energy}
    \calF[\varrho] = \frac{\rho_0^2}2\!\int\!\d\r\d\r'\,\d\theta\d\theta'\,
    V(\r\!-\!\r'\!,\theta,\theta')\,\varrho(\r,\theta)\,\varrho(\r'\!,\theta'),
\end{equation}
namely the fields of \cref{eq:force-torque} can be written as
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:force-torque-F}
  \F=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\r\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho ,
  \qquad
  \Gamma=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\theta\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho.
\end{align}
Applying It\^o's lemma
to $\varrho^{(i)}_t$ and summing over $i$ then gives a closed equation for $\varrho_t$
alone (\cref{app:dean-approach}),
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:final-langevin}
    \begin{aligned}
    \partial_t\varrho_t(\r,\theta) = {}&
    \frac1{\xi_0}\pd{}{\r}\tcdot\left(\varrho_t\pd{}{\r}\frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho}\right)
    + \frac1{\xir}\pd{}{\theta}\!\left(\varrho_t\pd{}{\theta}\frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho}\right)
    \\
    &+ \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha(\theta)p_\beta(\theta)\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
    + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{\theta^2}
    \\
    &- \pd{}{\r}\tcdot\left(\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p(\theta)\,k(\r,\theta,t)\right)
    \\
    &- \pd{}{\theta}\!\left(\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda(\r,\theta,t)\right).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Its second line comes from the second-order derivatives in It\^o's lemma.
The last two lines of \cref{eq:final-langevin} carry two noise fields, $k(\r,\theta,t)$ and
$\lambda(\r,\theta,t)$. Both are Gaussian white noises in position, orientation and
time,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:noise-correlators}
    \la k(\r,\theta,t)\,k(\r',\theta',t')\ra &= \frac{v_0^2}{\frev\rho_0}\;\Delta,
    \nonumber\\
    \la \lambda(\r,\theta,t)\,\lambda(\r',\theta',t')\ra &= \frac{2\Lambda}{\rho_0}\;\Delta,
\end{align}
with $\Delta\equiv\delta(t-t')\delta(\r-\r')\delta(\theta-\theta')$, and they are
uncorrelated with each other. Their amplitudes are inherited from the reversal noise
$k_i$ and the rotational noise $\lambda_i$ of \cref{eq:model}.
\Cref{app:dean-approach} derives that relation, together with the multiplicative factor
$\sqrt{\varrho_t}$ that accompanies both fields.


\Cref{eq:final-langevin} is nonlinear even without interactions. The noise is
multiplicative: where there are no rods, $\varrho_t$ cannot fluctuate. The product
$\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,k$ is an It\^o product, because \cref{eq:final-langevin} follows from
It\^o's lemma: $\varrho_t$ is evaluated before each noise increment \cite{dean1996}.
Because each realization of $k$ and $\lambda$ gives one random history of $\varrho_t$, the
statistics of the density, and of the pressure in \cref{sec:nucleation-1}, follow from
\cref{eq:final-langevin} directly.

Continuum theories usually work instead with the noise-averaged density. Averaging
\cref{eq:final-langevin} over the noise gives an equation for the mean density that
contains the pair correlation. The equation for the pair correlation contains the
three-point correlation, and so on \cite{dean1996}. Dynamical density functional theory
closes this hierarchy with an equilibrium free energy
\cite{marconi1999dynamic,tevrugt2020classical}, and kinetic theories of self-propelled
rods close the analogous hierarchy for the one-rod distribution
\cite{baskaran2008hydrodynamics}. Both give deterministic equations for the
ensemble-averaged density, because the fluctuations are averaged out before the closure.
The noise drops out of the equation for the mean, but not of \cref{eq:final-langevin},
which is the equation for one realization.

The joint density $\varrho_t$ is a sum of delta functions, an empirical density.
Strictly speaking, \cref{eq:final-langevin} is derived only for such generalized functions, and
rigorous results for it are few
\cite{konarovskyi2019dean,konarovskyi2020dean,cornalba2023dean}. However, it becomes
well-posed if the noise is correlated in space \cite{fehrman2024well}, or if each delta
function is replaced by a kernel of finite width \cite{cornalba2019regularized}.
A density coarse-grained over a finite region or time window also obeys a stochastic
equation \cite{archer2004dynamical}. Many
applications still use Dean's equation for smooth densities without a rigorous proof
\cite{kruger2017gaussian,demery2016conductivity,dean2014relaxation,demery2014generalized},
including the fluctuating theories of active particles
\cite{tailleur2008statistical,solon2015active,bertin2013}. We, too, assume that
\cref{eq:final-langevin} holds for a smooth $\varrho_t$, and use it in this form from
\cref{sec:model-closure} on.


\subsection{Free energy, closure, and the stochastic field equations}
\label{sec:model-closure}
The joint density $\varrho_t$ carries far more information than we need. The rest of the
paper works with its lowest angular moments, the fluctuating density and nematic order
parameter,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:density-definition}
    \rho(\r,t) = \int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta),
    \quad
    \Q(\r,t) = \int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)\,\hat\Q(\theta),
\end{align}
with $\hat\Q(\theta)=2\p(\theta)\p(\theta)-\I$ the single-rod nematic tensor.%
\footnote{%
The conventional nematic order parameter is normalized by the local density,
$\Q=\rho^{-1}\!\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\hat\Q$, and is bounded by unity. We use the
unnormalized moment \cref{eq:density-definition} instead; the two differ only at order
$\rho-1$, and a large bulk modulus keeps $\rho-1$ small (\cref{app:dean-normalisation}).}
In this subsection we reduce \cref{eq:final-langevin} to a closed pair of stochastic
equations for these two fields.

We do not know the interbacterial potential
${V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')}$ in any detail, so we do not attempt to evaluate
\cref{eq:pair-free-energy} from it. Instead we replace \cref{eq:pair-free-energy} by a
coarse-grained free energy $\calF$ that depends on
$\varrho$ only through the two moments \cref{eq:density-definition},
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:free-energy-parametrisation}
    \calF[\varrho] = F[\rho,\Q] = \int\!\d\r\;\mathfrak f\bigl(\rho(\r),\Q(\r),\partial_\r\Q(\r)\bigr),
\end{align}
which is both a physical assumption and a choice of parametrization. It gives a chain
rule,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:chain-rule}
    \frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}
    = \frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho(\r)}
    + \frac{\delta F}{\delta\Q(\r)}\!:\!\hat\Q(\theta).
\end{align}
This truncation of the $\theta$ dependence to a single harmonic is what makes the
interaction terms of \cref{eq:final-langevin} integrate over $\theta$ to the two moments
alone in the equation for the density (\cref{app:dean-angle}).

For the free energy density $\mathfrak f$ we take the sum of a compressibility penalty
and a Landau--de Gennes
nematic term,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:free-energy-choice}
    \mathfrak f(\rho,\Q,\partial_\r\Q) = {}& \frac B2(\rho-1)^2 + \frac a2(\Q\!:\!\Q)
    + \frac b4(\Q\!:\!\Q)^2
    \nonumber\\
    &+ \frac K2(\partial_\r\Q)\!:\!(\partial_\r\Q).
\end{align}
The first term enforces near-incompressibility of the monolayer with a large bulk
modulus $B$; the rest is the standard nematic free energy, with $a<0$ favoring order and $K$ penalizing distortion. The single $K$ is the one-constant approximation of the Frank free energy, in which the splay and bend moduli of the two-dimensional nematic are taken equal \cite{degennes1993physics,frank1958liquid}.

The first term also sets the pressure of the monolayer. In an incompressible monolayer the
pressure is the Lagrange multiplier of the incompressibility constraint, and at large $B$
the first term takes over the role of that constraint. We therefore take the pressure from
this term alone, $p=\rho\,\partial_\rho\mathfrak f_B-\mathfrak f_B$ with $\mathfrak
f_B=\tfrac B2(\rho-1)^2$,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:pressure-definition}
  p=\frac B2\left(\rho^2-1\right)\simeq B(\rho-1),
\end{align}
where the last form is the leading order in $\rho-1$.
This is the two-dimensional pressure $p$ of \cref{eq:dE-of-A} (\cref{app:extrusion}).

Integrating \cref{eq:final-langevin} over $\theta$ and keeping the leading terms in
$1/B$ at fixed pressure $B(\rho-1)$ (\cref{app:dean-angle}) gives a continuity equation for
the density,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:model-rho}
  \partial_t\rho = -\nabla\tcdot\Bigl[
    \frac1{\xi_0}\bigl(-B\nabla\rho + \Q\!:\!\nabla\H + \fa\bigr)
    + \bEta_{\rho}(\r,t)\Bigr].
\end{align}
The first two terms in the bracket are passive, inherited from the pair potential through
the free energy. The term $-B\nabla\rho$ resists compression, and the elastic force
$\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$ carries cells along gradients of the nematic force field
$\H(\r)=-\delta F/\delta\Q= -a\Q - b(\Q\!:\!\Q)\Q + K\nabla^2\Q$ \cite{degennes1993physics}. The third term is the active force density
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:active-force}
  f^\text{a}_\alpha(\r) = -\zeta\,\partial_\beta Q_\ab(\r)
  \quad\text{with}\quad
  \zeta = \frac{\xi_0v_0^2}{4\frev},
\end{align}
the divergence of the active nematic stress $-\zeta\Q$. Positive $\zeta$ denotes extensile active stress.
The coefficient comes entirely from self-propulsion and reversal, so fast-reversing colonies are less active.\footnote{For reversing rods that also diffuse rotationally,
Refs.~\cite{grossmann2016mesoscale,banerjee2026active} find
$\zeta={\xi_0v_0^2}/{(4\frev+2\Lambda)}$. This coefficient reduces to that of
\cref{eq:active-force} when the cells reverse much faster than they turn, $\frev\gg\Lambda$,
the limit of fast reversals on which the white-noise approximation of
\cref{sec:model-micro} relies.}
%  \todo{$\Lambda/\frev=1.3$ with the parameters of App.~A.2:
% separate the white-noise simplification (valid for large $\frev$) from the parameters taken
% from experiment.}
Unlike the passive terms, $\fa$ does not derive from the free energy $F$. Since
$\fa\propto\nabla\!\cdot\!\Q$, it vanishes in a uniform nematic field and is largest where the
field bends fastest, at a defect core (\cref{sec:defect-steady}).

The last term is the noise of the density flux,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-def}
  \bEta_{\rho}(\r,t) = \int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t(\r,\theta)}\,
  \p(\theta)\,k(\r,\theta,t).
\end{equation}
Its autocorrelation follows from
\cref{eq:noise-correlators},
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation}
  \la \eta^{\rho}_\alpha(\r,t) \,\eta^{\rho}_\beta(\r',t') \ra
  \\
  =
  \frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \left[Q_\ab(\r)+\rho(\r) \delta_\ab\right]
 \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t') ,
\end{multline}
an isotropic piece proportional to $\rho$ and an anisotropic piece proportional to $\Q$.
Both scale with $\zeta$ of \cref{eq:active-force}, so the noise strength is set by the
same reversal rate $\frev$ and self-propulsion speed $v_0$ as the active force. 
The noise $\bEta_{\rho}$ sits inside the
divergence, so $\int\!\d\r\,\rho$ is constant in every realization of the noise, not only
in the mean.
The
rotational noise does not appear in \cref{eq:model-rho}: every term it produces is a $\theta$ derivative, and
$\int\!\d\theta\,\partial_\theta(\cdot)=0$.

% equation, including the elastic part"): the force balance is back here, with Q:grad H.
Writing the flux of the continuity equation \cref{eq:model-rho},
$\partial_t\rho+\nabla\tcdot\bs j=0$, as $\bs j=\rho\v$ defines the velocity field $\v(\r,t)$
of the cells, and setting $\rho\approx1$ in the incompressible limit gives the
force balance
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:velocity}
  \xi_0 \v = -B\nabla\rho + \Q\!:\!\nabla\H + \fa + \xi_0 \bEta_{\rho}.
\end{align}
The friction force density $\xi_0\v$ on the cells is balanced by the pressure gradient
$B\nabla\rho\simeq\nabla p$ of \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, the elastic force, the active
force and the noise. The elastic force is the divergence of the nematic stresses, see \cref{app:pressure}. 
% Only the pressure of \cref{eq:pressure-definition}
% enters the layering probability \cref{eq:p-layer-def}, because these nematic stresses stay
% far below the fluctuations of the pressure (\cref{app:pressure}).

We also need a dynamical equation for the nematic order parameter $\Q$. Multiplying
\cref{eq:final-langevin} by $\hat\Q(\theta)$ and integrating over $\theta$ does not close
the system: angular moments like
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho=\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,\hat\Q\hat\Q$ appear, and $\rho$ and
$\Q$ alone do not determine them. We use
the lowest-order maximum-entropy closure,
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho \simeq \rho\,\J$
with $\J$ the rank-four identity on symmetric traceless tensors. The terms it drops are of
order $S^2$ relative to those it keeps, where $S=\sqrt{(\Q\!:\!\Q)/2}$ is the strength of
the nematic order. \Cref{app:closure} derives the
higher-order terms and the limitations of this closure.
The closure truncates the hierarchy of angular moments within one realization of the
noise, so the closed equations for $\rho$ and $\Q$ keep the noise of
\cref{eq:final-langevin}.

With the closure in place we obtain an equation for $\Q$ with two noises, $\bEta^\Q$ and
$\f^\Q$,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:model-Q}
  \partial_t \Q = {}& -\nabla\!\cdot\!\Bigl[\frac1{\xi_0}
    \bigl(-\Q B \nabla\rho + \nabla \H\bigr) + \bEta^\Q\Bigr]
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac4{\xir} \Ha + \f^\Q.
\end{align}
Here $\Ha$ is the
nematic force field with coefficients renormalized by the rotational noise and the activity,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:H-active}
  \Ha &= -a'\Q - b(\Q\!:\!\Q)\Q + K'\nabla^2\Q,
  \nonumber\\
  a' &= a+\Lambda\xir, \qquad K' = K+{\zeta\xir}/({4\xi_0}).
\end{align}
Like $\H=-\delta F/\delta\Q$, the field $\Ha$ is minus the functional derivative of the effective free energy, which is $F$ with $a'$ and $K'$ in place of $a$ and
$K$. The term $4\Ha/\xir$ relaxes $\Q$ toward a minimizer of the effective
free energy, so it is $a'$ and $K'$, not the bare $a$ and $K$, that set the defect profile
solved for in \cref{sec:defect-steady}.

The passive terms in the flux of $\Q$ have the same origin as those of \cref{eq:model-rho}.
By \cref{eq:chain-rule}, the interaction term of \cref{eq:final-langevin} moves a rod of
orientation $\theta$ with the velocity $\xi_0^{-1}[-B\nabla\rho+\hat\Q(\theta)\!:\!\nabla\H]$.
Its first part comes from the pressure gradient, which pushes every rod alike, and its second
part from the elastic force, which depends on the orientation of the rod. Summed over the
rods, this velocity gives the passive flux of the density in \cref{eq:model-rho}, and weighted
with $\hat\Q$ it gives the passive flux of $\Q$ in \cref{eq:model-Q}. The pressure gradient
thus carries the alignment of the rods through $\Q B\nabla\rho$, and the elastic force
carries it through $\nabla\H$.

The two noises of \cref{eq:model-Q} enter differently. $\bEta^\Q$ is a flux, the
rank-three tensor
\begin{equation}
\eta^\Q_{\ab\gamma}=\int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\hat
Q_\ab(\theta)p_\gamma(\theta)k(\r,\theta,t),
\end{equation}
with $\gamma$ the direction of the flux.
Both $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$ carry the same
reversal noise $k(\r,\theta,t)$, so the two are correlated. The
second noise $\f^\Q$ is local and non-conservative. It carries the rotational noise
$\lambda(\r,\theta,t)$ alone and is uncorrelated with $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$. It is
the only noise that changes the orientation of the rods.
\Cref{app:dean-angle} gives their correlators.

The reversal noise enters the equation for $\Q$ although a reversal does not change the
nematic tensor $\hat\Q$ of a rod. It enters through the transport of alignment: each rod
carries its alignment as it runs along its axis, and $\bEta^\Q$ is the random part of this
transport, as $\bEta_\rho$ is the random part of the transport of the rods. Like
$\bEta_\rho$, it sits inside a divergence, so it moves alignment between neighboring points
and leaves $\int\!\d\r\,\Q$ unchanged in every realization. Such a conserved noise is weak at
long wavelengths, and Ref.~\cite{bertin2013} discarded it as irrelevant. We keep it, because it
comes from the same reversals as $\bEta_\rho$ and is correlated with it, and because layers
form at the scale of a single cell, at short wavelengths.

The same equations follow from a theory that keeps the direction of motion of each cell as a
variable (\cref{sec:model-micro}). Such a theory has a third field, the local polar order
$\bs P(\r,t)=\rho_0^{-1}\sum_ik_i\,\p(\theta_i)\,\delta(\r-\r_i(t))$, with $k_i=\pm1$ the
dichotomous variable of \cref{eq:k-dichotomous}, and $\bs P$ has an equation of its
own~\cite{baskaran2008hydrodynamics,banerjee2026active,spinney2025dean}. Reversals relax it at
the rate $2\frev$, and splay and bend of the director drive it. In the white-noise limit (large $\frev$),
$\bs P$ follows $\rho$ and $\Q$ instantaneously, and eliminating it gives back
\cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q}. The mean of $\bs P$ contains the polarity
$\bs P_Q=-(v_0/4\frev)\nabla\tcdot\Q$ that splay and bend induce, and the active force is the
friction force of this polarity, $\fa=\xi_0v_0\bs P_Q$. The fluctuation of $\bs P$ about its
mean gives the density noise, $\bEta_\rho=v_0(\bs P-\la\bs P\ra)$.

\label{sec:model-elastic}

With the elastic force, the lowest-order closure makes \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} ill
posed: in \cref{app:closure} we show that they become antidiffusive wherever the nematic
order is strong, $S>1/\sqrt2$. To resolve this consistently, we drop the elastic part of
the force on a rod. By \cref{eq:langevin-position,eq:force-torque-F,eq:chain-rule,eq:free-energy-choice},
this force is $\F_i = \F(\r_i,\theta_i) =\rho_0^{-1} \bigl(- B \nabla\rho +
\hat\Q(\theta_i)\!:\!\nabla\H\bigr)$, and its second, elastic part gives the $\nabla\H$
terms of the passive fluxes in both equations (\cref{sec:model-closure}). Dropping it
therefore removes them at once: $\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$ from the flux of the density and $\nabla\H$
from the flux of $\Q$. The pressure becomes the only passive stress, and the force balance
\cref{eq:velocity} loses its elastic term.

Both $\nabla\H$ terms are usually dropped in
continuum models of dry active
nematics~\cite{ramaswamy2003active,bertin2013,mishra2010dynamic,ngo2014large,putzig2014phase,shankar2018low},
including those of \textit{M.\ xanthus} monolayers~\cite{copenhagen2021,han2025local}.
The usual argument is that, in those models, the nematic force field vanishes in the
ordered state, so these terms enter at higher order in gradients than the active
ones~\cite{shankar2018low,srivastava2016negative}. Here, in contrast, the closure forces
the simplification on us.

\Cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q}, without their $\nabla\H$ terms, are the fluctuating active
nematic equations this paper solves. Activity enters them in three places: the force $\fa$,
the amplitude of the reversal noises $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$, and the renormalization of
the nematic force field $\Ha$ in \cref{eq:H-active}. The transport terms leave the shape of a
defect, which $\Ha$ sets, almost unchanged, but they couple the density and the nematic field
in both directions, through $\fa\propto\nabla\!\cdot\!\Q$ in one equation and through
$\Q B\nabla\rho$ in the other. This coupling sets a $+1/2$ defect in motion
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}).

Everything that follows comes from solving \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} at a $+1/2$
defect, with the parameters collected in \cref{app:parameters}.

Solving \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} at a $+1/2$ defect takes three steps: find the
 steady state of the noiseless equations (\cref{sec:defect-steady}); linearize the stochastic equations around it; and solve the

The main text
gives analytical solutions of simplified

\subsection{The steady state}
\label{sec:defect-steady}
% Pages ~1.5. dean.tex 705-747, 819-894, 2460-2597 (solver moved to appendix, results summarised here).
The density and nematic order fields are cross-coupled in \cref{eq:model-Q}. This sets the
$+1/2$ defect in motion (see \cref{app:comoving}).
We first solve a simplified system: dropping the cross-coupling term in the $\Q$-equation
leaves a static problem:
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:simplified-noiseless}
  0 = \nabla\tcdot\left[B\nabla\rho - \fa\right] , \qquad \Ha = 0.
\end{align}
We use polar coordinates $(r,\phi)$ centered on the core, with $\phi$ measured from the
$+x$ axis, the symmetry axis of the defect, which points from its head to its tail
(\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)). At a $+1/2$ defect the director then makes the
angle $\theta=\phi/2$ with this axis. The solution of the second equation is given by a Pad\'{e} approximation \cite{pismen1999vortices,pismen2006patterns}:
\begin{align}
  \Q(r,\phi) = S(r)\hat\Q(\phi/2),
  \qquad
  S(r) = S_0 f_\pade(r/\elld),
  \label{eq:Q-pade}\\
  \label{eq:pade-approximation}
  f_\pade(x) = x \sqrt{\frac{0.34 + 0.07 x^2}{1 + 0.41 x^2+0.07 x^4}},
  \\
  S_0=\sqrt{-a'/(2b)}, \qquad \elld=\sqrt{K'/(-a')}.
  \label{eq:S0-elld}
\end{align}
Two scales appear. The far-field nematic order $S_0$ is the value $S(r)$
saturates to, and the core size $\elld$ is the distance over which $S$
recovers from $S(0)=0$ at the defect core. Both are built from the renormalized $a'$
and $K'$ of \cref{eq:H-active}, not from the bare coefficients of the free energy
density \eqref{eq:free-energy-choice}. We take $S_0=0.9$ and $\elld=1~\micron$, close to the
direct measurement of Ref.~\cite{han2025local} (\cref{app:parameters-choice}).

Now, the first equation in \cref{eq:simplified-noiseless} is a Poisson equation for the density.
Its solution needs a boundary condition, and that condition is a
choice. Without a boundary, the density would not return to one far from the core. No boundary at a finite distance describes a defect in an extended colony, and none
of the possible boundaries is more physical than the others. We take the boundary to be a
reservoir: on a circle of radius $R$ we impose $\rho=1$, as if the region inside the
boundary were embedded in a monolayer held at the density that incompressibility imposes.
We take $R=20~\micron$, comparable to the nematic correlation length 
measured in Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}, beyond which the director field of one defect
gives way to that of others.
With this condition, the Poisson equation has the exact solution
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-analytic}
  \rho(r,\phi) = 1 - \frac{\zeta}{B}\left[ S(r) - \frac rR\,S(R)\right]\cos \phi.
\end{align}
The boundary adds the linear term
$r S(R)/R$ of \cref{eq:rho-analytic}, which pulls the density back to one at $r=R$.
\Cref{app:density-poisson} derives \cref{eq:rho-analytic} and compares it with a no-flux boundary, which
blocks the flux of cells. In either case the density departs from uniformity only at
order $\zeta/B$.

Restoring the cross-coupling sets the defect in motion. To find the direction of the
motion we need the active force at the defect. For the $+1/2$ defect
\cref{eq:pade-approximation} gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:active-force-defect}
  f^\text{a}_x(r, \phi) = -\zeta \left(S'(r) +S(r)/r\right),
  \qquad f^\text{a}_y = 0,
\end{align}
a vector field along the defect axis with no transverse component. It points along $-x$,
toward the head of the defect (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)). The active force moves
the cells, not the defect. The cells build up a density gradient until the flux driven by
the gradient cancels the active flux, $B\nabla\rho\simeq\fa$ (cf.~\cref{eq:velocity}). Within the closure of
\cref{sec:model-closure}, however, the nematic order couples to the density gradient and not
to the active force. The cross-coupling term of \cref{eq:model-Q} carries it with the
velocity $-B\nabla\rho/\xi_0$, against the active force, so the defect moves along $+x$,
toward its tail (\cref{app:comoving}).

In experiments, $+1/2$ defects move the other way, toward their head (Supplementary Note
of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}). The difference comes from the closure approximation. Self-propulsion also
transports nematic order: with reversals, each rod diffuses along its own axis and carries
its alignment with it. At the
tail of the defect the rods lie along the axis and carry their alignment into the core, whereas at the
head they lie across the axis, so this transport moves the defect toward its head. The
lowest-order closure drops it, although it is comparable to the cross-coupling term $QB\nabla\rho$ in \cref{eq:model-Q}.
In \cref{app:drift}, we recover the term we dropped and we show that the transport outweighs the cross-coupling, and the defect moves
toward its head as in experiments.


A drifting defect has no steady state in the laboratory frame.
\Cref{app:comoving} therefore solves the noiseless \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q}
numerically in a frame comoving with the core, retaining the cross-coupling.
The closed form \cref{eq:rho-analytic} fixes $\Q$ to the Pad\'e profile
\cref{eq:pade-approximation} and solves only the density equation, whereas the numerics
solve both fields together and let $\Q$ depart from that profile.

defect axis against \cref{eq:rho-analytic}.

\subsection{Linearization and the fluctuation spectrum}
\label{sec:defect-fluct}
% Pages ~2.5. dean.tex 748-818, 895-1266.
In the frame comoving with the defect, let $\delta\rho(\r,t)=\rho(\r,t)-\rho_\ss(\r)$ and
$\delta\Q(\r,t)=\Q(\r,t)-\Q_\ss(\r)$ be the fluctuations about its steady state
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}). Linearizing \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} in this frame around
the steady state gives
(\cref{app:dean-angle})
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:linearized}
\begin{align}
    &\partial_t\delta\rho =-\nabla\!\cdot\!\Bigl[
    \frac1{\xi_0}\bigl(-B\nabla\delta\rho -\zeta\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\delta\Q \bigr) + \bEta_\rho(\r,t)\Bigr],
    \label{eq:linearized-rho}
    \\
    &\partial_t\delta\Q = \frac B{\xi_0}\nabla\!\cdot\!(\Q_\ss\nabla\delta\rho)
    + \nabla\!\cdot\!(\delta\Q\,\bs w_\ss) + \frac4{\xir}\delta\Ha
    \nonumber\\
    &\qquad\qquad
    {}- \nabla\!\cdot\!\bEta^\Q(\r,t)+ \f^\Q(\r,t),
    \label{eq:linearized-Q}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
with $\u$ the velocity of the comoving frame, $\bs w_\ss\equiv\u+(B/\xi_0)\nabla\rho_\ss$,
so that $\delta\Q$ is transported with velocity $-\bs w_\ss$, and
$\delta\Ha$ the linearization of \cref{eq:H-active} at $\Q_\ss$. 
% The frame velocity term is
% kept only in the $\Q$-equation, through $\bs w_\ss$; in \cref{eq:linearized-rho} it is
% at most $3\times10^{-4}$ times the retained $B\nabla^2\delta\rho$ (\cref{app:dean-angle}).
% 2026-11-01: removed from the main text at the author's prompt; app:dean-angle (C.2) keeps the
% frame-term estimate (2.7e-4 at L = 20 um) and the delta-Ha formula, eq:dH-active.
The three noises
are those of \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} with their amplitudes evaluated on the steady
state. The dependence of the amplitudes on $\delta\rho$ and $\delta\Q$ would add products of
a fluctuation and a noise, which are of second order in the fluctuations, so the linearized
equations carry additive noise. \Cref{eq:linearized} defines a
linear
stochastic field theory on the background $(\rho_\ss,\Q_\ss)$. \Cref{app:lyapunov}
obtains the equal-time covariance of $\delta\rho$ and $\delta\Q$ numerically, by solving
a Lyapunov equation. 
% Its noise covariance is built from the exact moments of the
% maximum-entropy angular distribution, because the lowest-order closure gives covariance
% matrices with a negative eigenvalue where the nematic order is strong (\cref{app:realizability}).

The main text presents the analytically tractable case, in which we neglect nematic order fluctuations ($\delta\Q=0$) and only the density fluctuates.
This drops from \cref{eq:linearized-rho} the force $-\zeta\nabla\tcdot\delta\Q$, which is not
small: it fluctuates more strongly than the mean active force (\cref{app:lyapunov-results}).
But $\delta\Q$ relaxes nearly four orders of magnitude more slowly than the density at the
scale of a cell, so the density follows this force quasi-statically, as it follows the mean
active force in \cref{eq:rho-analytic}, and shifts only by about $(\zeta/B)\,\delta\Q$, far
less than the fluctuations that $\bEta_\rho$ drives.
% 2026-11-01, author's prompt: the delta-Q force is large but slow, so only the quasi-static
% case is kept, and the argument is made here once (app:lyapunov-results, App. H.5).

\Cref{eq:linearized-Q} then drops out; $\bEta_\rho$ is the only source of the fluctuations.
The steady density departs from unity only at order $\zeta/B$ (\cref{eq:rho-analytic}), so we use a flat density profile in the noise autocorrelation \eqref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation}:
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-ss}
  \la \eta^{\rho}_\alpha(\r,t) \,\eta^{\rho}_\beta(\r',t') \ra
  \\
  =\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \left[Q^\ss_\ab(\r)+\delta_\ab\right]
 \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t').
\end{multline}
The defect then enters \cref{eq:linearized-rho} in exactly one place, through the
$\Q_\ss$ term in \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss}, so it reaches $\delta\rho$
through the noise amplitude alone, not through the relaxation rate.

Taking the Fourier transform of \cref{eq:linearized-rho} over space and time gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-fourier}
  \delta\tilde\rho(\k,\omega) =
  -\frac{\ii\,\k\cdot\tilde \bEta_\rho(\k,\omega)}{\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2},
  \qquad \gamma_0 \equiv B/\xi_0,
\end{align}
with $\k$ the wavevector and $k=|\k|$. The noise is conservative, so its power carries a
factor $k^2$ that cancels the $k^2$ of the relaxation rate $\gamma_0k^2$. The spectrum is
then flat in $\k$, and the equal-time variance
$\la\delta\rho(\r,t)\delta\rho(\r',t)\ra_\ss$ diverges as $\r'\to\r$.

The divergence comes from treating the monolayer as a continuum down to arbitrarily short
length scales. The theory therefore needs a cutoff, and the system supplies one: there is no length
shorter than the width of a cell. We therefore set
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cutoff}
    \elln = 0.5~\micron.
\end{equation}
We implement the cutoff by suppressing wavevectors above $1/\elln$ with a Gaussian filter,
multiplying \cref{eq:rho-fourier} by $\ee^{-k^2\elln^2/4}$.
Everything below carries that factor.

Consider first the isotropic disordered case $\Q_\ss=0$. Inverting the smoothed
\cref{eq:rho-fourier} over space and time gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic}
  \la\delta\rho(\r,t)\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra =
  \frac{\zeta}{2\pi B\rho_0 \elln^2}
  \frac{
      \exp\!\!\left(\!-\frac{|\r-\r'|^2}{2 \elln^2+4 \gamma_0 |t-t'|}\right)
      }{1 + 2 \gamma_0 |t-t'| / \elln^2},
\end{align}
so that the equal-point, equal-time variance is the constant pedestal
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:pedestal}
  \sigmarho(\r) = \sigmarho\big|_\text{pedestal}
  = \frac{\zeta}{2\pi B\rho_0\elln^2}.
\end{align}
\Cref{eq:pedestal} gives the standard deviation of the density,
$\sigma_\rho \sim B^{-1/2}$, whereas the scale of the modulation of the average density in
\cref{eq:rho-analytic} is $B^{-1}$. Thus, in the large-$B$ limit the average density profile is
overwhelmed by the fluctuations.

\Cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic} also gives the two scales over which a density
fluctuation stays coherent: a correlation length equal to the cutoff $\elln$ and a correlation time
\begin{align}
 \label{eq:tau-c}
 \tau_c = \frac{\elln^2}{2 \gamma_0},
\end{align}
the time a fluctuation of size $\elln$ takes to diffuse away. 
% In this isotropic case
% neither scale involves the defect. Uniform nematic order leaves $\tau_c$ unchanged,
% although it makes the
% correlation between two points anisotropic (\cref{app:variance-uniform}). We neglect the
% change of $\tau_c$ near the defect, and only $\tau_c$ is used below.
Uniform nematic order leaves $\tau_c$ unchanged (\cref{app:variance-uniform}), however, the inhomogeneities around the defect will introduce higher order corrections. \Cref{sec:nucleation-2}
uses $\tau_c$ to turn the layering probability into a rate.
% , where it cancels from the ratio
% of the rates near the defect and far from it.

With the parameters of \cref{app:parameters}, $\tau_c\simeq0.4~\mathrm{ms}$, far shorter
than the correlation time of the reversal noise, $1/(2\frev)=5~\minute$
(\cref{eq:k-dichotomous}), so the white-noise limit of \cref{sec:model-micro} does not hold
at $\elln$, and we keep it as a deliberate simplification. A fluctuation at $\elln$ decays
within $\tau_c$, and an excursion of the pressure above $\pcrit$ lasts only a fraction of it,
whereas the bottom layer needs about $\Acell/(2\gamma_0)\simeq5~\mathrm{ms}$ to relax around
a departing cell, so within the model the slow limit assumed in \cref{sec:nucleation-1} does
not hold either. Real cells push in one direction for minutes between reversals and cross
$\elln$ in about half a minute, so the pressure they build would change slowly enough for the
trigger of \cref{eq:p-layer-def}. Lifting the white-noise limit would also change the
amplitude of the fluctuations;
\cref{sec:model-assumptions} discusses what these limits mean for the comparison with experiment.

Restoring the anisotropic piece of \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss}, the one that
carries $\Q_\ss$, gives the total density variance (\cref{app:variance})
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:total-gauss}
  \frac{\sigmarho(\r)}{\sigmarho|_\text{pedestal}}
  = 1 + \bigl\langle S\cos2(\theta_\text{nb}-\theta_{\bs d})\bigr\rangle_\Omega .
\end{equation}
The average runs over the neighbors $\r+\bs d$ of the point $\r$,
$\langle g\rangle_\Omega=\int\!\d^2\bs d\,\Omega(d)\,g(\r+\bs d)$. Here $S$ is the strength of
the steady nematic order at the neighbor, $\theta_\text{nb}$ the angle of its director with the
$x$ axis, and $\theta_{\bs d}$ the angle of $\bs d$ with the same axis
(\cref{fig:neighbors}(a)). A neighbor thus counts
with $+S$ when its rods point at $\r$ and with $-S$ when they lie across the line to $\r$. The weight depends
only on the distance $d$ to the neighbor,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:omega-weight}
  \Omega(d)=\frac{\elln^2}{2\pi d^4}\Bigl[2-\ee^{-X}\bigl(X^2+2X+2\bigr)\Bigr],
  % \\
  \quad
  X=\frac{2d^2}{\elln^2}.
\end{align}
It is positive and normalized over the plane, $\int\!\d^2\bs d\,\Omega(d)=1$, and since
$\Omega(0)=0$, the point $\r$ itself does not count (\cref{fig:neighbors}(b)).

\Cref{eq:total-gauss} is the central result of \cref{sec:defect}.
The defect modulates the variance around a uniform pedestal. The pedestal is the
variance of a uniform monolayer, fixed by the noise amplitude $\zeta$, the stiffness $B$
and the resolution $\elln$.

We evaluate \cref{eq:total-gauss} for two nematic fields $\Q_\ss$: the static Pad\'e profile
\cref{eq:Q-pade} and the steady state of the moving defect obtained numerically in
\cref{sec:defect-steady}, whose director lines \cref{fig:nematicaxis} compares.
\Cref{fig:variance} shows both results along the axis of the defect and across it. We call the point where the variance is
largest $\r_\mathrm{max}$, and its relative height above the pedestal,
$\nu_0\equiv[\sigmarho(\r_\mathrm{max})/\sigmarho(\infty)]-1$, the peak modulation.

The variance differs between head and tail because a cell runs back and forth along its
own axis. It moves density into and out of the points ahead of it and behind it, but not
those beside it, and this sets the sign with which a neighbor counts in
\cref{eq:total-gauss} (\cref{fig:neighbors}(a)). In a uniform nematic, the neighbors that raise the variance above the
pedestal and those that lower it below the pedestal balance. Around the defect, however, the
director lines bend around the core
% : they cross the axis on the head side (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)), and run along it on the tail side.

and break this balance, in favor of the neighbors that raise the variance on the head side
of the defect and of those that lower it on its tail side. The caption of
\cref{fig:aimed-rods} explains how, panel by panel. 

The weight $\Omega$
reaches over a distance proportional to $\elln$, so the peak modulation $\nu_0$ decreases
with decreasing cutoff $\elln$ and vanishes at $\elln=0$ (\cref{app:variance-ln}).
The defect therefore modulates the variance only because the density is resolved at the
width of a cell, which makes $\elln$, the shortest length of the model, a physical
parameter.
Strictly, a continuum theory is not applicable, because the variance changes over the lengthscales comparable to the cutoff length $\elln$. Even for an ideal gas, the density resolved
over less than one particle per cutoff area is far from
Gaussian~\cite{velenich2008brownian}, and here $\rho_0\elln^2\simeq0.06$.
Copenhagen \textit{et al.}~\cite{copenhagen2021} nevertheless described the flows around
these defects successfully with a continuum active-nematic theory. Fitted to the velocity
measured along the defect axis, the theory reproduced it near the defects at lengthscales smaller than the length of one cell.

We likewise apply the continuum theory, with its Gaussian statistics, down to $\elln$
as a deliberate simplification (\cref{sec:model-assumptions}), and compare its prediction
with experiment in \cref{sec:nucleation-2}.

In \cref{fig:variance} we also show the density variance from the full numerical solution
of \cref{eq:linearized}, retaining nematic order fluctuations ($\delta\Q\ne0$,
\cref{app:lyapunov}), and \cref{fig:variance3d}(a) shows its two-dimensional profile. The
analytic result of \cref{eq:total-gauss} already captures its main features.

Evaluated on the steady
state of the moving defect instead of the Pad\'e profile (see \cref{fig:nematicaxis}),
\cref{eq:total-gauss} follows the numerical solution closely, along the axis and across it
(dotted in \cref{fig:variance}), so the slow force of $\delta\Q$ indeed changes the variance
little.
The
motion of the defect deforms the nematic field,
% (\cref{fig:nematicaxis} and \cref{fig:comoving-deformation}(a,b)).
and this deformation makes the modulation
unequal between head and tail (\cref{fig:aimed-rods}(b,d)) and also lifts the modulation above zero at the core.
Seen from the core, the rods on the tail side point at it and
raise its variance, and those on the head side lie across the line to it and lower it (\cref{fig:aimed-rods}(c)). 
On the
static Pad\'e profile the two halves cancel, but in the moving defect the tail side, where the order
is stronger, outweighs the head side. Both the head--tail asymmetry and the raised core thus
come from the motion of the defect, whose direction the lowest-order closure sets
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}). Relaxing the closure approximation should therefore change the
modulation quantitatively, but we believe not qualitatively.



A modulation of two to three percent is the entire position\nobreakdash-dependent signal in the density variance profile. In \cref{sec:nucleation-2} we show that it modulates the layering probability by two orders of magnitude.

\section{Layer formation probability from the pressure fluctuations}
\label{sec:nucleation-2}
% The calculation motivated in \cref{sec:nucleation-1}, now that \cref{sec:defect}
% supplies the variance. Pressure statistics from the former sec:nucleation-theory,
% comparison with experiment from the former sec:nucleation-results
% (dean.tex 1392-1487). Former fig:match is panel (c) of fig:defects-layering.

\Cref{sec:nucleation-1} reduced layer formation to the probability \eqref{eq:p-layer-def} that
the pressure at a point exceeds the critical pressure $\pcrit$, and \cref{sec:model,sec:defect}
gave the variance of the density around a $+1/2$ defect (\cref{sec:defect-fluct}). We now turn
this variance into the statistics of the pressure, compute that probability and compare its
profile with the measurements.

By \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, a density fluctuation $\delta\rho$ changes the
pressure by $B\,\delta\rho$. In the linear theory, $\delta\rho$ is Gaussian with the
variance of \cref{eq:total-gauss}, so the pressure $p$ is Gaussian too, with
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sigma-p}
    \sigma_p^2(\r) = B^2\sigmarho(\r),
    \qquad
    \sigma_{p,\infty}^2 \equiv\sigma_p^2(\infty)= \frac{B\zeta}{2\pi\rho_0\elln^2},
\end{equation}
the second expression its far-field value, from the isotropic pedestal
\cref{eq:pedestal}.
As we argued for the average density, in the large-$B$ limit the mean pressure is $\la p(\r) \ra \ll \sigma_p(\r)$. We therefore set it to zero.%
\footnote{Far in the tail of the distribution, the mean shifts the exponent by
$\pcrit|\la p\ra|/\sigma_{p,\infty}^2\lesssim0.14$, which changes the probability by less
than $15\%$, far less than the modulation of the variance does.}
The probability in \cref{eq:p-layer-def} is then the Gaussian
tail
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:p-layer}
    \calP(\r) &\propto \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\,z(\r)}\ee^{-z(\r)^2/2},
    \qquad
    z(\r)= \frac{\pcrit}{\sigma_p(\r)}.
\end{align}
% Moved 2026-10-09b to sec:nucleation-1 (his prompts): point vs cell-averaged pressure, and the
% nucleus need not be one whole cell. The number cut there: averaged over a disc of one cell
% area, the variance is 0.32 of the point variance (eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic,
% claude_experiments/discussion_numbers_2026_10_09.py).
Recall that $\calP(\r)$ measures the fraction of time during which the pressure at $\r$
exceeds $\pcrit$. Since the pressure decorrelates over the time $\tau_c$ of \cref{eq:tau-c},
the layering rate at $\r$ is of the order of $\calP(\r)/\tau_c$.


The comparison with experiment is not to an absolute layering rate but to a \emph{ratio}:
Copenhagen \textit{et al.}~\cite{copenhagen2021} report the distribution of distances
between defects and new
layers, normalized by the same distribution for random points in the monolayer. 
In our case, the ratio of layering
probabilities, up to the slowly varying prefactor $1/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,z)$ of \cref{eq:p-layer}, is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:R-ratio}
    \frac{\calP(\r)}{\calP(\infty)} \simeq
    \exp\!\left[\frac{z_\infty^2}2\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{p,\infty}^2}{\sigma_p^2(\r)}\right)\right],
    \quad z_\infty \equiv \frac{\pcrit}{\sigma_{p,\infty}}.
\end{equation}
Only the \emph{relative} modulation of the variance matters, and the large factor
$z_\infty^2/2$ amplifies it.
With the bulk modulus $B=90~\kPa\tcdot\micron$, fitted below, the cutoff $\elln=0.5~\micron$ and the parameters of
\cref{app:parameters}, $\sigma_{p,\infty}=1.7~\kPa\tcdot\micron$ and
$z_\infty=20.8$. 
A modulation of a few percent therefore produces an enhancement of two
orders of magnitude. The numerical variance of \cref{eq:linearized}, on the steady state of
the moving defect, peaks at
$\nu_0\simeq2.4\%$, off the core at the head of the defect
(\cref{fig:variance}), and \cref{eq:R-ratio} then gives the peak enhancement
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:R-value}
    \frac{\calP(\r_\mathrm{max})}{\calP(\infty)}
    = \exp\!\left[\frac{z_\infty^2}2\,\frac{\nu_0}{1+\nu_0}\right] \simeq 1.7\times10^2 ,
\end{equation}
since the layering probability, like the variance, is largest at $\r_\mathrm{max}$. We chose
$B$ so that this peak enhancement is close to $2\times10^2$, the
enhancement that Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} measures near $+1/2$ defects (its Fig.~3f).

\Cref{fig:defects-layering}(c) shows, from the same variance, the layering probability
averaged over a circle of radius $r$ about a center $\r_\mathrm c$,
with $\phi$ the polar angle about that center,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:p-layer-radial}
    \calP(r) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \oint\limits_{|\r-\r_\mathrm c|=r}\!\d\phi\,\calP(\r),
\end{equation}
relative to its far-field value. The profile depends on the choice of the center (\cref{fig:variance3d}(b,c)). The
variance modulation nearly vanishes at the core, so the profile
centered there is small at $r=0$, peaks where the circle passes close to
the maximum of the variance, and then decays (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(c), orange). Centered on
$\r_\mathrm{max}$, the circle starts on the maximum and moves away from it as $r$ grows
(\cref{fig:variance3d}(b)), so the profile starts at its maximum and decays monotonically (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(c), blue). We show both, without deciding which center the measurement corresponds to.
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} locates the core at the minimum of the order parameter in images
smoothed over $1.7~\micron$, a length comparable to the distance between the core and
$\r_\mathrm{max}$.

Layers in \textit{M.\ xanthus} monolayers form preferentially at $+1/2$
defects~\cite{copenhagen2021}. Above a critical pressure $\pcrit$ (\cref{eq:pcrit}) a new
layer grows from a single extruded cell, whereas below it an extruded patch shrinks back. This threshold is the same
everywhere in the monolayer, and the mean pressure at a defect stays three to four orders of
magnitude below it. We have therefore proposed that layers form when fluctuations of the
pressure exceed $\pcrit$. A defect then favors layers only because the pressure fluctuates
differently around it. We derived these fluctuations by coarse-graining self-propelled rods
that reverse, keeping their noise in Dean's equation. The density variance
(\cref{fig:variance,fig:variance3d}) is raised by a few percent at the head of a $+1/2$
defect (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)) and lowered at its tail. In the far tail of the pressure
distribution, this small change is amplified exponentially, so that the layering probability at the head of the defect rises by two
orders of magnitude, as measured (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(c), \cref{eq:R-value}).

The comparison has one fitted parameter, the bulk modulus $B$. The mean pressure stays of
the order of the active stress $\zeta$ for any $B$, whereas the pressure variance grows as
$B\zeta$ (\cref{eq:sigma-p}). The fluctuations can therefore reach the threshold when the
mean cannot. The shape of the variance profile, in contrast, is not fitted. Due to self-propulsion, each cell is displaced
along its own axis, so the noise in the flux of cells is stronger along the director than
across it (\cref{eq:eta-rho-ss}). Following the director field of the defect, this
anisotropic noise places the peak of the variance off the core and makes the modulation
decay over a few micrometers (\cref{eq:total-gauss}).

\subsection{Model assumptions and simplifications}
\label{sec:model-assumptions}
% 2026-11-01, author's prompts on this subsection: opening sentence dropped; the background
% assumptions only mentioned; each deliberate simplification with its reason and its cost.

We have aimed at the simplest description that still captures the mechanism. It rests on a
coarse-grained Landau--de Gennes free energy with a single Frank constant in place of the
unknown pair potential of the cells (\cref{sec:model-closure}), on Dean's equation applied to
a smooth density (\cref{sec:model-coarse}), and on a friction with the substrate that is the
same along and across the cells (\cref{sec:model-micro}). Several other assumptions hold only
approximately, and correcting them would make the model more elaborate, so we keep them as
deliberate simplifications.

The first is the lowest-order closure of the angular moments in the field equations. It is
inaccurate where the nematic order is strong, away from the defect core (\cref{app:closure}),
and the higher-order terms it drops reverse the direction in which the defect drifts
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}), on which the head--tail asymmetry of the variance depends
(\cref{sec:defect-fluct}). The closure also requires us to drop the elastic force that moves
the cells, so that the pressure is the only passive stress (\cref{sec:model-elastic}). This
breaks the Onsager symmetry of the passive transport: the pressure-driven flow still carries
the alignment of the cells, but gradients of the nematic force field no longer move the
cells. Despite the broken symmetry, the model stays consistent: since it is active, its free
energy need not decrease in time, and since the passive transport has no noise of its own,
no fluctuation--dissipation relation ties it to the noise. The equations without the elastic
force also remain linearly stable in a uniform nematic state (\cref{app:closure}).

Like the closure, the white-noise limit of the reversals serves to close the theory on the
density and the nematic order (\cref{sec:model-micro}), and the dichotomous noise behind it
makes the reversals a Poisson process, without the refractory period of real cells. The
limit requires the cells to reverse faster than the density relaxes, but at the scale of a
cell the density relaxes within a millisecond, whereas the cells reverse every few minutes
(\cref{sec:defect-fluct}). The same limit makes an excursion of the pressure shorter than the
time the bottom layer needs to relax around a departing cell, so within the model the slow
limit of the trigger in \cref{sec:nucleation-1} does not hold. Real cells, in contrast, push
in one direction for minutes, so the pressure they build would change slowly enough.

The linear theory makes the density Gaussian, and we use this Gaussian even far in its tail,
at the scale $\elln$ of a cell. The central limit theorem would make the density Gaussian
only over an area that holds many cells, whereas $\elln^2$ holds on average much less than
one~\cite{velenich2008brownian}. Even over an area with many cells, it describes fluctuations
near the mean, not far in the tail. Finally, the layering criterion idealizes the nucleus as
a disc of one cell area, which keeps it independent of the orientation of the rod-shaped
cells, and it assumes that a cell leaves the monolayer as soon as the pressure at a point
exceeds $\pcrit$ (\cref{sec:nucleation-1}).

The white-noise limit, the Gaussian tail and the idealized nucleus keep the theory closed and
linear and the layering criterion simple, so that the variance around the defect, and from
it the layering probability, can be computed analytically. They affect absolute quantities
most, such as the rate of layering, which depends exponentially on $(\pcrit/\sigma_p)^2$,
with $\sigma_p$ the standard deviation of the pressure (\cref{eq:sigma-p}). The ratio of the
layering probabilities at the defect and far from it is less sensitive, and this is why we
compare only this ratio with experiment, although it too rests on the Gaussian tail of the
pressure distribution.

\subsection{Outlook and future work}
\label{sec:overlook-and-future-work}

The nucleation criterion is only one way to turn the statistics of the pressure into a
layering probability. In growing biofilms and colonies, a rod instead tilts out of the plane,
triggered by a division, once the compression exceeds a threshold that grows with its
length~\cite{beroz2018verticalization,you2019mono}, and in three-dimensional models of cell
monolayers extrusion follows a local build-up of stress~\cite{monfared2023mechanical}; such
criteria could be applied to the same density and pressure fluctuations.

% linearly with cell length, adhesion ~ contact area; reorientation right after division,
% p. 956); You 2019 (arXiv:1811.08875v2) abstract and p. 3 (instability of single cells,
% division drops the length, stress above that of a cell of minimal length, asynchronous
% divisions make it stochastic; same mechanism as Beroz, pp. 4-5); Monfared 2023 pp. 6-7.

In the coarse-grained equations the same coefficient $\zeta=\xi_0v_0^2/(4\frev)$ of
\cref{eq:active-force} sets the active stress and the amplitude of the density noise, so the
reversal rate controls both. The factor $z_\infty^2=\pcrit^2/\sigma_{p,\infty}^2$ in the
exponent of \cref{eq:R-ratio}
is therefore inversely proportional to $\zeta$ at fixed $B$ and $\elln$ (\cref{eq:sigma-p}),
and for a fixed defect profile the logarithm of the enhancement is proportional to
$\frev/v_0^2$. Slower reversals then make layers
more frequent everywhere, but less concentrated at defects. Layers do become more
frequent when cells reverse less often. Han \textit{et al.}~\cite{han2025local} observed
this with a
\textit{frzE} mutant, whose cells reverse
about once every two hours instead of every few minutes~\cite{blackhart1985frizzy}. 
Its
colonies have a larger area covered by multilayers, and traction fluctuations more than
twice those of
reversing cells. A non-reversing strain also forms multilayers at high cell
density~\cite{peruani2012collective}. Such rare or absent reversals lie even farther outside the
white-noise limit, so these observations support the trend rather than the quantitative
prediction. That layering then also becomes less concentrated at defects has not been
measured, and layering around defects at several reversal rates would test it.

Three observables beyond the radial profile of the layering probability in
\cref{fig:defects-layering}(c) could test the mechanism. The first is the angular profile
of layer formation, which would test the predicted maximum off the core, at the head of the defect. 
% In the snapshots of \cref{fig:defects-layering}(b) the new layer
% starts there, and \cref{fig:2examples} shows two more layers forming at the head of $+1/2$
% defects.
% No quantitative measurement of where layers start has been published, however, and the
% positions of new layers relative to the defect axis, taken from existing movies, would

The second observable is the traction.
Han \textit{et al.}~\cite{han2025local} measured traction fluctuations about ten times the
mean around defects, and the fluctuations of the active force in our model also exceed its
mean everywhere
(\cref{app:lyapunov-results}). The traction is the divergence of the full stress, which
includes the pressure. A quantitative comparison therefore needs the traction variance at
the resolution of the measurement, which we have not computed. The third observable, the
density variance itself, is the most direct. However, at the scale of a cell, where the model
predicts the modulation, an estimate of the variance is noisy, so resolving a modulation of
a few percent requires an average over many defects. The predicted size of the modulation
also depends on the scale $\elln$ at which the density is resolved (\cref{app:variance-ln}).

At $-1/2$ defects, Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} finds mainly holes opening rather than new
layers forming. In the model, a $-1/2$ defect also raises the layering probability near
its core, but less than a $+1/2$ defect does (\cref{app:negative,fig:negative-layering}).
Why holes open there instead needs additional modeling, which we leave aside.

In future work we will relax the simplifications listed in \cref{sec:model-assumptions} within the same framework, with the exact
moments of the angular distribution in place of the lowest-order closure, a finite
correlation time and a refractory period of the reversals, the discreteness of the cells in
the far tail of the density distribution, and the extrusion of a cell followed in time, and
investigate how each of them changes the results. With one parameter fitted to the height of the peak, the minimal model reproduces the
measured enhancement. It
shows that a defect can set where layers form without raising the mean pressure to the
threshold, only by reshaping the fluctuations that the self-propulsion and reversals of
single cells produce.

\subsection{Parameters of the field equations}
\label{app:parameters-choice}
% Rewritten 2026-10-01 from Ashot's summary of the new estimates (jobscripts/
% set2_2_comove_optimized_2/params.md), then restyled for flow the same day at his prompt
% ("written in a boring way, too many times 'take'"). Numbers: newset_numbers.py.

\Cref{tab:field-params} collects the parameters of the field equations. Most of them
have been measured, but the reported values spread widely, because they depend on the
experimental conditions and change in time. In the experiments of
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}, sparse cells glide at $0.92~\micron/\minute$ and reverse at
$0.17~\minute^{-1}$, whereas isolated cells glide at about $2~\micron/\minute$ and reverse
every $6.8~\minute$~\cite{blackhart1985frizzy}, and cells of a non-reversing strain glide
at $3.1~\micron/\minute$~\cite{peruani2012collective}. Under starvation, cells glide faster
and reverse less often as development proceeds: over $15~\mathrm h$ the mean speed rises
from $1.7$ to $2.7~\micron/\minute$, while the reversal frequency falls from $0.10$ to
$0.045~\minute^{-1}$~\cite{jelsbak2002pattern,liu2019selfdriven}. Since
$\zeta=\xi_0v_0^2/(4\frev)$, these two changes alone raise $\zeta$ sixfold. Cells in large
groups also reverse less often than isolated cells~\cite{shi1996cell}, and the density
rises as the cells aggregate. Where the reports differ, we therefore choose a value within
their range.

We take the density $\rho_0=0.25~\micron^{-2}$ from Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}, and the
friction coefficient $\xi_0=5~\Pa\tcdot\minute/\micron$ from Ref.~\cite{han2025local},
which estimates it in two ways (its Supplementary Fig.~S12 and Sec.~II.C).

The reported values of $\zeta/\xi_0$ span
$0.5$--$5~\micron^2/\minute$~\cite{han2025local,copenhagen2021}. Instead of picking one, we
use \cref{eq:active-force}, which ties $\zeta$ to the motility of single cells:
$v_0=1~\micron/\minute$~\cite{copenhagen2021} and
$\frev=0.1~\minute^{-1}$~\cite{liu2019selfdriven} give
$\zeta/\xi_0=v_0^2/(4\frev)=2.5~\micron^2/\minute$, and hence $\zeta=12.5~\Pa\tcdot\micron$.

The defect core has been measured in two ways. Fits of the flow around $+1/2$ defects give
$\elld\simeq2.5~\micron$ in Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}, with $S_0\simeq1$ from a separate
measurement (its Supplementary Note), and $\elld\simeq2.0~\micron$ in
Ref.~\cite{han2025local}. Ref.~\cite{han2025local} also measures the nematic field
directly, from the texture of bright-field images, and finds $\elld\simeq0.8~\micron$, with
$S$ rising to $0.9$ before it decays far away from the defect. We set $\elld=1~\micron$,
close to the direct measurement, and $S_0=0.9$.

We take the rotational diffusion coefficient $\Lambda=0.13~\minute^{-1}$, which
Ref.~\cite{liu2019selfdriven} infers from the decorrelation of isolated cells. The
rotational friction has not been measured, so we estimate it from $\xi_0$ as for a rigid
rod of length $L$ with uniform friction, $\xir=\xi_0L^2/12$, which gives
$\xir=15~\Pa\tcdot\micron\tcdot\minute$ for $L=6~\micron$. With these values the rotational
noise of \cref{eq:langevin-angle} is far from equilibrium: at $T=20^\circ\mathrm C$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rotational-fdt}
  \frac{\Lambda\xir}{\rho_0\kB T}\simeq1.9\times10^3,
\end{equation}
with $\kB$ the Boltzmann constant, whereas in equilibrium the fluctuation-dissipation  
theorem would make this ratio one~\cite{kubo1966fluctuation}. 
% The rotational diffusion
% coefficient exceeds the reversal rate $\frev=0.1~\minute^{-1}$ that we use,
% $\Lambda/\frev=1.3$, so the cells do not reverse
% much faster than they turn. We nevertheless keep $\zeta$ of \cref{eq:active-force}, which
% holds for fast reversals, as part of the white-noise simplification of
% \cref{sec:model-micro}.

\Cref{eq:S0-elld} gives only two relations among the coefficients $a'$, $b$ and $K'$ of
the nematic free energy, and we fix the third with the rate at which the nematic order
relaxes. A small perturbation of a uniform $\Q$ with $\Q\!:\!\Q=2S_0^2$, relaxing through
the local term $\partial_t\Q=4\Ha/\xir$ of \cref{eq:model-Q}, obeys
(cf.~\cref{eq:dH-active})
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:relaxation-rate}
  \partial_t\delta\Q=\frac4{\xir}\bigl[-(a'+b\,\Q\!:\!\Q)\,\delta\Q
  -2b\,(\Q\!:\!\delta\Q)\,\Q\bigr],
\end{equation}
so that a perturbation parallel to $\Q$ decays at the rate
$\gamma_Q=16bS_0^2/\xir=-8a'/\xir$.

The model bounds this rate from below. The bare free energy
\cref{eq:free-energy-choice} is stable only for $K>0$, which by \cref{eq:H-active}
requires $K'>\zeta\xir/(4\xi_0)$, and with $K'=-a'\elld^2$ from \cref{eq:S0-elld} this
gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gamma-bound}
  \gamma_Q=\frac{8K'}{\xir\elld^2}>\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\elld^2}=5~\minute^{-1}.
\end{equation}
% The bound does not contain $\xir$, so it holds whatever the true $\xir$ is. 
We choose
$\gamma_Q=10~\minute^{-1}$, so the nematic field relaxes over $6$ seconds.
The coefficients $a'$, $b$ and $K'$, and the bare $a$ and $K$ of
\cref{eq:free-energy-choice}, then follow from the relations in \cref{tab:field-params}.

This leaves the bulk modulus $B$, the only parameter of the model that we fit. We set
$B=90~\kPa\tcdot\micron$, for which the peak of the layering probability is
$1.7\times10^2$ times its far-field value, close to the enhancement of about $2\times10^2$
that Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} measures near $+1/2$ defects (\cref{sec:nucleation-2}). No direct measurement exists to
compare it with, but related moduli, multiplied by the layer height $h$, span five orders
of magnitude. At the soft end, colonies of \textit{Serratia marcescens} on agar have shear
and compressive moduli of $10^2$--$10^4~\Pa$, or
$0.05$--$5~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{kochanowski2024}. The turgor pressure of
\textit{E.\ coli}, $29~\kPa$, corresponds to $15~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{deng2011direct},
and the effective cell modulus of $375~\kPa$ that fits the planar-to-bulk transition of
growing \textit{E.\ coli} colonies corresponds to $190~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{grant2014}.
Indentation of live \textit{M.\ xanthus} cells gives a local Young's modulus of
$0.25~\mathrm{MPa}$, or about $125~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{pelling2005nanoscale}, although
this is the stiffness of the cell envelope, not the compressibility of the monolayer.
At the stiff end, the cell wall has a Young's modulus of $25$--$50~\mathrm{MPa}$, or about
$10^4~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{yao1999thickness,deng2011direct}. The fitted $B$ thus lies
between the turgor pressure and the effective cell modulus, close to the envelope
stiffness of \textit{M.\ xanthus}. It is stiffer than the moduli measured for bacterial
colonies and of the order of the stiffness of single cells.

\section{Elastic energy released by the bottom layer}
\label{app:extrusion}
% dean.tex 1293-1337. Supports \cref{sec:nucleation-1} and \cref{eq:pressure-definition}.
% Written as prose 2026-09-30. The V9 prompts stand: no threshold on rho d_rho f, no size
% estimate of the finite-area term.

The pressure work $pA$ in \cref{eq:dE-of-A} is the elastic energy that the compressed
cells release when a patch leaves the monolayer. We derive it from the compression term
$\mathfrak f_B=\tfrac B2(\rho-1)^2$, the only term of \cref{eq:free-energy-choice} that
depends on $\rho$.

Consider a region of area $A_d$ that is compressed uniformly to $\rho=1+\epsilon$, so that
it stores the energy $\mathfrak f_BA_d=\tfrac B2\epsilon^2A_d$. A patch of area $A$ inside
this region then forms the new layer and takes $\rho_0(1+\epsilon)A$ cells with it. As the
bottom layer spreads back over the whole of $A_d$ (\cref{fig:nucleation}(a)), its strain
falls to $\epsilon_f=\epsilon-(1+\epsilon)A/A_d$. The extruded cells, in contrast, are
unconfined in the new layer and relax to zero strain. The elastic energy therefore changes
by $\Delta E_e=\tfrac B2(\epsilon_f^2-\epsilon^2)A_d$, which we write as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dEe-full}
    \Delta E_e = \frac B2\left[-2\epsilon - 2\epsilon^2
    + (1+\epsilon)^2\frac A{A_d}\right]A.
\end{equation}

Of this energy, the bottom layer releases $pA-\tfrac B2(1+\epsilon)^2A^2/A_d$, where $p=\tfrac B2(\rho^2-1)$ is the exact
pressure of \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, while the extruded cells release their own
strain energy $\mathfrak f_BA$. For a patch much smaller than the compressed region,
$A\ll A_d$, the term in $A^2/A_d$ is negligible, and
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dEe}
    \Delta E_e \simeq -(p+\mathfrak f_B)A \simeq -pA.
\end{equation}
The $p$ of \cref{eq:dE-of-A} is thus the pressure of \cref{eq:pressure-definition}.

Dropping $\mathfrak f_B$ in the last step of \cref{eq:dEe} is consistent at leading order,
the order at which \cref{eq:dE-of-A} works, because $\mathfrak f_B$ is quadratic in
$\epsilon$ and $p$ is linear. At the pressure where a layer forms, $p=\pcrit$
(\cref{eq:pcrit}), however, $\epsilon=\pcrit/B\simeq0.39$ at leading order, and the dropped
term adds about $20\%$ to $pA$. This correction lowers the pressure at which a layer forms.
Like the other simplifications of the nucleus in \cref{sec:nucleation-1}, the disc of one
cell area and the pressure taken at a point, it is absorbed by the fit of $B$.

% The flux noises in \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} follow from the reversals of the
% individual rods, with an amplitude set by the coefficient $\zeta$ of the active force and

In this appendix we obtain Dean's equation for
the joint density of positions and orientations, then integrate out the orientation to
reach the field equations for $\rho$ and $\Q$ of \cref{sec:model-coarse,sec:model-closure},
identify the pressure and the passive stress they contain, and finally explain why we work
with the unnormalized order parameter.

\subsection{Derivation of Dean's equation}
\label{app:dean-approach}
We derive \cref{eq:final-langevin} with It\^o's lemma, so \cref{eq:model} is read in
the It\^o sense, although $k_i$ is the white limit of a colored noise and such a limit
converges to the Stratonovich interpretation~\cite{wong1965convergence}. Here the two
interpretations coincide. The drift that separates them is $\tfrac12 g\,\partial g$, where
$g$ is the amplitude of a noise and the derivative is taken along the variables that this
noise drives. It vanishes for both noises: the amplitude $v_0\p(\theta_i)$ of $k_i$ depends
only on $\theta_i$, which $k_i$ does not drive, and $\lambda_i$ is additive.

We follow the rod through a smooth test function $\phi(\r,\theta)$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dean-ito-single}
  \phi(\r_i,\theta_i)=\int\!\d\r\,\d\theta\,\phi(\r,\theta)\,\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta),
\end{equation}
where $\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta) =
\delta(\r-\r_i(t))\,\delta(\theta-\theta_i(t))$ is the single-particle density.
Differentiating both sides in time gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dean-ito-time}
  \frac{\d\phi}{\d t} = \int\!\d\r\,\d\theta\,\phi(\r,\theta)\,\pd{}{t}\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta),
\end{equation}
whose right-hand side contains $\partial_t\varrho^{(i)}_t$. We evaluate the left-hand side
a second time with the It\^o formula,
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:dean-ito}
  \d\phi(\r_i,\theta_i) = \pd{\phi}{\r}\!\cdot\!\d\r_i + \pd{\phi}{\theta}\,\d\theta_i
  \\
  + \frac12\pd{^2\phi}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}\d r_\alpha \d r_\beta
  + \pd{^2\phi}{r_\alpha\partial \theta}\d r_\alpha \d \theta
  + \frac12\pd{^2\phi}{\theta^2}\d \theta \d\theta,
\end{multline}
with the derivatives of $\phi$ evaluated at the rod.
In the white-noise limit, the quadratic variations of the increments of
\cref{eq:model} are $\d r_\alpha\d r_\beta=(v_0^2/\frev)\,p_\alpha p_\beta\,\d t$,
$\d \theta \d\theta=2\Lambda\,\d t$ and $\d\r\,\d\theta=0$.
With $\d\r_i$ and $\d\theta_i$ from \cref{eq:model}, the left-hand side of
\cref{eq:dean-ito-time} therefore becomes
\begin{multline}
  \frac{\d\phi}{\d t} = \pd{\phi}{\r}\!\cdot\!\left[
    \frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F_i + v_0\,\p(\theta_i) k_i
  \right]
  + \pd{\phi}{\theta}\left[
    \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma_i + \lambda_i
  \right]
  \\
  + \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha(\theta_i) p_\beta(\theta_i)\,\pd{^2\phi}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\phi}{\theta^2},
\end{multline}
where $\F_i = \F(\r_i,\theta_i)$ and $\Gamma_i = \Gamma(\r_i,\theta_i)$, with the
fields of \cref{eq:force-torque}.
Every term on the right is again a function evaluated at the rod, so
\cref{eq:dean-ito-single} applies to each of them:
\begin{multline}
\frac{\d\phi}{\d t} = \int\!\d\r\,\d\theta\,\varrho^{(i)}_t
\Bigg(\pd{\phi}{\r}\tcdot\left[
    \frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F + v_0\,\p k_i
  \right]
  \\
  + \pd{\phi}{\theta}\left[
    \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma + \lambda_i
  \right]
  \\
 + \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha p_\beta\,\pd{^2\phi}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\phi}{\theta^2}
\Bigg),
\end{multline}
with $\F$, $\Gamma$ and $\p$ now evaluated at $(\r,\theta)$.
Integrating by parts, once in the first-order terms and twice in the second-order ones,
moves the derivatives from $\phi$ onto $\varrho^{(i)}_t$, as in \cref{eq:dean-ito-time}.
There are no boundary terms, because $\varrho^{(i)}_t$ vanishes away from the rod and is
periodic in $\theta$, and $p_\alpha p_\beta$, which depends only on $\theta$, passes through
the $\r$ derivatives unchanged,
\begin{multline}
  \frac{\d\phi}{\d t} =
  \int\d\r\d\theta\, \phi(\r,\theta) \Bigg(
    -\pd{}{\r}\tcdot\Bigl[\varrho^{(i)}_t
    \Bigl(\frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F+v_0\p\,k_i\Bigr)\Bigr]
  \\
  - \pd{}{\theta}\Bigl[\varrho^{(i)}_t\Bigl(\tfrac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma+\lambda_i\Bigr)\Bigr]
  + \frac{v_0^2 p_\alpha p_\beta}{2\frev}\,
    \pd{^2\varrho^{(i)}_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho^{(i)}_t}{\theta^2}
\Bigg).
\end{multline}
Since this equals \cref{eq:dean-ito-time} for every $\phi$, the integrands agree,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:dean-single}
  \partial_t\varrho^{(i)}_t \!=&\!  -\pd{}{\r}\tcdot\Bigl[\varrho^{(i)}_t
    \!\Bigl(\tfrac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F\!+v_0\p\,k_i\Bigr)\Bigr]
 \!-\! \pd{}{\theta}\Bigl[\varrho^{(i)}_t\Bigl(\tfrac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma\!+\!\lambda_i\Bigr)\Bigr]
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha p_\beta\,
    \pd{^2\varrho^{(i)}_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho^{(i)}_t}{\theta^2}.
\end{align}
The It\^o term of self-propulsion, in the last line, carries the same amplitude
$v_0^2/\frev$ as the noise $v_0\p\,k_i$, and this shared amplitude ties the active stress to
the noise (\cref{app:dean-angle}).

Summing \cref{eq:dean-single} over $i$, with the weight $\rho_0^{-1}$ of
\cref{eq:joint-density-definition}, turns each drift term into the same term for
$\varrho_t$, because $\F$, $\Gamma$ and $\p$ do not depend on $i$. The noise terms keep
their rod index, and we collect them in
$\bs\chi=\rho_0^{-1}v_0\sum_i\varrho^{(i)}_t\,\p\,k_i$ and
$\psi=\rho_0^{-1}\sum_i\varrho^{(i)}_t\lambda_i$. The joint density therefore obeys
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:dean-sum}
  \partial_t\varrho_t = {}& -\frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\pd{}{\r}\tcdot(\varrho_t\F)
  - \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\pd{}{\theta}(\varrho_t\Gamma)
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha p_\beta\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{\theta^2}
  \nonumber\\
  &- \pd{}{\r}\tcdot\bs\chi - \pd{}{\theta}\psi .
\end{align}
The force and torque of \cref{eq:force-torque} follow from $\varrho_t$ through the pair
free energy of \cref{eq:pair-free-energy} and its functional derivative,
\begin{align}
  \calF[\varrho] =& \frac{\rho_0^2}2\!\int\!\d\r\d\r'\,\d\theta\d\theta'\,
  V(\r\!-\!\r'\!,\theta,\theta')\,\varrho(\r,\theta)\,\varrho(\r'\!,\theta'),
  \nonumber
  \\
  \frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}=&\rho_0^2\int\!\d\r'\d\theta'\,
V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')\,\varrho_t(\r',\theta')
\nonumber\\
=& \rho_0\sum_jV(\r-\r_j,\theta,\theta_j).
\label{eq:dean-functional-derivative}
\end{align}
Both factors of $\varrho$ contribute equally, by the pair symmetry
$V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')=V(\r'-\r,\theta',\theta)$, and the last step uses
\cref{eq:joint-density-definition}. With \cref{eq:force-torque},
$\F=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\r\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$ and
$\Gamma=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\theta\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$, which turns
\cref{eq:dean-sum} into the first two lines of \cref{eq:final-langevin}.

The noises $\bs\chi$ and $\psi$ still carry the individual $k_i$ and $\lambda_i$. They are
Gaussian, so it suffices to match their correlators. Different rods have independent
noises, so only $i=j$ survives the average of $\bs\chi\bs\chi$, and two delta functions at
the same rod collapse to one,
$\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta)\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r',\theta')
=\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta)\,\delta(\r-\r')\delta(\theta-\theta')$. Hence
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dean-noise-sum}
  \la\chi_\alpha(\r,\theta,t)\,\chi_\beta(\r',\theta',t')\ra
  = \frac{v_0^2}{\frev\rho_0}\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)\,p_\alpha(\theta)p_\beta(\theta)\,\Delta,
\end{equation}
with $\Delta\equiv\delta(t-t')\delta(\r-\r')\delta(\theta-\theta')$. The field
$\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p\,k$, with $k$ of \cref{eq:noise-correlators}, has the same correlator.
In the same way, the rotational noise $\psi$ has the correlator
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dean-noise-sum-psi}
  \la\psi(\r,\theta,t)\,\psi(\r',\theta',t')\ra
  = \frac{2\Lambda}{\rho_0}\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)\,\Delta,
\end{equation}
and so has $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda$. Both identities hold at fixed $\varrho_t$, which is
all an It\^o increment needs, so replacing $\bs\chi$ by $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p\,k$ and $\psi$
by $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda$ gives the last two lines of \cref{eq:final-langevin}.

\subsection{The fluctuating active nematic equations}
\label{app:dean-angle}

Integrating \cref{eq:final-langevin} over $\theta$ needs the $\theta$ dependence of
$\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$. In the parametrization \cref{eq:free-energy-parametrisation},
$\calF[\varrho]=F[\rho,\Q]$, the fields $\rho$ and $\Q$ are functionals of $\varrho$ through
\cref{eq:density-definition}, and the chain rule \cref{eq:chain-rule} follows from 
\begin{align}
  \frac{\delta\rho(\r')}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}=\delta(\r-\r'),
  \quad
  \frac{\delta\Q(\r')}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}=\hat\Q(\theta)\,\delta(\r-\r'),
\end{align}
 so
$\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$ holds only the angular harmonics $0$ and $2$. The interaction
terms of the density equation therefore integrate to $\rho$ and $\Q$ alone, whereas in the
$\Q$-equation they leave angular moments, which we write as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:average-def}
\la X\ra_\varrho\equiv\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)X(\theta).
\end{equation}
For example, density and nematic order fields in \cref{eq:density-definition} we be also written as $\rho=\la 1\ra_\varrho$ and $\Q = \langle \hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$.

Integrating \cref{eq:final-langevin} over $\theta$ removes every
$\theta$ derivative, and the self-propulsion term needs only
$\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,p_\alpha p_\beta=\tfrac12(Q_\ab+\rho\,\delta_\ab)$, which
follows from $\p\p=\tfrac12(\hat\Q+\I)$. Before any approximation, and with
$\zeta=\xi_0v_0^2/(4\frev)$ of \cref{eq:active-force}, the density obeys
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-full}
  \partial_t\rho = {}& \frac1{\xi_0}\nabla\tcdot\Bigl(\rho\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}
    + \Q\!:\!\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\Q}\Bigr)
  \nonumber\\
  &+\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\bigl(\nabla^2\rho+\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta Q_\ab\bigr)
  - \nabla\tcdot\bEta_\rho,
\end{align}
where the noise is the angular integral of the noise $\bs\chi$ of
\cref{eq:dean-sum},
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-chi}
  \bEta_\rho=\int\!\d\theta\,\bs\chi
  =\frac{v_0}{\rho_0}\sum_i\delta(\r-\r_i)\,\p(\theta_i)\,k_i .
\end{equation}
Replacing $\bs\chi$ by $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p\,k$ gives \cref{eq:eta-rho-def}. With
\cref{eq:free-energy-choice}, the first term of \cref{eq:rho-full},
$\rho\nabla(\delta F/\delta\rho)=B\rho\nabla\rho=\nabla p$, is the gradient of the exact
pressure \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, and the second is $-\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$, which is
minus the divergence of the nematic stresses (\cref{app:pressure}). The $\zeta$
term splits into the diffusion $\zeta\nabla^2\rho/\xi_0$ and the divergence of
the active force \cref{eq:active-force}.

The large modulus $B$ keeps $\rho$ close to one. In the steady state $\rho-1$ is of order
$\zeta/B$ (\cref{eq:rho-analytic}). We therefore keep the leading
order in $\rho-1$, counting $B(\rho-1)$ as of order one.
This truncation
removes the diffusion $(\zeta/\xi_0)\nabla^2\rho$, whose ratio to $B\nabla^2\rho/\xi_0$ is $\zeta/B$, and it reduces
$B\rho\nabla\rho$ to $B\nabla\rho$, so that \cref{eq:rho-full} becomes
\cref{eq:model-rho}. These truncated equations give the variance of
\cref{sec:defect-fluct}. Layers, however, form far out in the tail of the density
distribution, where
$\rho-1\simeq\pcrit/B\simeq0.39$ (\cref{app:extrusion}), so the layering
probability is a Gaussian extrapolation of these equations, one of the deliberate
simplifications of \cref{sec:model-assumptions}. The noise correlator
\cref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation}, in contrast,
needs neither the truncation nor a closure. It follows from
\cref{eq:eta-rho-chi}, the correlator \cref{eq:dean-noise-sum} of $\bs\chi$ and the
angular integral used above,
\begin{align}
  &\la\eta^\rho_\alpha(\r,t)\,\eta^\rho_\beta(\r',t')\ra
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\int\!\d\theta\,\d\theta'\,
  \la\chi_\alpha(\r,\theta,t)\,\chi_\beta(\r',\theta',t')\ra
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{v_0^2}{\frev\rho_0}\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,p_\alpha p_\beta\;
  \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t')
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}\bigl(Q_\ab+\rho\,\delta_\ab\bigr)\,
  \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t').
  % \nonumber
\end{align}

For the $\Q$-equation we multiply \cref{eq:final-langevin} by $\hat\Q(\theta)$ before
integrating. One integration by parts turns the surviving $\theta$ derivatives into
$\hat\Q'\equiv\partial_\theta\hat\Q$, and the identity $\hat\Q''=-4\hat\Q$ removes the
second derivatives. In components, with summation over repeated indices and the moments of
\cref{eq:average-def},
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:Q-full}
  \partial_t Q_\ab = {}& \frac1{\xi_0}\partial_i\Bigl(Q_\ab\,\partial_i\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}
  + \langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_\mn\rangle_\varrho\,\partial_i\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_\mn}\Bigr)
  \nonumber\\
  &- \frac1{\xir}\langle\hat Q'_\ab\hat Q'_\mn\rangle_\varrho\,\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_\mn}
  - 4\Lambda Q_\ab
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\bigl(\nabla^2Q_\ab
  + \partial_i\partial_j\langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_{ij}\rangle_\varrho\bigr)
  \nonumber\\
  &- \partial_i\eta^Q_{\ab i} + f^Q_\ab .
\end{align}
The four lines come from the translational friction, the rotational friction and
diffusion, self-propulsion and the two noises. Like $\bEta_\rho$, the noises are angular
integrals of those of \cref{eq:dean-sum}, now weighted by $\hat\Q$,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:Q-noise-chi}
  \eta^Q_{\ab \gamma}=\int\!\d\theta\,\hat Q_\ab\,\chi_\gamma,
  \qquad
  f^Q_\ab=\int\!\d\theta\,\hat Q'_\ab\,\psi.
\end{align}
The second follows from $-\int\!\d\theta\,\hat\Q\,\partial_\theta\psi$ by one
integration by parts in $\theta$, which turns the minus sign into a plus. Replacing
$\bs\chi$ by $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p\,k$ and $\psi$ by $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda$ gives the
$\bEta^\Q$ of \cref{sec:model-closure} and
$f^Q_\ab=\int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\hat Q'_\ab\,\lambda$. With these
definitions every noise carries a plus sign: $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$ enter
\cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} as $-\nabla\tcdot\bEta$, with the plus sign inside the
flux as in \cref{eq:final-langevin}, and $\f^\Q$ enters with a plus sign.

\Cref{eq:Q-full} is not closed, because the moments
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$ and $\langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho$ are not functions of
$\rho$ and $\Q$. We close it with the lowest-order closure of \cref{app:closure}, which sets
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho\simeq\rho\J$ and $\langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho\simeq4\rho\J$, with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:J-definition}
  J_\abmn=\tfrac12(\delta_{\alpha\mu}\delta_{\beta\nu}+\delta_{\alpha\nu}\delta_{\beta\mu}
  -\delta_\ab\delta_\mn).
\end{equation}
The tensor $\J$ leaves the symmetric traceless $\delta F/\delta\Q$
unchanged, and it gives $\J\!:\!\nabla\nabla\rho=\nabla\nabla\rho-\tfrac12\I\nabla^2\rho$.
With this closure \cref{eq:Q-full}
becomes
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:Q-closed}
  \partial_t Q_\ab =
  \frac{1}{\xi_0}\partial_\gamma\Bigl(Q_\ab\,\partial_\gamma\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}\Bigr)
  +\frac{1}{\xi_0}\partial_\gamma\Bigl(\rho\,\partial_\gamma\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_\ab}\Bigr)
  \\
  -\frac{4\rho}{\xir}\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_\ab} -4\Lambda Q_\ab
  +\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\Bigl(\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta\rho
  +\nabla^2\bigl(Q_\ab-\tfrac12\rho\,\delta_\ab\bigr)\Bigr)
  \\
  - \partial_\gamma\eta^Q_{\ab \gamma}+ f^Q_\ab .
\end{multline}
The truncation of the density equation removes the two places where the density now
enters. The derivative terms of the $\zeta$ term,
$(\zeta/\xi_0)(\nabla\nabla\rho-\tfrac12\I\nabla^2\rho)$, are
smaller than the retained $(B/\xi_0)\,\nabla\cdot(\Q \nabla\rho)$ by a factor of order
$\zeta/B$, and the prefactor $\rho$ of the two $\delta F/\delta\Q$ terms can be set to one.
The rotational friction then gives $(4/\xir)\H$, and the translational friction
$-\nabla^2\H/\xi_0$. Rotational diffusion, $-4\Lambda\Q$, and the self-propulsion term
$(\zeta/\xi_0)\nabla^2\Q$ have the form of the terms $-a\Q$ and $K\nabla^2\Q$ of $\H$ and
combine with it into $(4/\xir)\Ha$, with the $a'$ and $K'$ of \cref{eq:H-active}. With
$\delta F/\delta\rho=B(\rho-1)$ the result is \cref{eq:model-Q}.

The correlators of the two $\Q$ noises follow from their definitions
\cref{eq:Q-noise-chi} exactly as for $\bEta_\rho$, with the correlators
\cref{eq:dean-noise-sum,eq:dean-noise-sum-psi} of $\bs\chi$ and $\psi$ now weighted by
$\hat\Q$ or $\hat\Q'$. For the noises of \cref{eq:model-Q} they read
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:Q-noise-correlators}
  \langle\eta^Q_{\ab i}\,\eta^Q_{\mn j}\rangle &= \frac{2\zeta\Delta'}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \bigl[\langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_\mn\hat Q_{ij}\rangle_\varrho
  + \langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_\mn\rangle_\varrho\delta_{ij}\bigr],
  \nonumber\\
  \langle\eta^Q_{\ab i}\,\eta^\rho_j\rangle &= \frac{2\zeta\Delta'}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \bigl[\langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_{ij}\rangle_\varrho + Q_\ab\,\delta_{ij}\bigr],
  \nonumber\\
  \langle\!f^Q_\ab\,f^Q_\mn\rangle &= \frac{2\Lambda\Delta'}{\rho_0}\,
  \langle\hat Q'_\ab\hat Q'_\mn\rangle_\varrho,
\end{align}
with $\Delta'\equiv\delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t')$ and the moments taken at $\r$. Since
$\psi$ and $\bs\chi$ are independent, $\f^\Q$ is uncorrelated with $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$.
\Cref{app:closure} evaluates the moments; leading order moments are given in \cref{eq:leading-closure}.

The linearized equations \cref{eq:linearized} follow from \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} in
the frame comoving with the core, where $\partial_t\to\partial_t-\u\tcdot\nabla$. We
substitute $\rho=\rho_\ss+\delta\rho$ and $\Q=\Q_\ss+\delta\Q$ and keep the first order in
$\delta\rho$ and $\delta\Q$. For the nematic force field this gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:dH-active}
  \delta\Ha = {}& -a'\delta\Q
  - b\bigl[(\Q_\ss\!:\!\Q_\ss)\,\delta\Q + 2(\Q_\ss\!:\!\delta\Q)\,\Q_\ss\bigr]
  \nonumber\\
  &+ K'\nabla^2\delta\Q ,
\end{align}
whereas the transport term of \cref{eq:model-Q} gives
$(B/\xi_0)\nabla\tcdot(\Q_\ss\nabla\delta\rho+\delta\Q\nabla\rho_\ss)$. Its second part
combines with the frame term $\nabla\tcdot(\delta\Q\,\u)$ into the advection by
$\bs w_\ss$ in \cref{eq:linearized-Q}.
The noise amplitudes are evaluated on the steady state, so the linearized equations
carry additive noise, and for them the It\^o and Stratonovich interpretations coincide
trivially.

The frame velocity term matters only in the $\Q$-equation. In the
density equation, $\u\tcdot\nabla\delta\rho$ competes with
$(B/\xi_0)\nabla^2\delta\rho$, and for $\delta\rho$ varying over a length $L$ their
ratio is $\xi_0|\u|L/B$. Our steady

\subsection{The pressure and the passive stress}
\label{app:pressure}
% 2026-10-23e, his prompts ("It is hard to read until here, rewrite and simplify this
% section", "ij -> alpha beta"): the passive flux is split by the two parts of the free energy
% first; the total pressure p_f and the chemical potential of a rod are gone; Greek indices.
% Identity checked by hand: Q:d_a mu_Q = d_a(Q:mu_Q - f_n) + d_b[d_aQ : df_n/d(d_bQ)].
% 2026-10-26, his prompt ("Start from gibbs duhem, then decompose it into two part using the
% property that free energy can also be split"): the Gibbs-Duhem relation of the whole free
% energy comes first and splits, by f = f_B(rho) + f_n(Q, dQ), into parallel relations for p
% and sigma^n; the opening paragraph on the two roles of p was cut.
The interaction terms of \cref{eq:rho-full} form the passive flux
$-\xi_0^{-1}(\rho\nabla\mu_\rho+\Q\!:\!\nabla\bs\mu_Q)$, with the chemical potentials
$\mu_\rho=\delta F/\delta\rho$ and $\bs\mu_Q=\delta F/\delta\Q=-\H$. For a free energy
density $\mathfrak f(\rho,\Q,\partial_\r\Q)$ they are $\mu_\rho=\partial\mathfrak f/\partial\rho$
and
$\bs\mu_Q=\partial\mathfrak f/\partial\Q-\partial_\beta[\partial\mathfrak f/\partial(\partial_\beta\Q)]$.
Writing $\partial_\alpha\mathfrak f$ with the chain rule and inserting them gives the
Gibbs--Duhem relation~\cite{callen1985thermodynamics}, which turns the passive force density into the divergence of a
stress,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:gibbs-duhem}
  \rho\,\partial_\alpha\mu_\rho+\Q\!:\!\partial_\alpha\bs\mu_Q &= -\partial_\beta\sigma_{\alpha\beta},
  \nonumber\\
  \sigma_{\alpha\beta} &= -\bigl(\rho\mu_\rho+\Q\!:\!\bs\mu_Q-\mathfrak f\bigr)\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad-\partial_\alpha\Q\!:\!\frac{\partial\mathfrak f}{\partial(\partial_\beta\Q)} .
\end{align}
The free energy density \cref{eq:free-energy-choice} is the sum of the compression term
$\mathfrak f_B(\rho)=\tfrac B2(\rho-1)^2$ and a nematic part $\mathfrak f_n(\Q,\partial_\r\Q)$.
Since $\mu_\rho$ depends on $\mathfrak f_B$ alone and $\bs\mu_Q$ on $\mathfrak f_n$ alone,
\cref{eq:gibbs-duhem} splits into one relation for each part,
\begin{align}
  \rho\,\partial_\alpha\mu_\rho &= \partial_\alpha p,
  \qquad p=\rho\mu_\rho-\mathfrak f_B,
  \nonumber\\
  \Q\!:\!\partial_\alpha\bs\mu_Q &= -\partial_\beta\sigma^n_{\alpha\beta},
  \nonumber\\
  \sigma^n_{\alpha\beta} &= -\bigl(\Q\!:\!\bs\mu_Q-\mathfrak f_n\bigr)\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}
  -K\,\partial_\alpha\Q\!:\!\partial_\beta\Q ,
\end{align}
and the stress of \cref{eq:gibbs-duhem} is $\bs\sigma=-p\,\I+\bs\sigma^n$. The first relation is
the Gibbs--Duhem relation of a simple fluid~\cite{callen1985thermodynamics}, and its $p$ is
the pressure \cref{eq:pressure-definition}. In the second, the last term of $\bs\sigma^n$ is
$-\partial_\alpha\Q\!:\!\partial\mathfrak f_n/\partial(\partial_\beta\Q)$ for the Frank term
of \cref{eq:free-energy-choice}. The nematic stress $\bs\sigma^n$ is thus the sum of an
isotropic nematic pressure, whose local part is
$\tfrac a2\Q\!:\!\Q+\tfrac{3b}4(\Q\!:\!\Q)^2$, and the anisotropic Ericksen
stress~\cite{degennes1993physics,doostmohammadi2018active}. Since
$\bs\mu_Q=-\H$, its divergence is the elastic force, $\nabla\tcdot\bs\sigma^n=\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$,
and the passive flux is $\xi_0^{-1}\nabla\tcdot\bs\sigma$.

Dropping the elastic force (\cref{sec:model-elastic,app:closure}) drops $\bs\sigma^n$ and
leaves $-p\,\I$ as the passive stress of \cref{eq:velocity}, so the pressure that drives the
cells is the same $p$ whose work releases the elastic energy in \cref{app:extrusion}. The
nematic stresses are also small. On the Pad\'e profile \cref{eq:pade-approximation}, with the
bare $a$, $b$ and $K$ of \cref{tab:field-params}, every component stays below about
$6~\Pa\tcdot\micron$, of the order of the mean pressure. Both are far below the standard
deviation of the pressure, $\sigma_{p,\infty}=1.7~\kPa\tcdot\micron$, which is why
\cref{sec:nucleation-2} sets the mean pressure to zero.

\subsection{Definitions of the nematic order parameter}
\label{app:dean-normalisation}
We use the unnormalized $\Q$ of \cref{eq:density-definition} because it is linear in
$\varrho_t$, so its functional derivative is simple, whereas the normalized $\Q/\rho$
carries the additional term
$\delta(\Q/\rho)/\delta\varrho=(\hat\Q-\Q/\rho)\,\delta(\r-\r')/\rho$, which would crowd the
final equations. In exchange, the strength of the nematic order acquires a factor
$\rho^{-1}$, $S=\rho^{-1}\sqrt{(\Q\!:\!\Q)/2}$, which at $\rho=1$ is the $S$ of
\cref{sec:model-closure}, and the closure acquires factors of $\rho$. The two definitions
differ only at order $\rho-1$, so they coincide in the incompressible limit of large $B$.
The truncation of \cref{app:dean-angle} drops this order, and the Pad\'e profile
\cref{eq:pade-approximation} is accordingly written at $\rho=1$.

\section{Maximum-entropy closure}
\label{app:closure}
% dean.tex 2090-2262 (construction), 2263-2450 (realizability). Written 2026-10-02.
% Numbers at the fourth-generation set (N150, B = 9e4 steady state of the Lyapunov run),
% not dean.tex's (87.6% there is the old set). Checks: claude_experiments/appD_check.wls.

The $\Q$-equation \cref{eq:Q-full} and the correlators \cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators} of
its noises contain the angular moments $\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$,
$\langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho$ and $\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$,
which depend on the whole angular distribution and not only on $\rho$ and $\Q$. We close
them with the distribution of maximum entropy at given $\rho$ and $\Q$, compute its
moments exactly and expand them by the strength of the nematic order. 
Any turnucation of the expansion lead to patologies at the strong order of the defect: the
expansion gives a noise covariance with a negative eigenvalue (\cref{app:realizability})
and makes the transport driven by $\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$ ill posed (see below).

At a point $\r$, consider all angular distributions $\varrho(\theta) = \varrho_t(\theta \,|\, \r)$ with
$\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho=\rho$ and $\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\,\hat\Q=\Q$. Maximizing the
entropy $\int\!\d\theta\varrho\ln(1/\varrho)$ under these constraints, with a scalar and a
tensor Lagrange multiplier, gives $\varrho\propto\exp[\bs\lambda\!:\!\hat\Q(\theta)]$.
Since $\hat\Q$ contains only $\cos(2\theta)$ and $\sin(2\theta)$, this is a von Mises
distribution in $2\theta$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:von-mises}
  \varrho(\theta)=\frac{\rho\,\ee^{\kappa\cos2(\theta-\theta_0)}}{2\pi I_0(\kappa)},
\end{equation} 
where $\theta_0$ is the angle of the director, $\kappa\ge0$ sets how sharply the rods are
aligned, and $I_n$ is the modified Bessel function \cite{olver2010nist}. Because the distribution depends on
$\theta$ only through $2\theta$, it gives $\p$ and $-\p$ the same weight, and all its odd
harmonics vanish. The even ones are
$\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\,\ee^{2\ii n\theta}=\rho\,m_n\ee^{2\ii n\theta_0}$, with the
Bessel ratios $m_n=I_n(\kappa)/I_0(\kappa)$. The first of them gives
$\Q=\rho\,m_1\hat\Q(\theta_0)$. With the strength of the nematic order
$S=\rho^{-1}\sqrt{(\Q\!:\!\Q)/2}$ of \cref{app:dean-normalisation}, $\kappa$ therefore
follows from $\Q$ through
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kappa-S}
  \frac{I_1(\kappa)}{I_0(\kappa)}=S .
\end{equation}
The left side increases monotonically from $0$ to $1$, so this equation has exactly one
root for every $S<1$.

The other moments follow from the same harmonics. Each is an isotropic tensor function
of $\Q$, so it can be built from the $\J$ of \cref{eq:J-definition} and three further
tensors,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:tensor-basis}
  T_{\abmn ij}(\Q) &= Q_\ab J_{\mn ij}+Q_\mn J_{\ab ij}+Q_{ij}J_\abmn,
  \nonumber\\
  \U(\Q) &= 2\bigl[\Q\Q-\tfrac12(\Q\!:\!\Q)\,\J\bigr],
  \nonumber\\
  \W(\Q) &= \Q\Q\Q-\tfrac14(\Q\!:\!\Q)\,\bT(\Q),
\end{align}
where $\Q\Q$ and $\Q\Q\Q$ are outer products, $(\Q\Q)_\abmn=Q_\ab Q_\mn$. The tensor $\bT$
is linear in $\Q$, whereas $\U$ and $\W$ are quadratic and cubic, respectively. The
subtractions make
$\U$ and $\W$ totally symmetric and traceless, so they are pure fourth and sixth
harmonics. For $\Q=\hat\Q(\theta_0)$, for example, $U_{1111}=\cos4\theta_0$ and
$W_{111111}=\tfrac14\cos6\theta_0$. Integrating against \cref{eq:von-mises} then gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:exact-moments}
  \langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho &= \rho\,\J+\frac{g_2}{2\rho}\,\U(\Q),
  \nonumber\\
  \langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho &= 4\rho\,\J-\frac{2g_2}{\rho}\,\U(\Q),
  \nonumber\\
  \langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho &= \tfrac12\,\bT(\Q)+\frac{g_3}{\rho^2}\,\W(\Q),
\end{align}
with the coefficients
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:g-coefficients}
  g_2=\frac{m_2}{m_1^2}=\frac{I_0I_2}{I_1^2},
  \qquad
  g_3=\frac{m_3}{m_1^3}=\frac{I_0^2I_3}{I_1^3},
\end{equation}
both functions of $S$ through \cref{eq:kappa-S}. The coefficients of $\J$ and $\bT$ do not
depend on the shape of the distribution, which therefore enters only through $g_2$ and
$g_3$, and the single function $g_2$ controls both second moments.

The exact moments are not polynomials in $\Q$, but at weak order they simplify. Expanding
the Bessel functions at small
$\kappa$ and eliminating $\kappa$ with \cref{eq:kappa-S} gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:g-small-S}
  g_2=\tfrac12+\tfrac16S^2+O(S^4),
  \qquad
  g_3=\tfrac16+\tfrac18S^2+O(S^4).
\end{equation}
Since $\U=O(S^2)$ and $\W=O(S^3)$, whereas the leading terms are of order $1$ and $S$, the
$\U$ and $\W$ terms of \cref{eq:exact-moments} are of order $S^2$ relative to the leading
ones. Dropping them leaves the lowest-order closure
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:leading-closure}
  &\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho\simeq\rho\,\J,
  \quad
  \langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho\simeq4\rho\,\J,
  \quad \langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho\simeq\tfrac12\,\bT(\Q).
\end{align}
The first two close \cref{eq:Q-full} into \cref{eq:Q-closed}, and hence into
\cref{eq:model-Q}, and all three, inserted into \cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators}, give the
lowest-order noise correlators. The defect, however, is not weakly ordered. At $S=0.9$
the coefficients are $g_2=0.82$ and $g_3=0.55$, against the small-$S$ values $1/2$ and
$1/6$, so the lowest-order closure is not accurate around the defect. We nevertheless keep
the closed equation \cref{eq:model-Q}, without its $\nabla\H$ terms, as continuum theories
derived from particle models usually do
\cite{baskaran2008hydrodynamics,bertin2013,grossmann2016mesoscale,peshkov2012nonlinear}.
For the noise correlators, in contrast, the closure produces covariance matrices with a
negative eigenvalue, which makes the stochastic equations ill defined, so
\cref{app:realizability} builds the noise from the exact moments.

The closure also decides which passive terms the closed equations can keep. The first line
of \cref{eq:Q-full} transports the alignment of the rods with two passive terms, one driven
by the pressure through $\delta F/\delta\rho$ and one by the elastic force through
$\delta F/\delta\Q=-\H$. Both come from the velocity
$\xi_0^{-1}[-B\nabla\rho+\hat\Q(\theta)\!:\!\nabla\H]$ of a rod of orientation $\theta$
(\cref{sec:model-closure}): summed over the rods it gives the passive flux of the density,
and weighted with $\hat\Q$ the first line of \cref{eq:Q-full}. The same coefficient $\Q$
therefore couples the flux of $\Q$ to the pressure and the flux of the density to the
elastic force $\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$, so these two cross terms are Onsager partners. We
linearize about a uniform state with $\rho=1$ and order $\Q_0$ of strength $S$, keeping
both $\nabla\H$ terms. At large $B$ and short wavelengths the pressure builds up until its
gradient balances the elastic force on the cells, $B\,\delta\rho\simeq\Q_0\!:\!\delta\H$,
so the cells barely move. Their alignment is still transported: the elastic force carries
it forward with the second moment $\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$, and the balancing
pressure gradient carries it back with $\Q_0\Q_0$, the product of two first moments. The
first line of \cref{eq:Q-full} becomes
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:antidiffusion}
  \partial_t\delta\Q&\simeq-\frac1{\xi_0}\bigl(\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho-\Q_0\Q_0\bigr)\!:\!\nabla^2\delta\H
  \nonumber\\
  &\simeq-\frac K{\xi_0}\bigl(\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho-\Q_0\Q_0\bigr)\!:\!\nabla^4\delta\Q ,
\end{align}
with $\delta\H\simeq K\nabla^2\delta\Q$ at short wavelengths. The difference in the brackets is
the covariance of $\hat\Q$ over the rods, which cannot be negative, so for any distribution
of the rods this transport is a stable fourth-order diffusion. The lowest-order closure
replaces $\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$ by $\J$, which is too small at strong order.
For a change of the strength of the order, $\delta\Q\propto\Q_0$, the coefficient then
becomes $1-2S^2$, so the return flux outweighs the forward one for $S>1/\sqrt2$. Such
perturbations grow at the rate $(K/\xi_0)(2S^2-1)k^4$, which increases without bound with
the wavenumber $k$, so the equations are ill posed.

(\cref{sec:model-elastic}). Keeping the $\nabla\H$ term only in the flux of $\Q$ would also
remove the instability, but it would drop the elastic force from the flux of the density
alone, although both fluxes come from the same velocity of a rod, and its fourth-order
derivatives of $\Q$ would make the equations much harder to solve, numerically as well as
analytically.

\subsection{Solution of the noiseless density equation}
\label{app:density-poisson}
% dean.tex 820-903. Written 2026-10-02.
The nematic profile follows from $\Ha=0$. The ansatz $\Q=S(r)\hat\Q(\phi/2)$ of
\cref{eq:pade-approximation} has the components $Q_{xx}=S\cos\phi$ and $Q_{xy}=S\sin\phi$.
Both are angular harmonics of order one, on which the Laplacian acts as $S''+S'/r-S/r^2$,
and $\Q\!:\!\Q=2S^2$ turns the cubic term of \cref{eq:H-active} into $-2bS^3\hat\Q(\phi/2)$.
With $S_0$ and $\elld$ of \cref{eq:S0-elld}, the condition $\Ha=0$ therefore reduces to the
radial equation
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:S-radial}
  S''+\frac{S'}{r}-\frac{S}{r^2}=\frac{1}{\elld^2}\Bigl(\frac{S^2}{S_0^2}-1\Bigr)S,
\end{align}
with $S(0)=0$ at the core and $S\to S_0$ far from it. It has no closed-form solution. Far
from the core it gives $S\simeq S_0(1-\elld^2/2r^2)$, the far field of the Pad\'e profile
\cref{eq:pade-approximation}, which departs from the numerical solution of
\cref{eq:S-radial} by less than $10^{-2}\,S_0$, most near $r\simeq1.5\,\elld$. A higher-order
approximant of $f_\pade$, with $x=r/\elld$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pade-accurate}
  f(x)=x\left[\tfrac{0.34011+0.04517\,x^2+0.00234\,x^4}
  {1+0.38387\,x^2+0.04751\,x^4+0.00234\,x^6}\right]^{1/2}\!\!\!,
\end{equation}
departs from it by less than $10^{-4}S_0$. In all our calculations
we use \cref{eq:pade-accurate} in place of \cref{eq:pade-approximation}.

$B\nabla^2\rho=\nabla\tcdot\fa$. The active force \cref{eq:active-force-defect} points along
$x$ and depends on $r$ alone, so

$(S'+S/r)'$ equals $S''+S'/r-S/r^2$, the operator of \cref{eq:S-radial}, so
$\nabla\tcdot\fa=-\zeta\nabla^2(S\cos\phi)$ whatever the profile $S$, and the density obeys
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-harmonic}
  \nabla^2\Bigl[\rho+\frac{\zeta}{B}\,S(r)\cos\phi\Bigr]=0 .
\end{align}
We therefore write the density as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rho-h}
  \rho(r,\phi)=1-\frac{\zeta}{B}\,S(r)\cos\phi+h(r,\phi),\qquad \nabla^2h=0,
\end{equation}
with $h$ regular for $r\le R$ and fixed by the boundary condition. Every condition below is
proportional to $\cos\phi$, so $h=c+A\,r\cos\phi$, the only regular harmonic function with
this angular dependence up to a constant.

The reservoir boundary $\rho(R,\phi)=1$ of \cref{sec:defect-steady} gives $c=0$ and
$A=(\zeta/B)S(R)/R$, which is \cref{eq:rho-analytic}. Since $S(0)=0$ and $\cos\phi$ averages
to zero over the disk, the density at the core and the mean density are both one.

Leaving the density free at the boundary, $\partial_r\rho=0$, is not consistent. This
condition exerts no pressure gradient, so the active flux $\fa/\xi_0$ crosses the boundary, and
once the cross-coupling term $(B/\xi_0)\nabla\tcdot(\Q\nabla\rho)$ of \cref{eq:model-Q} breaks
the antisymmetry of the defect, inflow and outflow no longer cancel. Cells then enter or leave
the disk, and no steady state is reached.

A no-flux boundary imposes $\nh\tcdot(B\nabla\rho-\fa)=0$, that is
$B\,\partial_r\rho=f^\text{a}_r$ at $r=R$, with the radial active force
$f^\text{a}_r=-\zeta(S'+S/r)\cos\phi$. This gives $A=-(\zeta/B)S(R)/R$, and since the number
of cells is conserved, the mean density stays one and $c=0$. Thus
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-noflux}
  \rho(r,\phi)=1-\frac{\zeta}{B}\Bigl[S(r)+\frac rR\,S(R)\Bigr]\cos\phi .
\end{align}
The cells that the active force drives against this boundary pile up there, so the linear
term has the opposite slope. Both boundaries change the density across the whole disk, but
only at order $\zeta/B$, far below the scale of its fluctuations, and

\cref{app:comoving}.

Without the $\nabla\H$ terms (\cref{sec:model-elastic}) and the noises, and with
$\partial_t\to\partial_t-\u\tcdot\nabla$, \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} give the steady-state
equations
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:comoving-equations}
\begin{align}
    0 ={}& \u\tcdot\nabla\rho + \frac1{\xi_0}\nabla\tcdot
    \bigl(B\nabla\rho - \fa\bigr),
    \\
    0 ={}& \nabla\tcdot\Bigl[\Q\Bigl(\u+\frac B{\xi_0}\nabla\rho\Bigr)\Bigr]
    + \frac4{\xir}\Ha .
    \label{eq:comoving-equations-Q}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

We drop the advection term $\u\tcdot\nabla\rho$ of the density equation. At large $B$ the
pressure gradient balances the active force, $B\nabla\rho\simeq\fa$, so at distance $r$ from
the core the dropped term is smaller than the flux divergence by $\xi_0ur/B$. $B$ then drops out of the problem: $\Q_\ss$ and $\u$ do not
depend on $B$, and $\rho_\ss-1\propto1/B$ (\cref{fig:rho1d-dirichlet}).


The frame velocity $\u$ has no closed form, and \cref{app:drift} estimates it only at first
order in the cross-coupling. It enters the numerical problem as an extra unknown, fixed by the
condition that the core stays at the origin. We solve \cref{eq:comoving-equations} by finite
differences in the square box of side $40~\micron$, for $\rho$, $Q_1=Q_{xx}$ and
$Q_2=Q_{xy}$ at every node of the grid, and for $u_x$ and $u_y$. On the boundaries of the box we impose the
reservoir $\rho=1$ of \cref{sec:defect-steady} and $\nh\tcdot\nabla\Q=0$, so that the boundary
forces no nematic axis. Starting from the Pad\'e profile, we impose the shape of the defect
only by pinning the core, $Q_1(0,0)=Q_2(0,0)=0$, and by setting $Q_2(x,0)=0$ on the axis, with
$x Q_1(x,0)>0$. These constraints are enough to keep the solution a $+1/2$ defect.

In this box $u_x=0.24~\micron/\minute$, along $+x$ as \cref{sec:defect-steady} explains, and
$|u_y|<10^{-10}~\micron/\minute$. The value of $u_x$ depends on the boundary conditions and on
the size of the box (\cref{eq:drift-radial}). The cross-coupling also deforms the defect,
weakly near the core and more away from it, where the solution deviates from the Pad\'e
profile (\cref{fig:comoving-deformation}(a,b) and \cref{fig:nematicaxis}). The cross-coupling
and the motion of the defect also shift the active force away from
\cref{eq:active-force-defect} (\cref{fig:comoving-deformation}(c)).

\label{app:drift}

In one term of the $\Q$-equation, the anisotropic transport of nematic order by
self-propulsion, we replace the lowest-order closure by the exact moments of the
maximum-entropy distribution in \cref{eq:exact-moments}, which \cref{app:realizability} already uses for the noise. With
this term the defect moves along $-x$, toward its head, instead of toward its tail as in
\cref{app:comoving}. This is the direction that the Supplementary Note of
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} reports, in polar coordinates oriented as ours.

With reversals, self-propulsion makes each rod diffuse along its own axis, with the
diffusivity $v_0^2/(2\frev)=2\zeta/\xi_0$ of the second line of \cref{eq:final-langevin}, and
each rod carries its alignment with it. In the $\Q$-equation this diffusion gives the third
line of \cref{eq:Q-full},
$(\zeta/\xi_0)(\nabla^2Q_\ab+\partial_i\partial_j\langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_{ij}\rangle_\varrho)$.
The first part is isotropic and enters $K'$ in \cref{eq:H-active}. The second part is
anisotropic, because the alignment moves along the axis of the rods and not across it, so it
depends on the angular distribution of the rods. The lowest-order closure
\cref{eq:leading-closure} reduces it to
$(\zeta/\xi_0)(\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta-\tfrac12\delta_\ab\nabla^2)\rho$, which is
smaller than the cross-coupling $(B/\xi_0)\nabla\tcdot(\Q\nabla\rho)$ by $\zeta/B$ and is
dropped (\cref{app:dean-angle}). The exact moments \cref{eq:exact-moments} instead add
$(g_2/2\rho)\,\U(\Q)$ to $\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$, with the fourth-harmonic tensor
$\U$ of \cref{eq:tensor-basis} and the coefficient $g_2$ of \cref{eq:g-coefficients}, and so add
to the right-hand side of the $\Q$-equation the term
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-U-term}
  T_\ab=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\,\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\Bigl[\frac{g_2}{2\rho}\,U_{\ab \mu\nu}(\Q)\Bigr].
\end{equation}
We add $w\bT$ to the right-hand side of the comoving equation \cref{eq:comoving-equations-Q},
with a weight $w$ that runs from $w=0$, the closure of the main text, to $w=1$, the exact
moments in this term. Everything else, including the closure of all other terms and the
parameters of \cref{tab:field-params}, stays as it is.

Since $\bT$ contains no density gradient, the large modulus $B$ does not suppress it. Its size
is set instead by the amplitude $g_2S^2$ of the fourth angular harmonic of the distribution
\cref{eq:von-mises}, which is $0.66$ at $S=0.9$. Near the core $\bT$ is therefore of order
$\zeta g_2S^2/(\xi_0\elld^2)$, the same order as the cross-coupling,
$\zeta S^2/(\xi_0\elld^2)$, because $B\nabla\rho\simeq\fa=-\zeta\nabla\tcdot\Q$
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}). The lowest-order closure thus drops a term as large as the one
that moves the defect.

On the defect, $\bT$ takes a simple form. We combine $Q_1=Q_{xx}$ and $Q_2=Q_{xy}$ of
\cref{app:comoving} into the complex field $\Psi=Q_1+\ii Q_2$ and write
$\bar\partial=\partial_x-\ii\partial_y$. Expanding \cref{eq:tensor-basis} in components then
gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-complex}
  T_{xx}+\ii T_{xy}=\frac{\zeta}{2\xi_0}\,\bar\partial^{\,2}\Bigl(\frac{g_2}{\rho}\,\Psi^2\Bigr).
\end{equation}
The Pad\'e profile \cref{eq:pade-approximation} is $\Psi_0=S(r)\,\ee^{\ii\phi}$. At
$\rho\simeq1$ we therefore have $g_2\Psi_0^2=F(r)\,\ee^{2\ii\phi}$ with $F=g_2S^2$, and
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-T-profile}
  \bar\partial^{\,2}\bigl(F\,\ee^{2\ii\phi}\bigr)=F''+\frac3r\,F' .
\end{equation}
On the static defect $\bT$ thus has no angular dependence and adds to $Q_1$ alone. At the core
it equals $2(\zeta/\xi_0)S'(0)^2$, about twice the cross-coupling, which has the opposite
sign. In the tail, where the director lies along the axis, $Q_1>0$, and in the head, where the director
lies across, $Q_1<0$. A positive source of $Q_1$ therefore shifts the zero of $\Psi$, the
core, toward $-x$. The cross-coupling acts the other way: it carries $\Q$ with the velocity
$-B\nabla\rho/\xi_0$, which points along $+x$ at the core (\cref{sec:defect-steady}).

The drift has no closed form (\cref{app:comoving}), but the competition between the two
transports can be estimated at first order. In the laboratory frame, the $\Q$-equation with
$wT$ added, of which \cref{eq:comoving-equations-Q} is the comoving steady form, splits into the
relaxation and the two transports,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-Q-split}
  \partial_t\Q=\frac4{\xir}\Ha+\mathbf{s},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{s}=\frac B{\xi_0}\nabla\tcdot(\Q\nabla\rho)+w\,\bT,
\end{equation}
with $\rho$ of \cref{eq:rho-analytic}. Like $\Q$, the tensor $\mathbf{s}$ is symmetric and
traceless, so its complex form $s=s_{xx}+\ii s_{xy}$ carries all of it. The relaxation lowers
an effective free energy that does not change when the defect is displaced, so a displaced
profile $\Psi_0(\r-\mathbf{a})$ is also a steady state of the relaxation alone. For a small
displacement along $x$, it differs from $\Psi_0$ by a multiple of the translation mode
$\partial_x\Psi_0$, which the linearized relaxation therefore maps to zero. This linearization
is self-adjoint, because it is the second variation of a free energy. When $s$ is weak, the
defect moves with only a small change of its shape, $\Psi\simeq\Psi_0(\r-\u t)$, and
projecting the complex form of \cref{eq:drift-Q-split} onto the translation mode removes this
unknown change of shape and leaves the estimate
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-solvability}
  u_x\simeq-\frac{\la\partial_x\Psi_0,s\ra}{\la\partial_x\Psi_0,\partial_x\Psi_0\ra},
  \qquad
  \la a,b\ra=\mathrm{Re}\!\int\!\d^2r\;a\,b^*,
\end{equation}
with $s$ evaluated on the static profile. Neither transport is small against the relaxation,
since their ratio to it is $\zeta\xir S_0/(4\xi_0K')=0.45$, so we expect
\cref{eq:drift-solvability} to give the trend of the drift rather than its value.

The translation mode contains only the angular harmonics $0$ and $2$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-translation}
  \partial_x\Psi_0=\frac12\Bigl(S'+\frac Sr\Bigr)+\frac12\Bigl(S'-\frac Sr\Bigr)\ee^{2\ii\phi}.
\end{equation}
Of the harmonics of $s=\sum_ms_m(r)\,\ee^{\ii m\phi}$, only $s_0$ and $s_2$ therefore
contribute, and on a disk of radius $R$ \cref{eq:drift-solvability} reads
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-radial}
  u_x\simeq-\frac{\int_0^R\bigl[(S'+S/r)\,s_0+(S'-S/r)\,s_2\bigr]\,r\,\d r}
  {\int_0^R(S'^2+S^2/r^2)\,r\,\d r}.
\end{equation}
Its denominator grows as $S_0^2\ln(R/\elld)$, the elastic energy of the far field, so the
drift depends on the size of the boundary and slows down as the boundary moves away.

Since $s$ is linear in $w$, so is the estimate, $u_x\simeq u_a+w\,u_b$, where $u_a$ comes
from the cross-coupling and $u_b$ from $\bT$. For the cross-coupling, \cref{eq:rho-analytic}
gives $s_0$ and $s_2$ in closed form, and \cref{eq:drift-radial} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-ua}
  u_a=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\,
  \frac{\int_0^R\bigl(S'^2P'+SS'\Delta S+S^2P/r^3\bigr)\,r\,\d r}{\int_0^R(S'^2+S^2/r^2)\,r\,\d r},
\end{equation}
with $P=S-(r/R)\,S(R)$ the radial factor of \cref{eq:rho-analytic} and
$\Delta S=S''+S'/r-S/r^2$. The term $T$ has the harmonic $0$ alone,
$s_0=w(\zeta/2\xi_0)(F''+3F'/r)$, which gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-ub}
  u_b=-\frac{\zeta}{2\xi_0}\,
  \frac{\int_0^R(S'+S/r)(F''+3F'/r)\,r\,\d r}{\int_0^R(S'^2+S^2/r^2)\,r\,\d r}.
\end{equation}
With the parameters of \cref{tab:field-params} and $R=20~\micron$,
$u_a=0.28~\micron/\minute$ and $u_b=-0.51~\micron/\minute$, so the estimate reverses the drift
before $w$ reaches one (dashed lines in \cref{fig:drift-closure}). With the small-$S$ value
$g_2=1/2$ of \cref{eq:g-small-S} in place of $g_2(S)$, $u_b$ is smaller in magnitude, so the
strong order of the defect matters too.

In the solver of \cref{app:comoving}, with its box, boundary conditions and grid, \unclear{we continue
the steady state from $w=0$ to $w=1$}. As $w$ grows, $u_x$ decreases and changes sign, so with
the exact moments in $\bT$ the defect moves toward its head (markers in
\cref{fig:drift-closure}). The estimate overshoots the numerical drift but captures its trend.
With $g_2=1/2$ the defect also moves toward its head at $w=1$, but only slowly.

Friction anisotropy does not reverse the drift. Copenhagen \textit{et al.}~\cite{copenhagen2021}
attribute the asymmetry of the flows around defects to a friction that is stronger across the
cells than along them, $\xi_0(\I-\varepsilon\hat\Q)$ for a single rod with the anisotropy
$\varepsilon$. In our model it acts only on the passive force, because self-propulsion sets the
velocity of the rods directly, and to first order in $\varepsilon$ and in the activity it adds
to the $\Q$-equation only the angular harmonics $\ee^{\pm\ii\phi}$ and $\ee^{\pm3\ii\phi}$,
which do not project onto the translation mode \cref{eq:drift-translation}. The drift
therefore changes only at higher order.


\section{Density variance with a short-length cutoff}
\label{app:variance}
% dean.tex 904-1211 ("Only density fluctuations"). Supports \cref{sec:defect-fluct}.
% Written 2026-10-02. Checks: claude_experiments/appF_check.wls (transforms, asymptotes,
% pedestal, inversion), fit_new.py (nu0 vs l_n at S0 = 0.9, l_d = 1), appF_corr.wls
% (lagged and two-point correlators with the Q_ss term).
The layering probability depends exponentially on the equal-point density variance,
so the few percent by which the defect modulates that variance decide the result of
\cref{sec:nucleation-2}. Here we derive the variance from \cref{eq:rho-fourier} at
$\delta\Q=0$, together with the correlation time and length that \cref{sec:defect-fluct}
quotes. Two features of the calculation need care: the variance of a continuum diverges,
so it depends on a short-length cutoff, and the nematic order makes the noise
anisotropic.

With the Fourier conventions $\tilde f(\k)=\int\!\d^2\r\,\ee^{-\ii\k\cdot\r}f(\r)$ in space and
$\tilde f(\omega)=\int\!\d t\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t}f(t)$ in time, the noise correlator
\cref{eq:eta-rho-ss} transforms to
\begin{multline}
  \la\tilde \eta^\rho_\alpha(\k,\omega)\tilde \eta^\rho_\beta(\k',\omega')\ra = \frac{4\pi\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}\,\delta(\omega+\omega')\,\times
  \\
  \times
  \left[(2\pi)^2 \delta_\ab \delta(\k+\k')\;+\;\tilde Q^\ss_\ab(\k+\k')\right].
\end{multline}
and \cref{eq:rho-fourier} gives
\begin{multline}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,\omega)\delta\tilde\rho(\k',\omega') \ra= \frac{4\pi\zeta\,\delta(\omega+\omega')}{\xi_0\rho_0\,[\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2]\![-\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k'^2]}\times
  \\
  \times
  \!\left[(2\pi)^2 k^2\,\delta(\k+\k')\;-\;k_\alpha k'_\beta\,\tilde Q^\ss_\ab(\k+\k')\right].
\end{multline}
Transforming back to $(t,t')$, the $\delta(\omega+\omega')$ removes one frequency integral.
Partial fractions of the remaining denominator,
\begin{multline}
    \frac{1}{[\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2]\,[-\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k'^2]} = 
    \\
    =\frac{1}{\gamma_0(k^2+k'^2)}\!\left[\frac{1}{\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2}\;+\;\frac{1}{-\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k'^2}\right],
\end{multline}
and the identity $\int\!\frac{\d\omega}{2\pi}\ee^{\ii\omega t}/(\ii\omega+a) = \theta(t)\ee^{-at}$ applied to each term give
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,t)\delta\tilde\rho(\k',t')\ra = \frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\,\gamma_0(k^2+k'^2)}\times
  \\
  \times \left[\theta(t-t')\ee^{-\gamma_0 k^2(t-t')}+\theta(t'-t)\ee^{-\gamma_0 k'^2(t'-t)}\right]
  \\
  \times\!\left[(2\pi)^2 k^2\delta(\k+\k') - k_ik'_j\tilde{Q}^\ss_{ij}(\k+\k')\right].
\end{multline}

At equal times, $t=t'$, the support of $\delta(\k+\k')$ sets $k^2=(k^2+k'^2)/2$ in the
first term, and the spectrum becomes
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-spectrum}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k)\,\delta\tilde\rho(\k')\ra
  = \frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\Bigl[{}&(2\pi)^2\delta(\k+\k')
  \nonumber\\
  &-\frac{2k_ik'_j\,\tilde Q^\ss_{ij}(\k+\k')}{k^2+k'^2}\Bigr].
\end{align}
The nematic field enters only through the second term.

\subsection{Isotropic case}
\label{app:variance-iso}
Without nematic order, $\Q_\ss=0$, only the first term of
\cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln} remains. On the support of
$\delta(\k+\k')$ we have $k'^2=k^2$, so the two exponentials combine into one, and
\begin{equation}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,t)\,\delta\tilde\rho(\k',t')\ra=(2\pi)^2\delta(\k+\k')\,
  \frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\,\ee^{-\gamma_0k^2|t-t'|}.
\end{equation}
Inverting over space gives a heat kernel,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rho-correlation-no-cutoff}
  \la\delta\rho(\r,t)\,\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\,
  \frac{\ee^{-|\r-\r'|^2/(4\gamma_0|t-t'|)}}{4\pi\gamma_0|t-t'|},
\end{equation}
and as $t'\to t$ it tends to $(\zeta/\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0)\,\delta(\r-\r')$. At equal times the density at two different points is
therefore uncorrelated, and the variance at one point diverges.

\subsection{The case with uniform nematic order}
\label{app:variance-uniform}
Let the nematic order be uniform, with strength $S$ and the director along $x$, so
that $Q^\ss_{xx}=-Q^\ss_{yy}=S$ and $Q^\ss_{xy}=0$. Its transform is
$\tilde\Q_\ss(\bs p)=(2\pi)^2\delta(\bs p)\,\Q_\ss$, so the second term of
\cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln} sits on $\k'=-\k$ as well, where it
becomes $(2\pi)^2\delta(\k+\k')\,(k_x^2-k_y^2)S$. Both terms then sit on $\k'=-\k$, where
$k'^2=k^2$, so the two exponentials combine into $\ee^{-\gamma_0k^2|t-t'|}$ and
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:rho-spectrum-uniform}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,t)\,\delta\tilde\rho(\k',t')\ra
  =(2\pi)^2\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\,\delta(\k+\k')\times
  \\
  \times\bigl[1+S\cos2\psi\bigr]\,\ee^{-\gamma_0k^2|t-t'|},
\end{multline}
with $\psi$ the angle between $\k$ and the director ($x$ axis). Uniform order thus keeps the
spectrum diagonal and only multiplies it by $1+S\cos2\psi$, which averages to one over the
directions of $\k$. A correlation at a single point integrates over all directions, so the
autocorrelation in time at a fixed point is exactly the isotropic
$(\zeta/\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0)/(4\pi\gamma_0|t-t'|)$ for every $t-t'$, and the equal-point
variance is that of the isotropic case as well.

Two distinct points, however, weight the directions of $\k$ unequally. Let $\r'-\r$
make the angle $\alpha$ with the director. The angular integral of
$\ee^{\ii\k\cdot(\r'-\r)}\cos2\psi$ gives $-2\pi J_2(k|\r'-\r|)\cos2\alpha$ \prompt{did we ever define $J_2$?}, and the
remaining radial integrals are elementary,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:corr-uniform}
  &\la\delta\rho(\r,t)\,\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\,\frac{1}{4\pi\gamma_0|t-t'|}
  \Bigl[\ee^{-u}-S\cos2\alpha\,g(u)\Bigr],
  \nonumber\\
  &g(u)=\frac{1-(1+u)\,\ee^{-u}}{u},\qquad u=\frac{|\r-\r'|^2}{4\gamma_0|t-t'|}.
\end{align}
The first term is the isotropic heat kernel. The second vanishes at $\r'=\r$, because
$g(u)\simeq u/2$ at small $u$, but at large separation, where $g(u)\simeq1/u$, it decays
only as $-\zeta S\cos2\alpha/(\pi\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0|\r-\r'|^2)$. This tail does not depend on the
time difference $|t-t'|$, and it comes from $k\to0$, where the direction factor is not analytic and the modes relax
arbitrarily slowly. Such power-law correlations are generic when the noise and the
relaxation of a conserved density have different
anisotropies~\cite{grinstein1990conservation,garrido1990longrange,mahdisoltani2021longrange}. At equal times the heat kernel collapses to a $\delta$ function and
only the tail is left at finite separation,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:corr-uniform-equal-time}
  \la\delta\rho(\r)\,\delta\rho(\r+\bs d)\ra
  =\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\Bigl[\delta(\bs d)-\frac{S\cos2\alpha}{\pi d^2}\Bigr].
\end{equation}
Uniform order thus anticorrelates two points along the director and correlates them across
it, whereas without order they are uncorrelated at equal times.

\subsection{Short-length cutoff}
\label{app:variance-cutoff}

The equal-point and -time variance of \cref{eq:rho-correlation-no-cutoff} is infinite, because the
flat isotropic spectrum integrates to infinity over $\k$. The anisotropic part, from the
second term of \cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln}, grows only
logarithmically with the largest wavevector (\cref{app:variance-ln}), so without a cutoff
the relative modulation by the defect vanishes. We suppress large wavevectors with a
Gaussian filter,
\begin{equation}
  \delta\tilde\rho(\k,\omega) =
  -\frac{\ii\,\k\cdot\tilde \bEta_\rho(\k,\omega)}{\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2} W(\k),
  \qquad W(\k)=\ee^{-k^2\elln^2/4}.
\end{equation}
so that the integrals below stay Gaussian. We choose the factor $1/4$ in the exponent so
that the correlation length of the density in the isotropic case equals $\elln$.

The correlator \cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln} thus carries the factor
$\ee^{-(k^2+k'^2)\elln^2/4}$, which on the support of $\delta(\k+\k')$ becomes
$\ee^{-k^2\elln^2/2}$. The isotropic spectrum of \cref{app:variance-iso} then integrates to
a finite equal-point variance,
\begin{equation}
  \sigmarho=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\int\!\frac{\d^2\k}{(2\pi)^2}\,
  \ee^{-k^2\elln^2/2}=\frac{\zeta}{2\pi\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0\elln^2},
\end{equation}
which with $\xi_0\gamma_0=B$ is the pedestal \cref{eq:pedestal}. The filter $W$ depends on
$|\k|$ alone, so uniform nematic order still leaves the pedestal and the autocorrelation in
time at a point unchanged (\cref{app:variance-uniform}).

At unequal times the isotropic spectrum carries the relaxation factor
$\ee^{-\gamma_0k^2|t-t'|}$ besides the smoothing, and inverting over space gives
\begin{equation}
  \la\delta\rho(\r,t)\,\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}
  \int\!\frac{\d^2\k}{(2\pi)^2}\,\ee^{\ii\k\cdot(\r-\r')}\,\ee^{-a k^2},
\end{equation}
with $a=\gamma_0|t-t'|+\elln^2/2$, so relaxation over the time $|t-t'|$ acts like a wider
filter. The integral is Gaussian,
\begin{equation}
  \int\!\frac{\d^2\k}{(2\pi)^2}\,\ee^{\ii\k\cdot\bs d-ak^2}=\frac{\ee^{-d^2/4a}}{4\pi a},
\end{equation}
and with $\xi_0\gamma_0=B$ it gives \cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic}: a Gaussian in
$\r-\r'$ of width $\elln$ at equal times, whose normalization $1/4\pi a$ has halved after
the correlation time $\tau_c$ of \cref{eq:tau-c}.

\subsection{The anisotropic piece}
\label{app:variance-aniso}
With the cutoff, the equal-point variance is the integral of
\cref{eq:rho-spectrum} over $\k$ and $\k'$, weighted by
$\ee^{\ii(\k+\k')\cdot\r}\ee^{-(k^2+k'^2)\elln^2/4}$. Its second term depends on the sum
$\bs p=\k+\k'$, which is conjugate to the slow variation of $\Q_\ss$, and on the internal
wavevector $\bs q=(\k-\k')/2$. We therefore change variables to
\begin{equation}
  \k=\tfrac12\bs p+\bs q,\qquad\k'=\tfrac12\bs p-\bs q,
\end{equation}
which has unit Jacobian, $\d^2\k\,\d^2\k'=\d^2\bs p\,\d^2\bs q$. In these variables
\begin{align}
  k_ik'_j&=\tfrac14p_ip_j-q_iq_j+\tfrac12(q_ip_j-p_iq_j),
  \nonumber\\
  k^2+k'^2&=\tfrac12p^2+2q^2 .
\end{align}
The last term of $k_ik'_j$ is antisymmetric and drops against the symmetric
$\tilde\Q_\ss$, and the smoothing factor splits into
$\ee^{-z}\ee^{-q^2\elln^2/2}$ with $z=p^2\elln^2/8$. The anisotropic
part of the variance thus reads
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:aniso-pq}
  \sigmarho(\r)-\sigmarho\big|_\text{pedestal}
  =-\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\int\!\frac{\d^2\bs p}{(2\pi)^2}\,\ee^{\ii\bs p\cdot\r}\,
  \ee^{-z}\,\tilde Q^\ss_{ij}(\bs p)
  \\
  \times\int\!\frac{\d^2\bs q}{(2\pi)^2}\,
  \frac{\tfrac14p_ip_j-q_iq_j}{p^2/2+2q^2}\,\ee^{-q^2\elln^2/2}.
\end{multline}
The rest of the inner integrand depends only on $q=|\bs q|$, so $q_iq_j$ averages over the
directions of $\bs q$ to $\tfrac12q^2\delta_{ij}$, which vanishes against the traceless
$\tilde\Q_\ss$. The $p_ip_j$ part leaves an elementary integral,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:aniso-inner}
  \int\!\frac{\d^2\bs q}{(2\pi)^2}\,\frac{\ee^{-q^2\elln^2/2}}{p^2/2+2q^2}
  =\frac{\ee^{z}E_1(z)}{8\pi},
\end{equation}
with $E_1$ the exponential integral. Its $\ee^{z}$ cancels the external
$\ee^{-z}$ exactly. Since $p_ip_j\tilde Q^\ss_{ij}$ is minus the Fourier
transform of $\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}$, the anisotropic variance is the double
divergence of the nematic field passed through a filter,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:aniso-kernel}
  &\sigmarho(\r)-\sigmarho\big|_\text{pedestal}
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{\zeta}{8\pi\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\!\int\!\frac{\d^2\bs p}{(2\pi)^2}\,
  \ee^{\ii\bs p\cdot\r}\,\hat\Phi(p)\,\widetilde{\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}}(\bs p),
  \nonumber\\
  &\hat\Phi(p)=\tfrac12E_1\bigl(p^2\elln^2/8\bigr).
\end{align}
No expansion and no fitted scale enter it. 
The filter interpolates between two branches,
\begin{alignat}{2}
  \label{eq:Phi-asymptotics}
  \hat\Phi(p)&\simeq\ln\frac1{p\elln}+\frac{3\ln2-\gamma_\mathrm{E}}2
  \quad &(p\elln\ll1),
  \nonumber\\
  \hat\Phi(p)&\simeq\frac4{p^2\elln^2}\,\ee^{-p^2\elln^2/8}
  \quad &(p\elln\gg1),
\end{alignat}
with $\gamma_\mathrm{E}$ Euler's constant. Variations of the nematic field on scales
larger than $\elln$ are therefore weighted logarithmically, and those below $\elln$
are cut off exponentially.

The inverse transform of $\hat\Phi$ is the real-space kernel
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kappa-real}
  \kappa(d) = \frac{1-\ee^{-2d^2/\elln^2}}{2\pi\,d^2},
\end{equation}
a function of the distance $d$ between two points. It is finite at the origin,
$\kappa(0)=1/(\pi\elln^2)$, and beyond $\elln$ it falls off as $1/(2\pi d^2)$, whose
integral produces the logarithm.
With the convolution $(\kappa\star g)(\r)=\int\!\d^2\bs d\,\kappa(d)\,g(\r+\bs d)$, dividing by
the pedestal gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:variance-kernel}
  \frac{\sigmarho(\r)}{\sigmarho|_\text{pedestal}}
  = 1 + \frac{\elln^2}{4}\bigl(\kappa\star\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}\bigr)(\r).
\end{equation}

\subsection{The variance as an average over neighbors}
\label{app:variance-neighbors}
Two integrations by parts move the derivatives in \cref{eq:variance-kernel} from the nematic
field onto the kernel and give
\begin{equation}
  (\kappa\star\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij})(\r)
  =\int\!\d^2\bs d\;\partial_i\partial_j\kappa(d)\,Q^\ss_{ij}(\r+\bs d),
\end{equation}
without boundary terms, because $\kappa$ and $\kappa'$ decay as $d^{-2}$ and $d^{-3}$,
whereas
$\Q_\ss$ and its gradient stay bounded. For the radial kernel,
$\partial_i\partial_j\kappa=\kappa''\hat d_i\hat d_j+(\kappa'/d)(\delta_{ij}-\hat d_i\hat d_j)$

\begin{align}
  \label{eq:variance-neighbors}
  \frac{\sigmarho(\r)}{\sigmarho|_\text{pedestal}}-1

\section{Lyapunov solver for the coupled fluctuations}
\label{app:lyapunov}
\prompt{I would shorten the intro. Keep the useful parts}
% dean.tex 1521-2089.
The analytic variance \cref{eq:total-gauss} neglects the force
$\zeta\nabla\cdot\delta\Q$ of the nematic fluctuations, which is appropriate because
$\delta\Q$ changes so slowly that the
density follows it quasi-statically and shifts only by about $(\zeta/B)\,\delta\Q$
(\cref{sec:defect-fluct}). To test this, and to obtain the variance without the
approximation, we solve the linearized equations \cref{eq:linearized} numerically for the
equal-time covariance of $(\delta\rho,\delta\Q)$ on the steady background of
\cref{app:comoving}. The solver implements the cutoff \cref{eq:cutoff} by
giving the noise a spatial correlation, chosen to reproduce the pedestal
\cref{eq:pedestal} (\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff}).

\Cref{app:lyapunov-method} turns the linearized equations into a Lyapunov equation for the
covariance and states when it has a valid solution. The next three subsections make the
discretized noise satisfy these conditions: the noise covariance
needs the exact moments of the maximum-entropy angular distribution to be positive
semi-definite
(\cref{app:realizability}), the drift and the noise need matching difference operators
(\cref{app:lyapunov-operators}), and the spatial correlation of the noise, which imposes the cutoff, needs a
calibration to the grid (\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff}). \Cref{app:lyapunov-walls} gives the
boundary conditions, and \cref{app:lyapunov-results} compares the solution with the analytic
variance and shows the fluctuations of the active force.

\subsection{Method}
\label{app:lyapunov-method}
% Written 2026-10-02 from dean.tex 1521-2047 and lyapunov_solver/ (lyapunov_solver.m,
% lyap_solve.py). Numbers: claude_experiments/appG_numbers.wls, appG_force.wls, and the
% job log jobscripts/logs/1773058_2.out (N150, B = 9e4).
In the variables $\psi=(\delta\rho,\delta Q_1,\delta Q_2)$ the linearized equations
\cref{eq:linearized} are a linear Ornstein--Uhlenbeck field theory,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ou-model}
  \partial_t\psi=\A\psi+\bxi,
  \qquad
  \la\bxi(\r,t)\,\bxi(\r',t')^\T\ra=\mathbf N(\r,\r')\,\delta(t-t'),
\end{equation}
where the drift $\A$ and the noise covariance $\mathbf N$ are linear operators on fields.
Both contain derivatives, $\mathbf N$ because the flux noises $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$ enter
through their divergence. The flux correlators are \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss,eq:Q-noise-correlators},
evaluated with the exact moments of \cref{app:realizability}. Since the noise is white in
time, It\^o's lemma applied to $\psi\psi^\T$ turns stationarity into an algebraic condition
on the equal-time covariance $\mathbf C(\r,\r')=\la\psi(\r,t)\,\psi(\r',t)^\T\ra$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:lyapunov}
  \A\,\mathbf C+\mathbf C\,\A^\T+\mathbf N=0,
\end{equation}
with $\A$ acting on the first argument of $\mathbf C$ and $\A^\T$ on the second. This step
needs only the delta function in time, so the spatial form of $\mathbf N$ is free, which lets
\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff} give the noise a finite correlation length. \Cref{eq:lyapunov} is
the Lyapunov equation of control theory \cite{lyapunov1992general,zhou1996robust}. Its
solution, $\mathbf C=\int_0^\infty\!\d t\,\ee^{\A t}\,\mathbf N\,\ee^{\A^\T t}$, adds up the
noise injected at every earlier time and carried to the present by the drift
\cite{zhou1996robust,simoncini2016computational}.

On a grid, \cref{eq:lyapunov} is a matrix equation with a unique positive semi-definite
solution if
($i$)~every eigenvalue of the discretized $\A$ has a negative real part, so that the steady
state is linearly stable, and
($ii$)~the discretized $\mathbf N$ is positive semi-definite, so that it is a covariance at
all.

We use a uniform grid of $N_\mathrm{int}=200$ interior nodes per direction on
$[-20,20]^2~\micron^2$, with spacing $\Delta x=40/201~\micron=0.199~\micron$, so the state
vector has $3N_\mathrm{int}^2=120\,000$ entries. Two-dimensional
operators are Kronecker products of one-dimensional ones.
The covariance is a dense matrix, and we solve \cref{eq:lyapunov} directly: a real
Schur decomposition of the discretized $\A$ is followed by a recursive Bartels--Stewart
back-substitution \cite{bartels1972solution}, which keeps most of the work in matrix
products. The Schur decomposition also yields the spectrum of $\A$, and in our run every
eigenvalue has a negative real part, so condition ($i$) holds.

\subsection{Realizability of the noise covariance}
\label{app:realizability}
% Moved here from the closure appendix (2026-10-05); only the opening and the references
% to the run were rewritten.
Condition ($ii$) is not automatic. The noise covariance $\mathbf N$ is assembled from the
local covariance of the noise fluxes at each point (\cref{app:lyapunov-operators}), so it is
positive semi-definite only if that local covariance is. Otherwise some combination of the
noises would have a negative variance, no noise could have the correlators
\cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators}, and \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} would be ill defined \prompt{many clauses, hard to read}. The
local covariance comes from the moments that the closure of \cref{app:closure} assigns to
the angular distribution, and the lowest-order closure keeps it valid only for weak order,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:realizability-condition}
  S\le S_\star \simeq 0.618,
\end{equation}
whereas around the defect the nematic order reaches $S_0=0.9$.

We collect the three flux noises at a point, $\eta^\rho_i$, $\eta^\Q_{11i}$ and
$\eta^\Q_{12i}$, into a vector with six components $(a,i)$. Each is an angular integral of
the reversal noise, $\int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,u_{(ai)}(\theta)\,k$, with the
weights
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:u-vector}
  \bs u(\theta)=\bigl(\p,\ \hat Q_{11}\p,\ \hat Q_{12}\p\bigr).
\end{equation}
By \cref{eq:dean-noise-sum}, their covariance is $\M\Delta'$, where $\Delta'=\delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t')$ as in
\cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators}, with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gram-matrix}
  \M=\frac{4\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,\bs u\,\bs u^\T .
\end{equation}
This $6\times6$ matrix holds the density correlator \cref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation} and
the first two correlators of \cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators}. Since the weight $\varrho_t$ is
non-negative, $\M$ is a Gram matrix, and
$\bs v^\T\M\bs v=(4\zeta/\xi_0\rho_0)\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,(\bs v\tcdot\bs u)^2\ge0$
for every $\bs v$. The covariance of $\f^\Q$ is a Gram matrix in the same way. Every
genuine distribution, and in particular the von Mises distribution with the exact
moments \cref{eq:exact-moments}, therefore gives a valid covariance.

There is no margin, however. Since $\hat\Q\cdot\p=\p$ for every $\theta$, the fluxes
obey the two exact relations $\eta^Q_{\alpha\beta\beta}=\eta^\rho_\alpha$, so $\M$ has
two zero eigenvalues whatever the distribution. Equivalently, the six components of
$\bs u$ span only the four functions $\cos\theta$, $\sin\theta$, $\cos3\theta$ and
$\sin3\theta$. A covariance built from approximate moments can move these two
eigenvalues in either direction, and a negative eigenvalue makes it invalid.

This is what happens with the lowest-order closure. The products $u_{(ai)}u_{(bj)}$
contain only the harmonics $n=0,2,4,6$, so $\M$ depends on the distribution only
through $\rho$ and the complex harmonics
$z_n=\rho^{-1}\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\,\ee^{\ii n\theta}$ with $n=2,4,6$. The closure
\cref{eq:leading-closure} keeps $z_2=S\ee^{2\ii\theta_0}$ ($\theta_0$ is the angle of the
director), which $\Q$ fixes, and sets
$z_4=0$ through $\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho=\rho\J$ and $z_6=0$ through
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho=\tfrac12\bT$. Its $\M$ is valid only if some
non-negative distribution has these harmonics, which is a truncated trigonometric moment
problem. Once the phase of $z_2$ is rotated away, such a distribution exists if and only
if the Toeplitz matrix of the harmonics,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:toeplitz}
  \mathcal T=\begin{pmatrix}1&S&0&0\\S&1&S&0\\0&S&1&S\\0&0&S&1\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
is positive semi-definite \cite{grenander1958toeplitz}. Its eigenvalues are
$1+2S\cos(k\pi/5)$ with $k=1,\dots,4$, and the smallest, $1-2S\cos(\pi/5)$, turns
negative above
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:golden-bound}
  S_\star=\frac1{2\cos(\pi/5)}=\frac{\sqrt5-1}2\simeq0.618 .
\end{equation}
Above $S_\star$, no non-negative distribution has the moments of the closure, and its
$\M$ acquires a negative eigenvalue. We checked numerically that the smallest
eigenvalue of $\M$ changes sign exactly at $S_\star$, so the bound is sharp.

The defect steady state lies far above this bound. In the steady state of
\cref{app:comoving}, $S$ rises from $0.10$ next to the core to $0.90$ far from it,
and it exceeds $S_\star$ everywhere except within about $1.5~\micron$ of the core. Keeping
the $\U$ term of \cref{eq:exact-moments}, with $g_2$ at its small-$S$ value $1/2$, raises
the bound only to $S\le0.775$. The analytic calculation of \cref{sec:defect-fluct} is not
affected, because at $\delta\Q=0$ it needs only the density correlator
\cref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation}, which is exact. The coupled equations need the full
covariance of $(\bEta_\rho,\bEta^\Q,\f^\Q)$, so the solver builds it from the exact moments
\cref{eq:exact-moments} of the von Mises distribution, which give a Gram matrix at every
$S$, solving \cref{eq:kappa-S} once per node.

In the continuum the spectrum of the density fluctuations is flat, because the factor $k^2$
of the noise power cancels the factor $k^2$ of the relaxation rate (\cref{sec:defect-fluct}).
Every wavevector below the cutoff contributes equally, so the variance is set by the shortest
wavelengths that are kept, and it depends on how the derivatives in
\cref{eq:linearized-rho} are discretized. On a grid each factor $k^2$ becomes the symbol of a
difference operator, the factor by which the operator multiplies a plane wave
\cite{leveque2007finite}, and the spectrum stays flat only if the noise and the drift have
equal symbols.

The one-dimensional Laplacian shows what can go wrong (\cref{fig:staggered-grid}). On nodes
spaced by $\Delta x$, the drift uses the compact second difference
\begin{equation}
  (Lu)_n=\frac{u_{n+1}-2u_n+u_{n-1}}{\Delta x^2}.
\end{equation}
A first derivative can be taken at a node, with the central difference
$(Du)_n=(u_{n+1}-u_{n-1})/2\Delta x$. Applying $D$ twice, however, does not give $L$ (\cref{fig:staggered-grid}(a)):
\begin{equation}
  (D^2u)_n=\frac{u_{n+2}-2u_n+u_{n-2}}{4\Delta x^2}\neq(Lu)_n .
\end{equation}

The forward difference $G$ takes the first derivative between two nodes instead, on the face
$n+1/2$, $(Gu)_{n+1/2}=(u_{n+1}-u_n)/\Delta x$. Its negative transpose,
$(-G^\T F)_n=(F_{n+1/2}-F_{n-1/2})/\Delta x$, takes a face flux $F$ back to the nodes as a
divergence (\cref{fig:staggered-grid}(b)), so that whatever leaves one node enters its
neighbor. Applying $G$ and then $-G^\T$ gives the compact Laplacian exactly, $L=-G^\T G$. This is the staggered grid of computational fluid dynamics, which avoids the
decoupling of neighboring nodes that $D^2$ produces \cite{ferziger2020computational}.

The density noise in \cref{eq:ou-model} is the divergence of the flux noise $\bEta_\rho$, so
its covariance contains a first difference on each side. If this divergence is taken with
$D$, the noise carries $|\hat D|^2$ where the drift carries $\hat L$. For a plane wave
$u_n=\ee^{\ii kn\Delta x}$ the two symbols are \cite{leveque2007finite}
\begin{align}
  \hat L(k)&=-\frac{4}{\Delta x^2}\sin^2\frac{k\Delta x}{2},
  \nonumber\\
  \label{eq:fd-symbols}
  |\hat D(k)|^2&=\frac{\sin^2k\Delta x}{\Delta x^2}=-\hat L(k)\cos^2\frac{k\Delta x}{2},
\end{align}
so the noise power of $D$ falls short of the drift by the factor $\cos^2(k\Delta x/2)$
at short wavelengths. We fix this by taking the divergence with $G$ instead, as is done in fluctuating hydrodynamics
\cite{donev2010accuracy,balboa2012staggered,kim2017stochastic}, so that the noise and the
drift share one operator and the spectrum is exactly flat.

In two dimensions, with $G_x$ and $G_y$ taking the nodes to the faces between horizontal and
vertical neighbors, respectively, the drift of the density is
$(B/\xi_0)L$ with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fd-identity}
  L=-G_x^\T G_x-G_y^\T G_y .
\end{equation}
Without the spatial correlation of \cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff}, the noise flux $\bEta_\rho$
lives on the faces, independent from face to face, with the
local covariance $M^\rho_\ab=(2\zeta/\xi_0\rho_0)(\delta_\ab+Q^\ss_\ab)$ of \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss}
sampled there, so the diagonal part of the density noise is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fd-noise}
  \mathbf N_{\rho\rho}=\frac1{\Delta x^2}\sum_{i=x,y}G_i^\T\,\mathrm{diag}(M^\rho_{ii})\,G_i ,
\end{equation}
where $1/\Delta x^2$ is the grid form of $\delta(\r-\r')$. For an isotropic noise the noise
matrix is then a multiple of the drift matrix, the spectrum is flat on the grid, and every
node has the same variance. 
% To check that the drift
% and the noise share one operator, the solver builds both sides
% of \cref{eq:fd-identity} in exact arithmetic and stops if they differ.

The other divergence terms of \cref{eq:linearized} follow the same construction. A drift term
$\nabla\tcdot(c\nabla\,\cdot\,)$ with a scalar coefficient $c$ becomes
$-\sum_iG_i^\T\,\mathrm{diag}(c)\,G_i$ with $c$ on the faces. In the equation for $\delta
Q_\ab$, the transport term $(B/\xi_0)\nabla\tcdot(Q^\ss_\ab\nabla\delta\rho)$ has this
form, with $c=(B/\xi_0)Q^\ss_\ab$. The advection
$\nabla\tcdot(\delta\Q\,\bs w_\ss)$ is $-G^\T$ applied to the face flux $\bs w_\ss\,\delta\Q$,
with $\delta\Q$ averaged onto the faces. The noise of $\delta\Q$ and its correlation with
$\bEta_\rho$ are assembled in the same way. Only the mixed noise terms,
$M^\rho_{xy}=M^\rho_{yx}\propto Q^\ss_{xy}$, keep the central difference at the nodes. Their symbol is
proportional to $\sin(k_x\Delta x)\sin(k_y\Delta x)$, which is odd in $k_x$ and averages to
zero, as in the continuum, where uniform nematic order leaves the variance unchanged
(\cref{app:variance-uniform}). Derivatives taken on different faces would instead add a
spurious pedestal proportional to $Q^\ss_{xy}$.

Each diagonal term of \cref{eq:fd-noise} is a congruence, so it is positive semi-definite
wherever the local covariance is. With the exact moments of \cref{app:realizability} the
local covariance is valid at every node, and the solver checks it there with a
$6\times6$ eigensolve.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The noise correlation length and its calibration}{The noise correlation length and its calibration}}
\label{app:lyapunov-cutoff}
% dean.tex 1200-1275 (sec-ln-vs-h, sec-cutoff-numerics), 2048-2089 (sec-noise-cutoff).
% Figure (l_eff/l_n against dx/l_n, 2026-10-06): plotgen/cutoff-calibration (run.sh).

If we used a white spatioal noise in \cref{eq:ou-model}, the discretised grid would set the cutoff: the solver would return the
pedestal $\zeta/(B\rho_0\Delta x^2)$, which is \cref{eq:pedestal} with
$\elln=\Delta x/\sqrt{2\pi}$. To impose the cutoff \cref{eq:cutoff} instead, we use a noise correlated over the
length $\elln$.

We construct it from the white noise $\bxi$ of \cref{eq:ou-model} with the operator
$W=\exp(\ell_W^2\nabla^2/4)$. Since $\nabla^2$ multiplies a plane wave by $-k^2$, the symbol of
$W$ is the Gaussian $\tilde W(\k)=\ee^{-k^2\ell_W^2/4}$ of \cref{app:variance-cutoff}, so $W$
correlates the noise over the length $\ell_W$. The noise $W\bxi$ is still white in time, with
the covariance
\begin{equation*}
  (W\mathbf NW)(\r,\r')=\int\!\d^2\r_1\d^2\r_2\,W(\r,\r_1)\mathbf N(\r_1,\r_2)W(\r_2,\r'),
\end{equation*}
with $W(\r,\r_1)$ the kernel of $W$ and $\mathbf N(\r_1,\r_2)$ the covariance of
\cref{eq:ou-model}. For any field $\bs v$ the quadratic form of $W\mathbf NW$ equals that of
$\mathbf N$ evaluated on $W\bs v$, so the correlated covariance is positive semi-definite
whenever $\mathbf N$ is. Moreover, $W$ commutes with the drift $(B/\xi_0)\nabla^2$ of the
density, so at $\delta\Q=0$ the correlated noise gives the same density covariance as the
density smoothed with $W$, which is what \cref{app:variance-cutoff} computes.

\todo{check this part}
On the grid we replace $\nabla^2$ in $W$ by the discrete Dirichlet Laplacian
$\nabla^2_{\Delta x}$, and the correlation then comes out too short. The discrete Laplacian
measures the curvature of a wave from three neighboring nodes, so it underestimates the
curvature of waves that are only a few nodes long. Its symbol is $-k^2$ with each $k_i^2$ replaced by
$(4/\Delta x^2)\sin^2(k_i\Delta x/2)$. For the shortest wave of the grid, which alternates
from node to node, this gives $4/\Delta x^2$ instead of $\pi^2/\Delta x^2$. The grid version of $W$
therefore damps the shortest waves too little, and since these set the variance, the pedestal
comes out too high. The error depends on the ratio $\Delta x/\elln$, and it
disappears on fine grids, where all the waves that $W$ lets through are long compared with
$\Delta x$. 

A finer grid is out of reach: the covariance is a dense matrix with
$(3N_\mathrm{int}^2)^2$ entries, so its memory grows as $N_\mathrm{int}^4$, and at
$N_\mathrm{int}=200$ it already takes $115$~GB. We therefore calibrate the correlation
length instead.

Quantitatively, the symbol of $W$ on the grid is
\begin{equation*}
  \exp\Bigl[-\frac{\ell_W^2}{\Delta x^2}\Bigl(\sin^2\frac{k_x\Delta x}2
  +\sin^2\frac{k_y\Delta x}2\Bigr)\Bigr],
\end{equation*}
which decays more slowly than $\ee^{-k^2\ell_W^2/4}$, since
$\sin^2(k\Delta x/2)<(k\Delta x/2)^2$. 
With $\ell_W=\elln$ the pedestal on the grid therefore exceeds \cref{eq:pedestal} by the factor
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:lattice-overshoot}
  \chi=2\pi\frac{\elln^2}{\Delta x^2}\,\mathcal I\Bigl(\frac{\elln}{\Delta x}\Bigr)^2
  \simeq1+\frac{\Delta x^2}{4\elln^2},
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal I(s)=\ee^{-s^2}I_0(s^2)$ and $I_0$ the modified Bessel function. On our
grid, $\Delta x/\elln=0.40$, the pedestal would come out $4\%$ too high.

The solver removes this factor by building $W$ from a length $\ell_\mathrm{eff}$
instead of $\elln$, chosen so that the pedestal on the grid equals \cref{eq:pedestal} at
the physical $\elln$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ell-eff}
  \mathcal I(\ell_\mathrm{eff}/\Delta x)=\frac{\Delta x}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\elln}.
\end{equation}
\Cref{fig:cutoff-calibration} shows the ratio $\ell_\mathrm{eff}/\elln$ that solves
\cref{eq:ell-eff} against $\Delta x/\elln$. On our grid $\ell_\mathrm{eff}$ lies $2\%$ above
$\elln$.


The calibration fixes the isotropic pedestal only. The anisotropic term of
\cref{eq:total-gauss} keeps a lattice correction of its own, so the peak modulation $\nu_0$
of the solver approaches its continuum value only as the grid is refined.

\subsection{Boundary conditions}
\label{app:lyapunov-walls}
\prompt{I want this subsection to stress that we need three boundary conditions. Sometimes it is not obvious that the noise also needs a boundary condition. The size of this subsection may increase about 50 percent.}
The boundary needs conditions on the steady state, on the fluctuating fields and on the
noise flux through it. The last two must agree, because both decide whether a cell may cross
the boundary. A reservoir pins the fluctuations and lets the noise flux through, like a
window cut from a larger colony. A sealed boundary blocks both, like a container. We use the
reservoir. Its boundary nodes are removed, so $\delta\rho=\delta\Q=0$ there, the noise flux
through the faces next to the boundary is kept, and $W$ is built on the Dirichlet Laplacian,
so the correlated noise vanishes on the boundary nodes too. The density condition is the one
of the steady state in \cref{app:comoving}. Only the nematic condition differs: the steady
state leaves $\Q_\ss$ free at the boundary, $\nh\tcdot\nabla\Q_\ss=0$, whereas the
fluctuations are anchored there, $\delta\Q=0$.

The difference has two sources. The first is the fluctuating order $\delta\Q$, which
\cref{eq:total-gauss} drops. The density that $\delta\Q$ drives adds about $10^{-4}$ of the
pedestal at the maximum of the variance, and its correlation with the density at fixed order
removes about as much (computed on the grid $N_\mathrm{int}=180$), so this source is an order
of magnitude below the difference. The second, and main, source is the lattice correction of
the anisotropic term (\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff}). It shrinks as the grid is refined: from
$N_\mathrm{int}=180$ to $200$, the peak of the solver rises from $2.39\%$ to $2.42\%$,
toward the $2.56\%$ of \cref{eq:total-gauss} on $\Q_\ss$ \prompt{I do not remember that we say this second source in the main text. Add it in a suitable place.}. 

The solver also gives the fluctuations of the active force $\fa=-\zeta\bs f$,
$f_\alpha=\partial_\beta Q_\ab$, which $\delta\Q$ carries as
$\delta f_\alpha=\partial_\beta\delta Q_\ab$ and which the approximation $\delta\Q=0$ of
\cref{sec:defect-fluct} drops. This force is not small. Its
fluctuations exceed the mean force everywhere along the axis of the defect, a few times near
the core and increasingly away from it, where the mean force decays and the fluctuations
level off at a uniform value (\cref{fig:force-axis}). Around the core the force fluctuates
mostly along the axis of the defect, the direction of the mean active force
(\cref{fig:force-axis,fig:force-map}). The traction measured in Ref.~\cite{han2025local}
shows the same pattern: its fluctuations lie far above the mean around defects, and they
stay strong in aligned regions, where the mean traction nearly vanishes. The traction also
contains the pressure gradient, however, so this resemblance is qualitative.

The density variance nevertheless changes little, as shown above. On the scale of the
cutoff the nematic order relaxes at most at the rate $4K'/(\xir\elln^2)=20~\minute^{-1}$,
nearly four orders of magnitude below the rate $1/\tau_c\simeq10^5~\minute^{-1}$ at which the
density relaxes there (\cref{eq:tau-c}), so the density responds to the force of $\delta\Q$ as
to a static force (\cref{sec:defect-fluct}).
