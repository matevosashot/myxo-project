\title{Fluctuating active nematics account for layer formation in \textit{Myxococcus xanthus} colonies}

\author{Ashot Matevosyan}
\author{Ricard Alert}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
In colonies of the bacterium \textit{Myxococcus xanthus}, cells leave the monolayer and
form new layers, the first step towards the fruiting bodies that starving colonies build.
Experiments show that these layers form preferentially at $+1/2$ topological defects of
the cell orientation. However, the mean compression at a defect falls three to four
orders of magnitude short of the pressure needed to extrude a cell, while the traction
measured around defects has a standard deviation about ten times its mean. We therefore propose that
layers form during rare pressure fluctuations, and we compute the statistics of these
fluctuations. Treating layer formation as nucleation gives a critical pressure, so the
layering probability is the probability that the pressure exceeds it. This probability
depends exponentially on the noise, but an active nematic has no
fluctuation-dissipation relation to fix that noise. We derive it instead by
coarse-graining self-propelled rods that reverse their direction of motion, and we keep
the noise through a Dean equation. As a result, the reversals set the active stress and
the noise amplitude together. Around a $+1/2$ defect, the pressure variance is then
modulated by two to three percent over a few micrometers. Because the critical pressure
lies twenty-one standard deviations into the tail, this small modulation becomes an
enhancement of the layering probability by a factor of about $10^2$. The enhancement
peaks off the defect core and, once the stiffness of the monolayer is fitted, matches
the measured one.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\textit{Myxococcus xanthus} is a soil bacterium that lives and hunts in groups
\cite{munoz2016}. While nutrients last, the cells glide together across the substrate as
a swarm, a thin film that feeds on prey bacteria (\cref{fig:myxo_life}(A)). When the
nutrients run out, the swarm instead aggregates into mounds, and these grow into
fruiting bodies of $10^5$ to $10^6$ cells, in which some of the cells become spores
(\cref{fig:myxo_life}(B)). A fruiting body is built by stacking layers of cells on top of
one another, so its first step is the formation of a second layer on top of the
monolayer (red arrow in \cref{fig:myxo_life}). We ask how this first new layer forms.

\textbf{(A)} Vegetative cycle. With nutrients, the cells glide together as a swarm, a thin film
on the substrate, and kill and lyse prey bacteria (gray). \textbf{(B)} Developmental cycle. Upon
starvation, the swarm aggregates into mounds, which grow into upright fruiting bodies
filled with spores (brown). The spores germinate when nutrients return. The red arrow
marks the transition this paper addresses: the flat swarm forms its first multilayered
structures.}
\label{fig:myxo_life}

The cells are rods about $6~\micron$ long and $0.5~\micron$ wide that glide along their
long axis and reverse their direction every few minutes
\cite{wu2009periodic,copenhagen2021}. Although each cell moves in one direction at a
time, it keeps reversing, and neighboring rods interact in the same way whichever end
leads. On long time scales the monolayer is therefore an active nematic
\cite{marchetti2013hydrodynamics,doostmohammadi2018active}, and since the substrate takes
up the momentum of the cells through friction, it belongs to dry active matter, in which
momentum is not conserved \cite{marchetti2013hydrodynamics}. The local average axis of the cells, the
director, is undefined at isolated points called topological defects. Because the
director has no head or tail, it can rotate by any integer multiple of $\pi$ along a loop
around a defect, so nematic defects carry integer or half-integer charge
\cite{degennes1993physics,mermin1979topological}. The elastic energy of a defect grows as
the square of its charge, so a $+1$ defect lowers its energy by splitting into two $+1/2$
defects \cite{degennes1993physics,duclos2017topological}. Half-integer defects therefore
dominate in two-dimensional nematics
\cite{doostmohammadi2018active,giomi2013defect}. Integer defects do appear
in living systems, for example in myoblasts confined to discs \cite{guillamat2022integer}
and in the actin fibres of regenerating \textit{Hydra} \cite{maroudassacks2021topological},
but \textit{M.\ xanthus} monolayers show half-integer defects \cite{copenhagen2021}: the
comet-shaped $+1/2$ defect and the three-armed $-1/2$ defect (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a,b)).
In other living active nematics, too, defects are where cells leave the plane:
epithelial cells are extruded at $+1/2$ defects \cite{saw2017topological}, neural
progenitor cells accumulate there into three-dimensional mounds
\cite{kawaguchi2017topological}, and in colonies of \textit{Pseudomonas aeruginosa},
cells rotate upright and become trapped where two $+1/2$ defects collide
\cite{meacock2021bacteria}. In chaining \textit{Bacillus subtilis} biofilms, cells
instead lift off near $-1/2$ defects, where simulations show stress accumulating
\cite{yaman2019emergence}.

    (red). The circle is centred on the point of maximum probability (blue) or on

  }

Copenhagen \textit{et al.} imaged \textit{M.\ xanthus} monolayers on a nutrient-rich
substrate, where no fruiting bodies form, and still saw new layers and holes appear
throughout the colony \cite{copenhagen2021}. Unlike in growing colonies
\cite{grant2014}, these layers need no critical colony size. They form, however, about
$200$ times more often near $+1/2$ defects than far from them (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(c),
red), while holes open about $80$ times more often near $-1/2$ defects. Here we address
the layers at $+1/2$ defects.
Copenhagen \textit{et al.} explained this preference with an active nematic model in
which the friction with the substrate is anisotropic \cite{copenhagen2021}. Around a $+1/2$
defect, the cells in front of the core are aligned perpendicular to the flow and so feel a
stronger friction than those behind it, which makes the inflow towards the core stronger
than the outflow. The model assumes that cells are readily extruded, so that the net inflow
becomes a new layer. The measured flows also converge at $+1/2$ defects, but a converging
flow is what layer formation itself requires, since the cells that leave the monolayer must
be replaced \cite{han2025local}. The measured flows therefore cannot tell whether the inflow
causes the layers or follows from them. Moreover, extrusion has an energy cost, and the mean
pressure at a defect is of the order of
the active stress, three to four orders of magnitude below the pressure needed to
extrude a cell.

The stress in these monolayers, however, fluctuates strongly. Han \textit{et al.} measured
the traction that the cells exert on the substrate around $\pm1/2$ defects and found that its standard
deviation is about ten times its mean \cite{han2025local}. The fluctuations stay strong in
aligned regions away from defects, where the mean traction nearly vanishes. They attributed these fluctuations
to local polar order, whose magnitude the cells tune through their reversal rate, and found
them stronger at $+1/2$ than at $-1/2$ defects. They proposed that the fluctuating polar
forces build up local pressure, which can trigger new layers, and noted that active nematic
theory, which explains the mean flows, must be extended to capture these fluctuations and
the stochasticity of layer formation. Here we provide such an extension. We propose that layers form
during rare pressure fluctuations that originate in the self-propulsion and reversals of
single cells, and we compute the statistics of these fluctuations around a $+1/2$
defect.

To connect layer formation to the pressure, we treat it as nucleation. Above a critical
pressure in the monolayer, it becomes energetically favorable to grow a new layer from a
single extruded cell. This critical pressure is set by adhesion, surface tension and cell
size, so it does not depend on position. A defect can therefore favor layers only through
the statistics of the pressure around it, and the layering probability is the probability
that the local pressure exceeds the critical one.


This probability is set by the fluctuations of the pressure, and hence by the noise
that drives them. In equilibrium, the fluctuation-dissipation theorem fixes that noise by
tying it to the dissipative terms in the equations of motion \citeTODO{}. An active nematic
is out of equilibrium and has no such relation, so phenomenological theories add the noise
to the continuum equations by hand, with a free amplitude and a chosen tensor structure
\cite{ramaswamy2003active}. We therefore derive the
noise from the dynamics of the cells. 

Kinetic theories derive continuum equations of
active matter from particle models, including self-propelled rods that reverse, but
they average the noise out on the way
\cite{bertin2006boltzmann,baskaran2008hydrodynamics,grossmann2016mesoscale,peshkov2012nonlinear}.
% We employ a similar approach to Dean's equation.
The Dean--Kawasaki
equation keeps it instead \cite{dean1996,kawasaki1994stochastic,illien2025dean}. It has been extended to run-and-tumble and
active Brownian particles \cite{tailleur2008statistical,solon2015active} and, for the
noise of the free motion, to apolar active nematics whose particles step back and forth
at random \cite{bertin2013}. We extend it to interacting rods that reverse at a finite
rate $\frev$, as \textit{M.\ xanthus} does (\cref{fig:model-micro}), and then integrate out
their orientation.

  rod (blue) in the nematic field around a $+1/2$ defect, whose core is the red dot;

  $\bs v_i=v_0\p(\theta_i)k_i$, reversing at rate $\frev$, and feels the force
  $\F_i$ and the torque $\Gamma_i$ of its neighbors; $\xi_0\dot\r_i$ is the
  friction.}
  \label{fig:model-micro}

The result is a pair of stochastic equations for the density and the nematic order
parameter. The reversals drive an active stress, which grows with the distance a cell
glides between reversals, and the same coefficient sets the amplitude of the density
noise. Neither can therefore be tuned without the other. The coarse-graining also fixes
how this noise depends on the local nematic order.

Solved around a $+1/2$ defect, these equations give a density variance, and hence a
pressure variance. The defect changes this variance only slightly, but since layers form
in the far tail of the pressure distribution, this small change is enough to make layers
form much more often near the defect. The variance is largest just
off the core, at the head of the defect, on the side where the director bends around it (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)). 
In experiments, it is also observed that more often layering starts at the head of the defect, \cref{fig:defects-layering}(b) as an example. Besides the radial profile of
\cref{fig:defects-layering}(c), the layering probability therefore has an angular profile around the
defect, but there is no quantitative measurement.
Fluctuations of
microscopic origin thus suffice to explain where layers form, although the calculation
does not exclude other mechanisms.

This paper is organized as follows. The next section describes layer formation as nucleation over a fluctuating
barrier and reduces it to the statistics of the pressure. 
\Cref{sec:model} then derives
the stochastic equations from the microscopic model, and \cref{sec:defect} solves them
at a $+1/2$ defect, first for the steady state and then for the fluctuations. With the
pressure variance in hand, \cref{sec:nucleation-2} computes the layering probability and
compares it with the measurements of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}. 
Finally,
\cref{sec:discussion} collects what is fitted, what is approximate, and what would test
the mechanism.
We relegated detailed derivations of our results to Appendices making
our conclusions self-contained.

\section{Layer formation as a nucleation event}
\label{sec:nucleation-1}
% Mechanism and motivation. The calculation resumes in \cref{sec:nucleation-2} once
% \cref{sec:model,sec:defect} supply the pressure variance. Assembled 2026-09-25 from
% Ashot's draft, the energetics and threshold parts of the former sec:nucleation-old

New layers are formed when it is energetically favorable. We model this from a nucleation theory perspective. First, we calculate the energy cost of a new layer.

We assume that cells adhere both to the substrate and
to other cells, and that water completely wets the colony. Let $\Wc$ and $\Ws$ be the
cell-cell and cell-substrate adhesion energies per unit area, and $\gammaw$ the surface
tension of water. Suppose a patch of cells of area $A$ and perimeter $\ell$ forms a new
layer (\cref{fig:nucleation}(a)). The cells of the patch need to (\textit{i}) detach from the
substrate, (\textit{ii}) detach from their neighbors, and (\textit{iii}) work against the surface tension of
water. This costs $\Ws A + \Wc\ell h + \gammaw\ell h$, where $h$ is the height of the
monolayer. At the same time, the bottom layer relaxes to fill the space the patch left.
The energy gain comes from (\textit{iv}) the adhesion of the patch to the cells beneath it and (\textit{v})
the work done by the pressure, $\Wc A + pA$. Here $p$ is the two-dimensional pressure of
the monolayer, a force per unit length. \Cref{app:extrusion} derives $pA$ from the
elastic energy the bottom layer releases.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth,page=3]{figures/handmade/main_v2.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{(a)} Formation of a new layer. Top: the monolayer of cells (yellow) on
  the substrate (gray), wetted by water (blue). Middle: a patch of area $A$ and
  perimeter $\ell$ detaches and forms a second layer, which costs
  $\Ws A+\Wc\ell h+\gammaw\ell h$ (green); $h$ is the height of the
  monolayer. Bottom: the bottom layer relaxes into the space the patch left, and
  adhesion and pressure return $\Wc A+pA$ (red), \cref{eq:dE-of-A}.
    \textbf{(b)} \todo{change usints to $\kPa\tcdot\micron$} Energy cost of the new layer, \cref{eq:dE-of-A}, in units of
    $10^5~\Pa\tcdot\micron^3$, against the patch area $A$ in $\micron^2$.
    The beam of curves is for different pressures $p$: the black curve is at the
    critical pressure $p=\pcrit$, and the orange and blue curves lie above and below it,
    so layers form only on the orange curves.
  }
  \label{fig:nucleation}
\end{figure*}

The cell-cell adhesion $\Wc$ enters twice with opposite signs: as a cost on
the side faces and as a gain on the bottom face. For a circular patch, $\ell=\sqrt{4\pi A}$, and the energy cost of the new layer is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dE-of-A}
    \Delta E(A) = (\Ws-\Wc-p)\,A + (\Wc+\gammaw)\,h\sqrt{4\pi A}.
\end{equation}
which is a function of the patch area $A$ and monolayer pressure $p$.

The perimeter term is a cost and grows as $\sqrt A$. The area term grows as $A$ and is a
gain once $p>\Ws-\Wc$. At such pressures $\Delta E(A)$ rises at small $A$, reaches a
maximum and then falls. This is the barrier of classical nucleation theory (\cref{fig:nucleation}(b)). Its maximum
is the critical nucleus, of area $A^*=\pi h^2(\Wc+\gammaw)^2/(p+\Wc-\Ws)^2$. A larger patch
lowers its energy by growing, a smaller one by shrinking.

In classical nucleation theory, thermal fluctuations or impurities in the system move $A$ along a fixed curve $\Delta E(A)$.
In our system, because of active and passive flctuations, the pressure fluctuates. The barier moves with it together with the critical nucleus area $A^*$.

Thermal energy is
negligible against the adhesion and surface energies of even one cell. Cells glide in the
plane of the substrate, and nothing lifts a group of them off it together. A new layer must therefore start from a single cell, as already observed in the experiments. If the maximum of $\Delta E(A)$ lies below one cell area($A^*<\Acell$), growth from
that cell is downhill of the energy. Single-cell perturbations, such as substrate roughness or a division event, then complete the extrusion. The discreteness of
the system thus sets the nucleation condition,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:nucleation-condition}
    \Delta E'(A)\big|_{A=\Acell} = 0,
\end{equation}
or $A^*=\Acell$. Solved for the pressure, it gives the critical pressure
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pcrit}
    \pcrit = (\Ws-\Wc) + \sqrt{\frac{\pi h^2}{\Acell}}\,(\Wc+\gammaw).
\end{equation}
So layers are formed only if the pressure in the monolayer exceeds $\pcrit$. Alternaticvely, the probability of layer formation at a point $\r$ is 
(\cref{fig:nucleation}(b))
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:p-layer-def}
 \calP(\r) \propto \mathbb P\bigl(p(\r)>\pcrit\bigr).
\end{equation}
With the parameter values of \cref{app:parameters-extrusion}, $\pcrit=35~\kPa\tcdot\micron$. The surface tension of water dominates it. With $\gammaw=65~\kPa\tcdot\micron$, the measured surface tension of the growth medium~\cite{bourque2025active}, the second term of \cref{eq:pcrit} contributes $33~\kPa\tcdot\micron$. The mean pressure in the monolayer is far below $\pcrit$, so layering is predominantly from the large and rare fluctuations.










\Cref{eq:pcrit} contains the cell size and the adhesion and surface energies. It
contains no property of the fluctuations and does not depend on position. A defect can
therefore favor layers only by changing the distribution of the pressure and its fluctuations around it, which
makes the nucleation heterogeneous. 

In the following sections we set up a microscopic model of the monolayer and calculate the pressure fluctuations around a defect. Then, in \cref{sec:nucleation-2}, when all the components are in place, we compute the spatial profile of the layering probability \eqref{eq:p-layer-def}.

\subsection{The microscopic model}
\label{sec:model-micro}
% dean.tex 109-173 ("Microscopic equations of motion").
We start from Langevin equations for $N$ overdamped, self-propelled rods
\cite{cugliandolo2015,bertin2013}, each with position $\r_i$ and orientation $\theta_i$ (\cref{fig:model-micro}):
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:model}
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:langevin-position}
    \frac{\d\r_i}{\d t} &= \frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F_i + v_0\,\p(\theta_i) k_i,
    \\
    \label{eq:langevin-angle}
    \frac{\d\theta_i}{\d t} &= \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma_i + \lambda_i,
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
with $\p(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ the unit vector along the rod axis. Here $\xi_0/\rho_0$ and $\xir/\rho_0$ are
translational and rotational friction coefficients ($\rho_0$ a reference density
carried along for later convenience), and $\lambda_i$ is Gaussian white
noise, $\la\lambda_i(t)\lambda_j(t')\ra=2\Lambda\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t')$, with $\Lambda$
the rotational diffusion coefficient. The rods
interact through a pairwise potential $V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')$, so that
$V_\mathrm{tot}=\tfrac12\sum_{i,j=1}^N V(\r_i-\r_j,\theta_i,\theta_j)$. The force and
torque on rod $i$ are the values at $(\r_i,\theta_i)$ of the fields
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:force-torque}
    \F(\r,\theta) &= -\pd{}{\r}\sum_{j=1}^N V(\r-\r_j,\theta,\theta_j),
    \nonumber\\
    \Gamma(\r,\theta) &= -\pd{}{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^N V(\r-\r_j,\theta,\theta_j),
\end{align}
The sums run over all $N$ rods, including $j=i$: a pair
potential between identical rods satisfies
$V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')=V(\r'-\r,\theta',\theta)$, and at $\r=\r'$ rotational
invariance leaves it a function of $\theta-\theta'$ alone. Both derivatives in
\cref{eq:force-torque} therefore vanish on the $j=i$ term.

The second term of \cref{eq:langevin-position} is self-propulsion at speed $v_0$ along
the rod axis. Here $k_i$ is a dichotomous noise: $k_i=\pm1$ with
equal probability, switching at rate $\frev$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:k-dichotomous}
    \la k_i(t)k_j(t')\ra = \delta_{ij}\,\ee^{-2\frev|t-t'|}.
\end{equation}
This models the reversals of \textit{Myxococcus xanthus} gliding motility \cite{blackhart1985frizzy,guzzo2018gated}. On timescales long compared with $\frev^{-1}$ we approximate $k_i$ by Gaussian white noise,
$\la k_i(t)k_j(t')\ra \approx \frev^{-1}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t')$. The coarse-graining of
\cref{sec:model-coarse} uses It\^o calculus and therefore requires this approximation.
Refs.~\cite{bressloff2024global,spinney2025dean} derive
density equations for particles that switch between discrete states, as our rods do at
each reversal, without taking this limit.


Self-propulsion is a multiplicative noise. The noise is $k_i$, and the amplitude it
multiplies, $v_0\p(\theta_i)$, is set by the orientation of the rod. Coarse-graining a
multiplicative noise with It\^o calculus leaves a deterministic term alongside the
fluctuation, and both carry the same amplitude $v_0^2/\frev$
(\cref{app:dean-approach}). The anisotropic part of $\p\p$ becomes the active force
density of \cref{eq:model-rho}; the isotropic part is a diffusion, negligible against $B$. The fluctuation becomes the
density noise of the same equation.

\subsection{Coarse-graining that preserves fluctuations}
\label{sec:model-coarse}
% dean.tex 174-359 ("Dean's approach").
We take Dean's approach. Dean's equation \cite{dean1996} is an \emph{exact} field-theoretic Langevin equation for
the density of interacting Langevin processes. Our model \eqref{eq:model} adds an
orientation, a self-propulsion and a reversal noise. The same derivation then gives an
exact equation for the joint density over the state space $(\r,\theta)$; the only
approximation is the Gaussian white-noise limit taken in \cref{sec:model-micro}.

Write the single-particle density $\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta) =
\delta(\r-\r_i(t))\,\delta(\theta-\theta_i(t))$, and let
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:joint-density-definition}
  \varrho_t(\r,\theta)=\rho_0^{-1}\sum_i\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta)
\end{align}
be the joint density
over $(\r,\theta)$, made dimensionless by $\rho_0$. The interaction is
pairwise, so the force and torque fields \cref{eq:force-torque} are gradients of a single
functional of $\varrho_t$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pair-free-energy}
    \calF[\varrho] = \frac{\rho_0^2}2\!\int\!\d\r\d\r'\,\d\theta\d\theta'\,
    V(\r\!-\!\r'\!,\theta,\theta')\,\varrho(\r,\theta)\,\varrho(\r'\!,\theta'),
\end{equation}
namely $\F=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\r\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$ and
$\Gamma=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\theta\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$. Applying It\^o's lemma
to $\varrho^{(i)}_t$ and summing over $i$ then gives a closed equation for $\varrho_t$
alone (\cref{app:dean-approach}),
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:final-langevin}
    \begin{aligned}
    \partial_t\varrho_t(\r,\theta) = {}&
    \frac1{\xi_0}\pd{}{\r}\tcdot\left(\varrho_t\pd{}{\r}\frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho}\right)
    + \frac1{\xir}\pd{}{\theta}\!\left(\varrho_t\pd{}{\theta}\frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho}\right)
    \\
    &+ \frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0}\,p_\alpha(\theta)p_\beta(\theta)\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
    + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{\theta^2}
    \\
    &- \pd{}{\r}\tcdot\left(\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p(\theta)\,k(\r,\theta,t)\right)
    \\
    &- \pd{}{\theta}\!\left(\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda(\r,\theta,t)\right).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The last line of \cref{eq:final-langevin} carries two noise fields, $k(\r,\theta,t)$ and
$\lambda(\r,\theta,t)$. Both are Gaussian white noises in position, orientation and
time,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:noise-correlators}
    \la k(\r,\theta,t)\,k(\r',\theta',t')\ra &= \frac{v_0^2}{\frev\rho_0}\;\Delta,
    \nonumber\\
    \la \lambda(\r,\theta,t)\,\lambda(\r',\theta',t')\ra &= \frac{2\Lambda}{\rho_0}\;\Delta,
\end{align}
with $\Delta\equiv\delta(t-t')\delta(\r-\r')\delta(\theta-\theta')$, and they are
uncorrelated with each other. Their amplitudes are inherited from the reversal noise
$k_i$ and the rotational noise $\lambda_i$ of \cref{eq:model}.
\Cref{app:dean-approach} derives that relation, together with the multiplicative factor
$\sqrt{\varrho_t}$ that accompanies both fields.


\Cref{eq:final-langevin} is nonlinear even without interactions. The noise is
multiplicative: where there are no rods, $\varrho_t$ cannot fluctuate. The product
$\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,k$ is an It\^o product, because \cref{eq:final-langevin} follows from
It\^o's lemma: $\varrho_t$ is evaluated before each noise increment \cite{dean1996}. Each
realization of $k$ and $\lambda$ gives one random history of $\varrho_t$. The statistics
of the density, and of the pressure in \cref{sec:nucleation-1}, therefore follow from
\cref{eq:final-langevin} directly.

The joint density $\varrho_t$ is a sum of delta functions, the empirical density.
Strictly speaking, \cref{eq:final-langevin} is derived only for distributions, and
rigorous results for it are few
\cite{konarovskyi2019dean,konarovskyi2020dean,cornalba2023dean}. However, it becomes
well-posed if the noise is correlated in space \cite{fehrman2024well}, or if each delta
function is replaced by a kernel of finite width \cite{cornalba2019regularized}. Many
applications still use Dean's equation for smooth densities without a rigorous proof
\cite{kruger2017gaussian,demery2016conductivity,dean2014relaxation,demery2014generalized},
including the fluctuating theories of active particles
\cite{tailleur2008statistical,solon2015active,bertin2013}. From
\cref{sec:model-closure} on we treat $\varrho_t$ as smooth.


\subsection{Free energy, closure, and the stochastic field equations}
\label{sec:model-closure}
The joint density $\varrho_t$ carries far more information than we need. The rest of the
paper works with its lowest angular moments, the fluctuating density and nematic order
parameter,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:density-definition}
    \rho(\r,t) = \int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta),
    \quad
    \Q(\r,t) = \int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)\,\hat\Q(\theta),
\end{align}
with $\hat\Q(\theta)=2\p(\theta)\p(\theta)-\I$ the single-rod nematic tensor.%
\footnote{%
The conventional nematic order parameter is normalized by the local density,
$\Q=\rho^{-1}\!\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\hat\Q$, and is bounded by unity. We use the
unnormalized moment \cref{eq:density-definition} instead; the two coincide in the
incompressible limit $\rho\to1$ taken throughout (\cref{app:dean-normalisation}).}
In this subsection we reduce \cref{eq:final-langevin} to a closed pair of stochastic
equations for these two fields.

We do not know the interbacterial potential
${V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')}$ in any detail, so we do not attempt to evaluate
\cref{eq:pair-free-energy} from it. Instead we assume that the free energy depends on
$\varrho$ only through the two moments \cref{eq:density-definition},
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:free-energy-parametrisation}
    \calF[\varrho] = F[\rho,\Q] = \int\!\d\r\;\mathfrak f\bigl(\rho(\r),\Q(\r),\partial_\r\Q(\r)\bigr),
\end{align}
which is both a physical assumption and a choice of parametrization. It gives a chain
rule,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:chain-rule}
    \frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}
    = \frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho(\r)}
    + \frac{\delta F}{\delta\Q(\r)}\!:\!\hat\Q(\theta).
\end{align}
This truncation of the $\theta$ dependence to a single harmonic is what makes
\cref{eq:final-langevin} integrable over $\theta$ (\cref{app:dean-angle}).

For the free energy density $\mathfrak f$ we take the sum of an incompressibility penalty and a Landau-de Gennes
nematic term,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:free-energy-choice}
    \mathfrak f(\rho,\Q,\partial_\r\Q) = {}& \frac B2(\rho-1)^2 + \frac a2(\Q\!:\!\Q)
    + \frac b4(\Q\!:\!\Q)^2
    \nonumber\\
    &+ \frac K2(\partial_\r\Q)\!:\!(\partial_\r\Q).
\end{align}
The first term enforces near-incompressibility of the monolayer with a large bulk
modulus $B$; the rest is the standard nematic free energy, with $a<0$ favoring order and $K$ penalizing distortion. The single $K$ is the one-constant approximation of the Frank free energy, in which the splay and bend moduli of the two-dimensional nematic are taken equal \citeTODO{Frank Free energy}.

The first term also sets the pressure of the monolayer. From
$p=\rho\,\partial_\rho\mathfrak f-\mathfrak f$,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:pressure-definition}
  p=\frac B2\left(\rho^2-1\right)\simeq B(\rho-1),
\end{align}
where the last form is the leading order in $\rho-1$. 
This is the two-dimensional pressure $p$ of \cref{eq:dE-of-A} (\cref{app:extrusion}).

Integrating \cref{eq:final-langevin} over $\theta$ and keeping the leading terms in
$1/B$ (\cref{app:dean-angle}) gives a continuity equation for the density,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:model-rho}
  \partial_t\rho = -\nabla\tcdot\Bigl[
    \frac1{\xi_0}\bigl(-B\nabla\rho + \Q\!:\!\nabla\H + \fa\bigr)
    + \bEta_{\rho}(\r,t)\Bigr].
\end{align}
The first two terms in the bracket are passive, inherited from the pair potential through
the free energy. The term $-B\nabla\rho$ resists compression, and $\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$
carries cells along gradients of the nematic force field
$\H(\r)=-\delta F/\delta\Q= -a\Q - b(\Q\!:\!\Q)\Q + K\nabla^2\Q$ \citeTODO{active nematic}. The third term is the active force density
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:active-force}
  f^a_\alpha(\r) = -\zeta\,\partial_\beta Q_\ab(\r)
  \quad\text{with}\quad
  \zeta = \frac{\xi_0v_0^2}{4\frev},
\end{align}
the divergence of the active nematic stress $-\zeta\Q$. Positive $\zeta$ denotes extensile active stress.
The coefficient comes entirely from self-propulsion and reversal, so fast-reversing colonies are less active.

The last term is the noise of the density flux,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-def}
  \bEta_{\rho}(\r,t) = \int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t(\r,\theta)}\,
  \p(\theta)\,k(\r,\theta,t).
\end{equation}
Its autocorrelation follows from
\cref{eq:noise-correlators},
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation}
  \la \eta^{\rho}_\alpha(\r,t) \,\eta^{\rho}_\beta(\r',t') \ra
  \\
  =
  \frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \left[Q_\ab(\r)+\rho(\r) \delta_\ab\right]
 \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t') ,
\end{multline}
an isotropic piece proportional to $\rho$ and an anisotropic piece proportional to $\Q$.
Both scale with $\zeta$ of \cref{eq:active-force}, so the noise strength is set by the
same reversal rate $\frev$ and self-propulsion speed $v_0$ as the active force. The
rotational noise does not appear: every term it produces is a $\theta$ derivative, and
$\int\!\d\theta\,\partial_\theta(\cdot)=0$. The noise $\bEta_{\rho}$ sits inside the
divergence, so $\int\!\d\r\,\rho$ is constant in every realization of the noise, not only
in the mean.

\Cref{eq:model-rho} is a continuity equation,
$\partial_t\rho=-\nabla\!\cdot\!\bs J$. If we write $\bs J = \rho \v$, where $\v(\r,t)$
is the velocity field, and set $\rho\approx1$ in the incompressible limit, we get a force balance
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:velocity}
  \xi_0 \v = -B\nabla\rho + \Q\!:\!\nabla\H + \fa + \xi_0 \bEta_{\rho},
\end{align}
where the friction force density $\xi_0 \v$ on the cell is balanced by the passive interactions, the active nematic force and the noise. By \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, $B\nabla\rho\simeq\nabla p$ is the pressure gradient.
The elastic force $\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$ redistributes
cells to relieve orientational stress. Only $\fa$ does not derive from the free energy $F$. Since $\fa\propto\nabla\!\cdot\!\Q$, it vanishes in a
uniform nematic field and is largest where the field bends fastest, at a defect core
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}).

We also need a dynamical equation for the nematic order parameter $\Q$. Multiplying
\cref{eq:final-langevin} by $\hat\Q(\theta)$ and integrating over $\theta$ does not close
the system: angular averages like $\la\hat\Q\hat\Q\ra_\varrho$ appear, and $\rho$ and $\Q$ do not determine them. We use
the lowest-order maximum-entropy closure,
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho \simeq \rho\,\J$
with $\J$ the rank-four identity on symmetric traceless tensors. It is accurate to
relative $O(S^2)$ in the strength of the nematic order $S=\sqrt{(\Q\!:\!\Q)/2}$. \Cref{app:closure} derives the
higher-order terms and the limitations of this closure.

With the closure in place we obtain
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:model-Q}
  \partial_t \Q = {}& -\nabla\!\cdot\!\Bigl[\frac1{\xi_0}
    \bigl(-\Q B \nabla\rho + \nabla \H\bigr) + \bEta^\Q\Bigr]
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac4{\xir} \Ha + \f^Q.
\end{align}
The first two terms inside the divergence are the Onsager conjugates of the passive
terms of \cref{eq:model-rho}. $\Ha$ is the
nematic force field with coefficients renormalized by the rotational noise and the activity,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:H-active}
  \Ha &= -a'\Q - b(\Q\!:\!\Q)\Q + K'\nabla^2\Q,
  \nonumber\\
  a' &= a+\Lambda\xir, \qquad K' = K+{\zeta\xir}/({4\xi_0}).
\end{align}
It is $a'$ and $K'$, not the bare $a$ and $K$, that set the defect profile solved for in
\cref{sec:defect-steady}.

The relaxational and the transport terms of \cref{eq:model-Q} do different jobs. The
relaxational term $\tfrac4{\xir}\Ha$ carries no flux divergence. It relaxes $\Q$ toward a
minimizer of the effective free energy and fixes the shape of the defect. The transport
terms leave that shape almost unchanged, but through $\Q B\nabla\rho$ they couple the
nematic field to the density field and translate the
defect. 

The two noises of \cref{eq:model-Q} enter differently. $\bEta^\Q$ is a flux, the
rank-three tensor 
\begin{equation}
\eta^Q_{\ab i}=\int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\hat
Q_\ab(\theta)p_i(\theta)k(\r,\theta,t).
\end{equation}
Both $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$ carry the same
reversal noise $k(\r,\theta,t)$, so the two are correlated. The
second noise $\f^Q$ is local and non-conservative. It carries the rotational noise
$\lambda(\r,\theta,t)$ alone and is uncorrelated with both.
\Cref{app:dean-angle} gives their correlators.

In the rest of the paper we drop the terms proportional to $\nabla\H$ from
\cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q}. They introduce fourth-order spatial derivatives of $\Q$,
which we cannot treat either analytically or numerically. The two terms must
go together, because they are Onsager conjugates: dropping one alone would break
reciprocity. These terms are usually dropped in continuum models of dry active
nematics~\cite{ramaswamy2003active,bertin2013,mishra2010dynamic,ngo2014large,putzig2014phase,shankar2018low},
including those of \textit{M.\ xanthus} monolayers~\cite{copenhagen2021,han2025local}.
The usual argument is that the molecular field vanishes in the ordered state, so these
terms enter at higher order in gradients than the active
ones~\cite{shankar2018low,srivastava2016negative}.

\todo{We need to understand what physics we are missing by discarding these terms.}

\Cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} are the fluctuating active nematic equations this paper
solves. Activity enters them in three places: the force $\fa$,
the amplitude of the reversal noises $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$, and the renormalization of the nematic force field $\Ha$ in \cref{eq:H-active}. The
density and the nematic field are coupled in both directions, through
$\fa\propto\nabla\!\cdot\!\Q$ in one equation and through $\Q B\nabla\rho$ in the other.
That coupling sets a $+1/2$ defect in motion in \cref{sec:defect-steady}.

Everything that follows comes from solving \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} at a $+1/2$
defect, with the parameters collected in \cref{app:parameters}.



The closure truncates a hierarchy of moments over $\theta$ within one realization of the noise.
Continuum theories are usually built by truncating a different hierarchy. Averaging
Dean's equation over the noise gives an equation for the mean density that contains the
pair correlation. The equation for the pair correlation contains the three-point
correlation, and so on \cite{dean1996}. Dynamical density functional theory closes this
hierarchy with an equilibrium free energy \cite{marconi1999dynamic,tevrugt2020classical}.
Kinetic theories of self-propelled rods close the analogous hierarchy for the one-rod
distribution \cite{baskaran2008hydrodynamics}. Both give deterministic equations for the
ensemble-averaged density, because the fluctuations are averaged out before the closure.
A noise term belongs only in the equation for a density coarse-grained in space or time
\cite{archer2004dynamical}. \Cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} are equations for moments of
one realization, so they keep the noise of \cref{eq:final-langevin}.

\section{Fluctuations around a $+1/2$ defect}
\label{sec:defect}
% structure.md restructure, 2026-09-21. dean.tex 681-1266.
% ============================================================
Solving \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} at a $+1/2$ defect takes three steps: find the
 steady state of the noiseless equations
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}); linearize the stochastic equations around it; and solve the
resulting linear field theory for the equal-time covariance of the fluctuations
(\cref{sec:defect-fluct}). \Cref{app:parameters} collects the parameters.

We solve \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} numerically in
\cref{app:comoving}, and their linearization in \cref{app:lyapunov}. The main text gives analytical solutions of simplified
equations, which agree well with the numerics. The analytical solutions also show how the
result depends on the parameters of the model; the numerical solutions do not.

\subsection{The steady state}
\label{sec:defect-steady}
% Pages ~1.5. dean.tex 705-747, 819-894, 2460-2597 (solver moved to appendix, results summarised here).

We first solve a simplified system. The $+1/2$ defect moves because the density and the
nematic order are cross-coupled. Dropping the cross-coupling term in the $\Q$ equation
leaves a static problem:
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:simplified-noiseless}
  0 = \nabla\cdot\left[B\nabla\rho - \fa\right] , \qquad \Ha = 0.
\end{align}
We use polar coordinates $(r,\phi)$ centred on the core, with $\phi$ measured from the
$+x$ axis, the symmetry axis of the defect. At a $+1/2$ defect the director then makes the
angle $\theta=\phi/2$ with this axis. The solution of the second equation is given by a Pad\'{e} approximation \citeTODO{Pade approximation}:
\begin{align}
  \Q(r,\phi) = S(r)\hat\Q(\phi/2)
  \qquad
  S(r) = S_0 f_\pade(r/\elld)
  \nonumber\\
  \label{eq:pade-approximation}
  f_\pade(x) = x \sqrt{\frac{0.34 + 0.07 x^2}{1 + 0.41 x^2+0.07 x^4}}.
  \\
  S_0=\sqrt{-a'/(2b)} \qquad \elld=\sqrt{K'/(-a')}
  \label{eq:S0-elld}
\end{align}
Two scales appear. The far-field nematic order $S_0$ is the value $S(r)$
saturates to, and the core size $\elld$ is the distance over which $S$
recovers from $S(0)=0$ at the defect. Both are built from the renormalized $a'$
and $K'$ of \cref{eq:H-active}, not from the bare coefficients of the free energy
density \eqref{eq:free-energy-choice}. \Cref{app:parameters-choice} gives their values
\cite{copenhagen2021,han2025local} and \cref{app:density-poisson,app:comoving} the
accuracy of the approximant.

Now, the first equation in \cref{eq:simplified-noiseless} is a Poisson equation for the density
with an exact solution:
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-analytic}
  \rho(r,\phi) = 1 - \frac{\zeta}{B}\left[ S(r) - \frac rR\,S(R)\right]\cos \phi.
\end{align}
The solution needs a condition at a wall, and that condition is a
choice. No wall at a finite distance describes a defect in an extended colony, and none
of the possible walls is more physical than the others. We take the wall to be a
reservoir: on a circle of radius $R$ we impose $\rho=1$, as if the region inside the
wall were embedded in a monolayer held at the density that incompressibility imposes. The
numerical solution of \cref{app:comoving} imposes the same condition on its walls. The wall adds the linear term
$(r/R)S(R)$ of \cref{eq:rho-analytic}, which pulls the density back to one at $r=R$.
\Cref{app:density-poisson} derives \cref{eq:rho-analytic} and compares it with a wall that
blocks the flux of cells. In either case the density departs from uniformity only at
order $\zeta/B$.

Restoring the cross-coupling sets the defect in motion. It moves
\emph{opposite} to the active force. The active force does not push the
defect; it pushes the cells. The cells build a density gradient until the diffusive flux
cancels the active one, $B\nabla\rho\simeq\fa$. The nematic order parameter, however,
couples to the density gradient and not to the active force. The nematic flux in
\cref{eq:model-Q} is $\bs J_\Q = -\Q B\nabla\rho/\xi_0 \simeq -\Q\fa/\xi_0$, so $\Q$ is
transported with velocity $-B\nabla\rho/\xi_0$, down the density gradient and therefore
against $\fa$.

For the $+1/2$ defect \cref{eq:pade-approximation} gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:active-force-defect}
  f^\text{a}_x(r, \phi) = -\zeta \left(S'(r) +S(r)/r\right),
  \qquad f^\text{a}_y = 0,
\end{align}
a vector field along the defect axis with no transverse component. It points along $-x$,
the side on which the director bends around the core (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)), so the
defect drifts along $+x$ (\cref{app:comoving}).

A drifting defect has no steady state in the laboratory frame.
\Cref{app:comoving} therefore solves the noiseless \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q}
numerically in a frame comoving with the core, with neither simplification made above.
The closed form \cref{eq:rho-analytic} fixes $\Q$ to the Pad\'e profile
\cref{eq:pade-approximation} and solves only the density equation, whereas the numerics
solve both fields together and let $\Q$ depart from that profile.
\Cref{fig:rho1d-dirichlet} shows the resulting density along the
defect axis against \cref{eq:rho-analytic}, and \cref{fig:rho2d} the
two-dimensional field.

The cross-coupling also deforms the defect itself (\cref{fig:nematicaxis}). Near the

\subsection{Linearization and the fluctuation spectrum}
\label{sec:defect-fluct}
% Pages ~2.5. dean.tex 748-818, 895-1266.
Let $\delta\rho(\r,t)=\rho(\r,t)-\rho_\ss(\r)$ and $\delta\Q(\r,t)=\Q(\r,t)-\Q_\ss(\r)$.
Linearizing the comoving-frame form of \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} around the
steady state of \cref{sec:defect-steady} gives
(\cref{app:dean-angle})
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:linearized}
\begin{align}
    &\partial_t\delta\rho =-\nabla\!\cdot\!\Bigl[
    \frac1{\xi_0}\bigl(-B\nabla\delta\rho -\zeta\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\delta\Q \bigr) + \bEta_\rho(\r,t)\Bigr],
    \label{eq:linearized-rho}
    \\
    &\partial_t\delta\Q = \frac B{\xi_0}\nabla\!\cdot\!(\Q_\ss\nabla\delta\rho)
    + \nabla\!\cdot\!(\delta\Q\,\bs w_\ss) + \frac4{\xir}\delta\Ha
    \nonumber\\
    &\qquad\qquad
    {}- \nabla\!\cdot\!\bEta^\Q(\r,t)+ \f^Q(\r,t),
    \label{eq:linearized-Q}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
with $\u$ the velocity of the comoving frame (\cref{app:comoving}), $\bs w_\ss\equiv\u+(B/\xi_0)\nabla\rho_\ss$,
so that $\delta\Q$ is transported with velocity $-\bs w_\ss$, and
$\delta\Ha$ the linearization of \cref{eq:H-active} at $\Q_\ss$. The frame term is
kept only in the $\Q$ equation, through $\bs w_\ss$; in \cref{eq:linearized-rho} it is
smaller than the retained $B\nabla^2\delta\rho$ by at most $3\times10^{-4}$ (\cref{app:dean-angle}).
The three noises
are those of \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} with their amplitudes evaluated on the steady
state, which is their leading order in the fluctuation. This is a linear
stochastic field theory on the background $(\rho_\ss,\Q_\ss)$. \Cref{app:lyapunov}
obtains the equal-time covariance of $\delta\rho$ and $\delta\Q$ numerically, by solving
a Lyapunov equation.

The main text presents the analytically tractable case, in which only the density
fluctuates and $\delta\Q=0$. 
This is the appropriate limit when the $\zeta\,\nabla\cdot\delta\Q$ term in
\cref{eq:linearized-rho} is small compared with the noise $\bEta_\rho$. The standard deviation of the traction measured around defects in Ref.~\cite{han2025local} is about
ten times its mean, which is consistent with the reversal noise dominating.

\Cref{eq:linearized-Q} then drops out, and both of its
noises with it: only $\bEta_\rho$ survives.
The steady density departs from unity only at order $\zeta/B$ (\cref{eq:rho-analytic}), so we use a flat density profile in the noise autocorrelation:
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-ss}
  \la \eta^{\rho}_\alpha(\r,t) \,\eta^{\rho}_\beta(\r',t') \ra
  \\
  =\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \left[Q^\ss_\ab(\r)+\delta_\ab\right]
 \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t').
\end{multline}

The defect then enters \cref{eq:linearized-rho} in exactly one place, through the
$\Q_\ss$ term in \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss}. The defect reaches $\delta\rho$
through the noise amplitude alone, not through the relaxation rate.

Taking the Fourier transform of \cref{eq:linearized-rho} over space and time gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-fourier}
  \delta\tilde\rho(\k,\omega) =
  -\frac{\ii\,\k\cdot\tilde \bEta_\rho(\k,\omega)}{\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2},
  \qquad \gamma_0 \equiv B/\xi_0,
\end{align}
with $\k$ the wavevector and $k=|\k|$. The noise is conservative, so its power carries a
factor $k^2$ that cancels the $k^2$ of the relaxation rate $\gamma_0k^2$. The spectrum is
then flat in $\k$, and the equal-time variance
$\la\delta\rho(\r,t)\delta\rho(\r',t)\ra_\ss$ diverges as $\r'\to\r$.

The divergence comes from treating the monolayer as a continuum down to zero length. However, there is a physical cutoff at the width of a cell. Thus we introduce a short length scale cutoff
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cutoff}
    \elln = 0.5~\micron.
\end{equation}
We implement this by suppressing wavevectors above $1/\elln$ with a Gaussian filter, multiplying \cref{eq:rho-fourier} by $\ee^{-k^2\elln^2/4}$.
Everything below carries that factor.

Consider first the isotropic case $\Q_\ss=0$. Inverting the smoothed
\cref{eq:rho-fourier} over space and time gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic}
  \la\delta\rho(\r,t)\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra =
  \frac{\zeta}{2\pi B\rho_0 \elln^2}
  \frac{
      \exp\!\!\left(\!-\frac{|\r-\r'|^2}{2 \elln^2+4 \gamma_0 |t-t'|}\right)
      }{1 + 2 \gamma_0 |t-t'| / \elln^2}
\end{align}
so that the equal-point, equal-time variance is the constant pedestal
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:pedestal}
  \sigmarho(\r) = \sigmarho\big|_\text{pedestal}
  = \frac{\zeta}{2\pi B\rho_0\elln^2}.
\end{align}
The fluctuation is much larger than the deterministic modulation. \Cref{eq:pedestal} gives the standard deviation of the density,
$\sigma_\rho \sim B^{-1/2}$, while the scale of the modulation of the average density in \cref{eq:rho-analytic} is $B^{-1}$. In the large-$B$ limit the average density profile is overwhelmed by the fluctuations.

\Cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic} also gives the two scales over which a density
fluctuation stays coherent: a correlation length equal to the cutoff $\elln$ and a correlation time
\begin{align}
 \label{eq:tau-c}
 \tau_c = \frac{\elln^2}{2 \gamma_0},
\end{align}
the time a fluctuation of size $\elln$ takes to diffuse away. Neither scale involves the
defect. Uniform nematic order leaves $\tau_c$ unchanged, although it makes the
correlation between two points anisotropic (\cref{app:variance-uniform}). We neglect the
change of $\tau_c$ near the defect, and only $\tau_c$ is used below. \Cref{sec:nucleation-2}
uses $\tau_c$ to turn the layering probability into a rate, where it cancels from the ratio.

Restoring the anisotropic piece of \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss}, the one that
carries $\Q_\ss$, gives the total variance (\cref{app:variance})
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:total-gauss}
  \sigmarho(\r) = \sigmarho\big|_\text{pedestal}
  \left(1 + \frac{\elln^2}{4}\bigl(\kappa\star\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}\bigr)(\r)\right),
\end{align}
a convolution of the double divergence of the steady nematic field against the
isotropic real-space kernel
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kappa-real}
  \kappa(w) = \frac{1-\ee^{-2w^2/\elln^2}}{2\pi\,w^2}.
\end{equation}
\Cref{eq:total-gauss} is the central result of \cref{sec:defect}.
The defect modulates the variance around a uniform pedestal. The pedestal is the
variance of a uniform monolayer, fixed by the noise amplitude $\zeta$, the stiffness $B$
and the resolution $\elln$.
\Cref{fig:variance} shows sections of \cref{eq:total-gauss}.
Along the defect axis the
modulation is odd in $x$ and vanishes at the core, because
$\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}=\partial_x[S'(r)+S(r)/r]$ is odd. The variance is raised
on the $-x$ side, where it peaks about $1.5~\micron$ from the core at
$\nu_0\equiv[\sigmarho(\r_\mathrm{max})/\sigmarho(\infty)]-1=2.9\%$, evaluated on the exact
defect profile (\cref{app:density-poisson}), and lowered by as much on the $+x$ side. Along the $y$ axis the
simplified model predicts no modulation at all.
The scale of the modulation $\nu_0$ decreases with decreasing cutoff $\elln$ as shown in \cref{fig:ln_curve} and vanishes at $\elln=0$.

In \cref{fig:variance} we also show the density variance from the full numerical solution of \cref{eq:linearized}, retaining $\delta\Q\ne0$ (see \cref{app:lyapunov} for details). The simplified model captures its main features. \Cref{fig:variance3d} shows the two-dimensional profile of the density variance.

A modulation of two to three percent is the entire position\nobreakdash-dependent signal in the density variance profile. In \cref{sec:nucleation-2} we show that it modulates the layering probability by two orders of magnitude.

\section{Layer formation probability from the pressure fluctuations}
\label{sec:nucleation-2}
% The calculation motivated in \cref{sec:nucleation-1}, now that \cref{sec:defect}
% supplies the variance. Pressure statistics from the former sec:nucleation-theory,
% comparison with experiment from the former sec:nucleation-results
% (dean.tex 1392-1487). Former fig:match is panel (c) of fig:defects-layering.
% ============================================================

By \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, a density fluctuation $\delta\rho$ changes the
pressure by $B\,\delta\rho$. Since $\delta\rho$ is Gaussian with the variance of
\cref{eq:total-gauss}, $p$ is Gaussian too, with
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sigma-p}
    \sigma_p^2(\r) = B^2\sigmarho(\r),
    \qquad
    \sigma_{p,\infty}^2 = \frac{B\zeta}{2\pi\rho_0\elln^2},
\end{equation}
the second expression its far-field value, from the isotropic pedestal of
\cref{eq:total-gauss}.
As we argued for the average density, in the large-$B$ limit the mean pressure is $\la p(\r) \ra \ll \sigma_p(\r)$. We therefore set it to zero.
The probability in \cref{eq:p-layer-def} is then the Gaussian
tail
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:p-layer}
    \calP(\r) &\propto \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\,z(\r)}\ee^{-z(\r)^2/2},
    \qquad
    z(\r) &= \frac{\pcrit}{\sigma_p(\r)}.
\end{align}

The comparison with experiment is not to an absolute rate but to a \emph{ratio}:
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} reports the distribution of distances between defects and new
layers, normalized by the same distribution for random points in the monolayer. The attempt rate is $\calP/\tau_c$, and $\tau_c$ of \cref{eq:tau-c} is
essentially position-independent, so that ratio equals the ratio of layering
probabilities up to a slowly varying prefactor. \Cref{eq:p-layer} then gives
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:R-ratio}
    \frac{\calP(\r)}{\calP(\infty)} \simeq
    \exp\!\left[\frac{z_\infty^2}2\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{p,\infty}^2}{\sigma_p^2(\r)}\right)\right],
    \qquad z_\infty \equiv \frac{\pcrit}{\sigma_{p,\infty}}.
\end{equation}
Only the \emph{relative} modulation of the variance matters, and the large prefactor
$z_\infty^2/2$ amplifies it. A modulation of a few percent therefore produces an
enhancement of two orders of magnitude.


\Cref{fig:defects-layering}(c) shows the layering probability averaged over a circle of radius $r$
about a centre $\r_\mathrm c$, with $\phi$ the polar angle about that centre,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:p-layer-radial}
    \calP(r) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \oint\limits_{|\r-\r_\mathrm c|=r}\!\d\phi\,\calP(\r),
\end{equation}
relative to its far-field value. The experimental histogram bins distances from the
defect core, $\r_\mathrm c=0$. The variance modulation nearly vanishes at the core
(\cref{fig:variance}), so the profile centred there is small at $r=0$, peaks near
$1.5~\micron$ and then decays (orange). The measured profile instead decreases
monotonically from its first bin. Only a centre at the point of maximum probability,
$\r_\mathrm c=\r_\mathrm{max}\equiv\mathrm{argmax}\,\calP(\r)$, gives such a profile
(blue).

The experiments point to the same centre. In the snapshots of \cref{fig:defects-layering}(b),
the new layer starts off the core, on the side where the director bends around it. This
is where the model places $\r_\mathrm{max}$. No quantitative measurement of where layers
start relative to the defect has been published.

With $B=90~\kPa\tcdot\micron$, $\elln=0.5~\micron$ and the parameters of
\cref{app:parameters}, $\sigma_{p,\infty}=1.7~\kPa\tcdot\micron$ and
$z_\infty=20.8$. The numerical variance of \cref{eq:linearized} peaks at
$\nu_0\simeq2.3\%$, about $1.2~\micron$ off the core, and \cref{eq:R-ratio} then gives
the peak enhancement
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:R-value}
    \frac{\calP(\r_\mathrm{max})}{\calP(\infty)}
    = \exp\!\left[\frac{z_\infty^2}2\,\frac{\nu_0}{1+\nu_0}\right] \simeq 1.4\times10^2 .
\end{equation}
The curves of \cref{fig:defects-layering}(c) use the same variance. On finer grids its peak
rises toward $2.5\%$, and the enhancement toward $2.1\times10^2$
(\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff}). Both values lie within the error bar of the first bin of
Fig.~3f of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}, which spans $40$--$500$ around $2\times10^2$. 

The match is a one-parameter fit. Every quantity in \cref{eq:R-value} is fixed in advance
except the bulk modulus $B$, the stiffness of the incompressibility penalty in
\cref{eq:free-energy-choice}. $B$ is a model parameter rather than a measured constant.
The \emph{shape} of the variance profile in \cref{fig:variance} is not fitted.
\Cref{eq:total-gauss} fixes it once $\Q_\ss$ is known, with no adjustable parameter.

\textbf{Topics in the previous text}
\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{D1}] \textbf{What the fit tests.} The fit fixes only
  $\rho_0\pcrit^2\elln^2\nu_0/[(1+\nu_0)B\zeta]$, so it does not test the nucleation
  condition \cref{eq:nucleation-condition}. The stricter $\Delta E(\Acell)=0$ doubles
  $\pcrit$ and raises the fitted $B$ about fourfold, with the same profile. [P1]
\item[\textbf{D2}] \textbf{Noise structure from the coarse-graining.} The tensor $\Q_\ss+\I$
  of \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss} sets the shape of the variance profile, which the comparison
  tests; $\nu_0$ and the noise amplitude enter only through the fitted combination. [P2]
\item[\textbf{D3}] \textbf{Reversal rate as a control knob.} Through $\zeta\propto1/\frev$,
  the logarithm of the enhancement is nearly proportional to $\frev$: slower reversals give
  more layers, less concentrated at the defect, which \textit{frz} mutants can test.
  In the limit of rare reversals the noise is strongest and layers form most often.
  This agrees with Ref.~\cite{han2025local}, where \textit{frzE} cells, which reverse about
  every two hours~\cite{blackhart1985frizzy}, show larger traction fluctuations and more
  layering. Non-reversing mutants also form multilayers~\cite{peruani2012collective}. We
  model the reversals as a Poisson process, although the measured intervals are more
  regular~\cite{blackhart1985frizzy} and come from a gated oscillator with a refractory
  period~\cite{guzzo2018gated}. Density equations for particles that switch between states
  also exist beyond the white-noise limit~\cite{bressloff2024global,spinney2025dean}. A
  reversal statistics fitted to the measured intervals, and the test with \textit{frz}
  mutants of known reversal rates, are left for future work. [P3]
  \todo{confirm the non-reversing (frzE) traction and layering observation is in
Ref.~\cite{han2025local} and not in a companion paper before this sentence stands.}
\item[\textbf{D4}] \textbf{$B$ and $\elln$ are model parameters.} No measurement of $B$
  exists at this scale, the velocity fit of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} does not fix it,
  the \textit{E.\ coli} modulus is not counted as support, and the fitted $B$ moves with
  $\elln$ roughly as $\elln^{3.3}$. [P4]
\item[\textbf{D5}] \textbf{The idealized nucleus.} The patch is circular while a cell is a
  rod; the patch-averaged pressure has a variance three times smaller; the energies move
  $\pcrit$. At $\pcrit$ one cell still faces about $2\times10^7\,\kB T$, which the
  single-cell perturbations of \cref{sec:nucleation-1} must overcome. [P5]
\item[\textbf{D6}] \textbf{Approximations of the field theory.} The $\nabla\H$ terms are
  dropped; $\delta\Q=0$ has no bound and is checked numerically; the closure is accurate to
  $O(S^2)$ at $S_0=0.9$ and forces the exact moments in the noise; the Gaussian tail is used
  twenty-one standard deviations out, at $39\%$ compression, where the exact equation of
  state lowers the ratio from $1.4\times10^2$ to $37$. The pressure of
  \cref{eq:pressure-definition} is that of the passive steric term. For active particles with
  torques the mechanical pressure is in general not a function of
  state~\cite{solon2015pressure}, but the active contributions are small against the pressure
  fluctuations that trigger layering. The Gaussian form of the tail is itself an assumption,
  because measured density fluctuations in active nematics and bacterial swarms are
  anomalously large and collective~\cite{narayan2007longlived,zhang2010collective}. [P6]
\item[\textbf{D7}] \textbf{$-1/2$ defects.} The model already prefers $+1/2$ defects
  for layers. At a $-1/2$ defect the source $\partial_i\partial_jQ_{ij}$ vanishes as $r^3$
  inside the core, so the variance modulation is half as large and twice as far out, and its
  three lobes largely cancel on a circle about the core (\cref{app:negative}). The
  core-averaged enhancement drops from $76$ to about $4$, which the few events near $-1/2$
  defects in Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} cannot rule out. Holes are a different matter. A
  Gaussian with zero mean treats compression and dilation alike, so wherever the variance
  rises, holes become more likely along with layers, and the model would place holes more
  often at $+1/2$ than at $-1/2$ defects. The mean density cannot fix this: at both defects
  it is compressed on the side where the variance rises, since both follow from
  $\partial_i\partial_jQ_{ij}$, and its shift is of the order of the active stress. Holes at
  $-1/2$ defects therefore need an ingredient that separates dilation from compression, such
  as the anisotropic friction of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}, which in their model drives a
  net outflow from $-1/2$ defects. We leave them
  for future work.
  [P7]
\item[\textbf{D8}] \textbf{Traction variance as an observable.} Ref.~\cite{han2025local}
  maps the traction variance around defects. The traction is the divergence of the stress,
  not the pressure, so a comparison needs the traction variance at the resolution of the
  measurement, which we have not computed. [P8]
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Candidate new topics}
\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{N1}] \textbf{Where the layers start.} The variance peaks off the core, on the
  side where the director bends around it, which is where new layers start in the
  experiment (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(b)): an angular profile beyond the radial one.
  [intro, \cref{sec:nucleation-2}]
\item[\textbf{N2}] \textbf{Mean fields against fluctuations.} The mean density departs from
  one at order $\zeta/B$, while $\sigma_\rho\propto B^{-1/2}$, so at large $B$ the
  fluctuations dominate and arguments from average fields cannot give the enhancement.
  [\cref{sec:defect-steady,sec:defect-fluct}]
\item[\textbf{N3}] \textbf{The $\delta\Q$ force is large but slow.} Its standard deviation
  exceeds the mean active force, yet it barely changes the density variance because it
  evolves orders of magnitude more slowly than $\tau_c$; this bears on the large traction
  fluctuations of Ref.~\cite{han2025local}. [\cref{app:lyapunov-results}]
\item[\textbf{N4}] \textbf{The cutoff is physical.} The signature of the defect vanishes as
  $\elln\to0$; it exists because the density is resolved at the scale of a cell.
  Strictly, a continuum theory does not hold at this scale, because the cutoff is
  comparable to the lengths over which the variance changes. Even for an ideal gas, the
  density resolved over less than one particle per cutoff area is far from
  Gaussian~\cite{velenich2008brownian}. Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} nevertheless showed that a
  continuum active-nematic theory describes the flows around these defects, and we follow the
  same logic.
  [\cref{app:variance-cutoff,app:variance-ln}]
\item[\textbf{N5}] \textbf{Walls and the finite box.} The reservoir wall is a choice, no wall
  is more physical, and we assume the wall does not reach the core.
  [\cref{sec:defect-steady,app:density-poisson,app:lyapunov-walls}]
\item[\textbf{N6}] \textbf{White-noise limit of the reversals.} The density modes that set the
  variance relax over $\tau_c$, far faster than $1/\frev$, so treating the reversal noise
  as white is stretched at $\elln$. [not in the text; raised in the audit of 2026-09-25]
\item[\textbf{N7}] \textbf{Absolute rate.} The predicted absolute layering rate is far below an
  observable one; only the ratio is compared with the experiment. [not in the text; same
  audit]
\item[\textbf{N8}] \textbf{The threshold as a trigger.} Escape over a fluctuating
  barrier follows the instantaneous barrier only when the barrier changes slowly compared
  with the time of the crossing. When it changes fast, the crossing sees the average
  barrier~\cite{doering1992resonant,hanggi1990reaction}. We assume the slow limit and treat
  $p>\pcrit$ as a trigger: once the pressure exceeds $\pcrit$, a cell leaves the monolayer.
  [\cref{sec:nucleation-1,sec:nucleation-2}]
\item[\textbf{N9}] \textbf{Nucleation is one choice.} The nucleation criterion is
  one way to turn the pressure statistics into a layering probability. In growing biofilms,
  rods leave the plane by tilting, through a mechanical instability above a pressure that
  grows with their length~\cite{beroz2018verticalization}. In three-dimensional models of
  cell monolayers, extrusion follows a local build-up of stress~\cite{monfared2023mechanical}.
  Criteria like these could be built on the same density and pressure fluctuations of the
  reversing rods. [\cref{sec:nucleation-1}]
\end{itemize}

The surface tension dominates the critical pressure. We take
$\gammaw=65~\kPa\tcdot\micron$, the surface tension of the growth medium of
\textit{M.\ xanthus} measured by pendant drop~\cite{bourque2025active}. For comparison,
clean water has $72~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{vargaftik1983}. The cells also excrete
surfactant~\cite{dworkin1983b,islam2020}, and the supernatant of a wild-type culture has
about $60~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{islam2020}, which sets the lower end of the range in
\cref{tab:pcrit}. Since the surface tension exceeds $\Ws$ by more than an order of
magnitude, it is the wetting film, and not the adhesion, that holds the monolayer flat.

In the experiments no direct cell-cell interaction is observed, so we set $\Wc=0$.
Neighboring cells do attract each other, because the water menisci around them
overlap~\cite{black2025capillary}. However, this capillary cohesion is the surface energy
of the water film, which  \cref{eq:dE-of-A} already accounts
for. Wild-type cells also cohere through extracellular
fibrils~\cite{shimkets1986correlation,behmlander1994biochemical,pelling2005nanoscale}, but we neglect this.

For the adhesion to the substrate we rely on other bacteria. Their contact-angle data give
at most $3.8~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{bayoudh2006quantification}, while detaching one
\textit{E.\ coli} cell from glass costs only $0.035~\kPa\tcdot\micron$ when spread over
$\Acell$~\cite{duanisassaf2024factors}. We take $\Ws=2~\kPa\tcdot\micron$, within this
range. The remaining two parameters follow from the size of a cell. We take
$h=0.5~\micron$, the thickness of one cell layer~\cite{han2025local}, and
$\Acell=3~\micron^2$, the footprint of a rod $6~\micron$ long and $0.5~\micron$ wide. With
these values, \cref{eq:pcrit} gives $\pcrit=35.3~\kPa\tcdot\micron$.

\subsection{Parameters of the field equations}
\label{app:parameters-choice}
% Rewritten 2026-10-01 from Ashot's summary of the new estimates (jobscripts/
% set2_2_comove_optimized_2/params.md), then restyled for flow the same day at his prompt
% ("written in a boring way, too many times 'take'"). Numbers: newset_numbers.py.

Most parameters of the field equations have been measured, but the reported values spread
widely. One reason is that they depend on the experimental conditions. In the
monolayers of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}, cells glide at $0.92~\micron/\minute$ and reverse
at $0.17~\minute^{-1}$, whereas isolated cells glide at about $2~\micron/\minute$ and
reverse every $6.8~\minute$~\cite{blackhart1985frizzy}, and cells of a non-reversing strain
glide at $3.1~\micron/\minute$~\cite{peruani2012collective}. Another reason is that
they change in time. Under starvation, for example, cells glide faster and reverse less
often as development proceeds. Over $15~\mathrm h$ the mean speed rises from $1.7$ to
$2.7~\micron/\minute$, while the reversal frequency falls from $0.10$ to
$0.045~\minute^{-1}$~\cite{jelsbak2002pattern}, and Ref.~\cite{liu2019selfdriven}
reports the same trends. Since $\zeta=\xi_0v_0^2/(4\frev)$, these two changes alone raise
$\zeta$ sixfold. The reversal frequency depends on the density as well, because cells in
large groups reverse less often than isolated cells~\cite{shi1996cell}, and the density
itself rises as the cells aggregate. Where the reports differ, we therefore choose a value
within their range. \Cref{tab:field-params} collects these choices.

The density, $\rho_0=0.25~\micron^{-2}$, comes from Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}. The
friction coefficient has been estimated in two ways in Ref.~\cite{han2025local} (its
Supplementary Fig.~S12 and Sec.~II.C), and we use $\xi_0=5~\Pa\tcdot\minute/\micron$.

The activity is less certain, since the reported values of $\zeta/\xi_0$ span
$0.5$--$5~\micron^2/\minute$~\cite{han2025local,copenhagen2021}. Rather than pick a value
from this range, we use the relation of our model, \cref{eq:active-force}, which ties
$\zeta$ to the motility of single cells. With $v_0=1~\micron/\minute$~\cite{copenhagen2021} and
$\frev=0.1~\minute^{-1}$~\cite{han2025local}, it gives
$\zeta/\xi_0=v_0^2/(4\frev)=2.5~\micron^2/\minute$, and hence $\zeta=12.5~\Pa\tcdot\micron$.

The defect core has been measured in two different ways. Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}
infers it from the flow around $+1/2$ defects and finds $\elld\simeq1.5~\micron$ and
$S_0\simeq1$. Ref.~\cite{han2025local} instead measures the nematic field directly, from
the orientations of single cells. There $\elld\simeq0.8~\micron$, and $S$ rises to $0.9$
before it decays far away from the defect. We therefore set $\elld=1~\micron$ and $S_0=0.9$.

For the rotational diffusion coefficient we use $\Lambda=0.13~\minute^{-1}$, which
Ref.~\cite{liu2019selfdriven} infers from the decorrelation of isolated cells. The
rotational friction $\xir$, on the other hand, has not been measured, so we estimate it
from $\xi_0$. A rigid rod of length $L$ with uniform friction has $\xir=\xi_0L^2/12$,
which gives $\xir=15~\Pa\tcdot\micron\tcdot\minute$ for $L=6~\micron$. Notice that with these
values the rotational noise of \cref{eq:langevin-angle} is far from equilibrium. At
$T=20^\circ\mathrm C$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rotational-fdt}
  \frac{\Lambda\xir}{\rho_0\kB T}\simeq1.9\times10^3,
\end{equation}
with $\kB$ the Boltzmann constant, whereas in equilibrium the fluctuation-dissipation
theorem would make this ratio one~\cite{kubo1966fluctuation}.

The values of $S_0$ and $\elld$ do not yet fix the coefficients $a'$, $b$ and $K'$ of the
nematic free energy, since \cref{eq:S0-elld} gives only two relations among the three. We
therefore need a third input, and a natural one is the rate at which the nematic order
relaxes. To find this rate, consider a uniform $\Q$ with $\Q\!:\!\Q=2S_0^2$ that relaxes
through the local term of \cref{eq:model-Q}, $\partial_t\Q=4\Ha/\xir$. A small
perturbation then obeys (c.f.~\cref{eq:dH-active})
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:relaxation-rate}
  \partial_t\delta\Q=\frac4{\xir}\bigl[-(a'+b\,\Q\!:\!\Q)\,\delta\Q
  -2b\,(\Q\!:\!\delta\Q)\,\Q\bigr],
\end{equation}
so that a perturbation parallel to $\Q$ decays at the rate
$\gamma_Q=16bS_0^2/\xir=-8a'/\xir$.

This rate cannot be chosen freely, because the model bounds it from below. The bare free
energy \cref{eq:free-energy-choice} is stable only for $K>0$, and by \cref{eq:H-active}
this requires $K'>\zeta\xir/(4\xi_0)$. Substituting $K'=-a'\elld^2$ from
\cref{eq:S0-elld} then gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gamma-bound}
  \gamma_Q=\frac{8K'}{\xir\elld^2}>\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\elld^2}=5~\minute^{-1}.
\end{equation}
The rotational friction drops out of this bound, so it holds whatever the true $\xir$ is.
We choose $\gamma_Q=10~\minute^{-1}$, twice the bound, which means that the nematic field
relaxes over $6$ seconds. The coefficients of the free energy then follow from the
relations in \cref{tab:field-params}.

This leaves the bulk modulus $B$, the only free parameter of the model. We set
$B=90~\kPa\tcdot\micron$, because this value reproduces the layering probability data of
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} (\cref{sec:nucleation-2}, \cref{fig:defects-layering}(c)). No direct measurement exists to
compare it with, but related moduli, multiplied by the layer height $h$, span five orders
of magnitude. At the soft end, colonies of \textit{Serratia marcescens} on agar have shear
and compressive moduli of $10^2$--$10^4~\Pa$, or
$0.1$--$5~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{kochanowski2024}. The turgor pressure of
\textit{E.\ coli}, $29~\kPa$, corresponds to $15~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{deng2011direct},
and the effective cell modulus of $375~\kPa$ that fits the planar-to-bulk transition of
growing \textit{E.\ coli} colonies corresponds to $190~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{grant2014}.
Indentation of live \textit{M.\ xanthus} cells gives a local Young's modulus of
$0.25~\mathrm{MPa}$, or about $125~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{pelling2005nanoscale}, although
this is the stiffness of the cell envelope, not the compressibility of the monolayer.
At the stiff end, the cell wall has a Young's modulus of $25$--$50~\mathrm{MPa}$, or about
$10^4~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{yao1999thickness,deng2011direct}. The fitted $B$ thus lies
between the turgor pressure and the effective cell modulus, close to the envelope
stiffness of \textit{M.\ xanthus}.

\section{Elastic energy released by the bottom layer}
\label{app:extrusion}
% dean.tex 1293-1337. Supports \cref{sec:nucleation-1} and \cref{eq:pressure-definition}.
% Written as prose 2026-09-30. The V9 prompts stand: no threshold on rho d_rho f, no size
% estimate of the finite-area term.

\Cref{eq:dE-of-A} counts the pressure work $pA$ as the energy a patch gains when it leaves
the monolayer. This gain comes from the elastic energy of the compressed cells, so here we
derive it from the free energy. Only the bulk term $\mathfrak f=\tfrac B2(\rho-1)^2$ of
\cref{eq:free-energy-choice} depends on $\rho$, and we therefore keep only this term.

Consider a region of area $A_d$ that is compressed uniformly to $\rho=1+\epsilon$, so that
it stores the energy $\mathfrak fA_d=\tfrac B2\epsilon^2A_d$. A patch of area $A$ inside
this region then forms the new layer and takes $\rho_0(1+\epsilon)A$ cells with it. As the
bottom layer spreads back over the whole of $A_d$ (\cref{fig:nucleation}(a)), its strain
falls to $\epsilon_f=\epsilon-(1+\epsilon)A/A_d$. The extruded cells, in contrast, are
unconfined in the new layer and relax to zero strain. The elastic energy therefore changes
by $\Delta E_e=\tfrac B2(\epsilon_f^2-\epsilon^2)A_d$, which we write as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dEe-full}
    \Delta E_e = \frac B2\left[-2\epsilon - 2\epsilon^2
    + (1+\epsilon)^2\frac A{A_d}\right]A.
\end{equation}

The two parts of the system contribute differently to this release. The bottom layer
releases $pA-\tfrac B2(1+\epsilon)^2A^2/A_d$, where $p=\tfrac B2(\rho^2-1)$ is the exact
pressure of \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, while the extruded cells release their own
strain energy $\mathfrak fA$. For a patch much smaller than the compressed region,
$A\ll A_d$, the term in $A^2/A_d$ is negligible, and
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dEe}
    \Delta E_e \simeq -(p+\mathfrak f)A \simeq -pA.
\end{equation}
The $p$ of \cref{eq:dE-of-A} is thus the pressure $\rho\,\partial_\rho\mathfrak f-\mathfrak f$
of \cref{eq:pressure-definition}.

In the last step of \cref{eq:dEe} we drop $\mathfrak f$. This is consistent at leading
order, because $\mathfrak f$ is quadratic in $\epsilon$ while $p$ is linear, and
\cref{eq:dE-of-A} works at this order. At the threshold, however, the correction is not
small: there $\epsilon=\pcrit/B\simeq0.39$, and the dropped term adds about $16\%$ to $pA$.


\section{Coarse-graining}
\label{app:dean}
% dean.tex 174-526 (derivation), 681-811 (truncation rule, comoving frame, linearization).
% Written as prose 2026-09-30. Identities checked in claude_experiments/appC_check.wls.
% dean.tex's frame-term numbers (10^-4 "at ell_d", its u) belong to the B = 10^4 set;
% the ones below come from the 2026-10-01 parameter set (set2_2_comove_optimized_2 meta
% files, B = 9e4; claude_experiments/newset_numbers.py).

The noise in \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} follows from the reversals of the
individual rods, with the amplitude of the active force and a tensor structure set by the
nematic order. To show this we obtain Dean's equation for the joint density
of positions and orientations, then integrate out the orientation to reach the field
equations for $\rho$ and $\Q$ of \cref{sec:model-coarse,sec:model-closure}, and
finally explain why we work with the unnormalized order parameter.


\subsection{Derivation of Dean's equation}
\label{app:dean-approach}
We derive \cref{eq:final-langevin} with It\^o's lemma, so \cref{eq:model} must be read in
the It\^o sense. This needs a justification, because $k_i$ is the white limit of a
coloured noise, and such a limit converges to the Stratonovich interpretation
instead~\cite{wong1965convergence}. In our model, however, the two interpretations
coincide. The drift that separates them is $\tfrac12 g\,\partial g$, where $g$ is the
amplitude of a noise and the derivative is taken along the variables that this noise
drives. The amplitude $v_0\p(\theta_i)$ of $k_i$ depends only on $\theta_i$, which
$k_i$ does not drive, so its drift vanishes.
The other noise term, $\lambda_i$, is additive, so its drift vanishes too.
\Cref{eq:final-langevin} therefore does not depend on the interpretation.

To apply It\^o's lemma, we follow the rod through a test function. For any smooth
test function $\phi(\r,\theta)$, we can write
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dean-ito-single}
  \phi(\r_i,\theta_i)=\int\!\d\r\,\d\theta\,\phi(\r,\theta)\,\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta),
\end{equation}
where $\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta) =
\delta(\r-\r_i(t))\,\delta(\theta-\theta_i(t))$ is the single-particle density.
Differentiating both sides in time gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dean-ito-time}
  \frac{\d\phi}{\d t} = \int\!\d\r\,\d\theta\,\phi(\r,\theta)\,\pd{}{t}\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta).
\end{equation}
The right-hand side contains the quantity we are after, $\partial_t\varrho^{(i)}_t$.
We therefore evaluate the left-hand side in a second way, using the It\^o formula:
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:dean-ito}
  \d\phi(\r_i,\theta_i) = \pd{\phi}{\r}\!\cdot\!\d\r_i + \pd{\phi}{\theta}\,\d\theta_i
  \\
  + \frac12\pd{^2\phi}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}\d r_\alpha \d r_\beta
  + \pd{^2\phi}{r_\alpha\partial \theta}\d r_\alpha \d \theta
  + \frac12\pd{^2\phi}{\theta^2}\d \theta \d\theta,
\end{multline}
with the derivatives of $\phi$ evaluated at the rod.
In the white-noise limit, the quadratic variations of the increments of
\cref{eq:model} are $\d r_\alpha\d r_\beta=(v_0^2/\frev)\,p_\alpha p_\beta\,\d t$,
$\d \theta \d\theta=2\Lambda\,\d t$ and $\d\r\,\d\theta=0$.
With $\d\r_i$ and $\d\theta_i$ from \cref{eq:model}, the left-hand side of
\cref{eq:dean-ito-time} therefore becomes
\begin{multline}
  \frac{\d\phi}{\d t} = \pd{\phi}{\r}\!\cdot\!\left[
    \frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F_i + v_0\,\p(\theta_i) k_i
  \right]
  + \pd{\phi}{\theta}\left[
    \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma_i + \lambda_i
  \right]
  \\
  + \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha(\theta_i) p_\beta(\theta_i)\,\pd{^2\phi}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\phi}{\theta^2},
\end{multline}
where $\F_i = \F(\r_i,\theta_i)$ and $\Gamma_i = \Gamma(\r_i,\theta_i)$, with the
fields of \cref{eq:force-torque}.
Every term on the right is again a function evaluated at the rod, so
\cref{eq:dean-ito-single} applies to each of them:
\begin{multline}
\frac{\d\phi}{\d t} = \int\!\d\r\,\d\theta\,\varrho^{(i)}_t
\Bigg(\pd{\phi}{\r}\tcdot\left[
    \frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F + v_0\,\p k_i
  \right]
  \\
  + \pd{\phi}{\theta}\left[
    \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma + \lambda_i
  \right]
  \\
 + \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha p_\beta\,\pd{^2\phi}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\phi}{\theta^2}
\Bigg),
\end{multline}
with $\F$, $\Gamma$ and $\p$ now evaluated at $(\r,\theta)$.
Here the derivatives act on $\phi$, whereas \cref{eq:dean-ito-time} has $\phi$ itself
under the integral. We therefore integrate by parts, once in the first-order terms and
twice in the second-order ones. There are no boundary terms, because $\varrho^{(i)}_t$
vanishes away from the rod and is periodic in $\theta$. Since $\p$ depends only on
$\theta$, the coefficient $p_\alpha p_\beta$ passes through the $\r$ derivatives unchanged,
and we obtain
\begin{multline}
  \frac{\d\phi}{\d t} =
  \int\d\r\d\theta\, \phi(\r,\theta) \Bigg(
    -\pd{}{\r}\tcdot\Bigl[\varrho^{(i)}_t
    \Bigl(\frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F+v_0\p\,k_i\Bigr)\Bigr]
  \\
  - \pd{}{\theta}\Bigl[\varrho^{(i)}_t\Bigl(\tfrac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma+\lambda_i\Bigr)\Bigr]
  + \frac{v_0^2 p_\alpha p_\beta}{2\frev}\,
    \pd{^2\varrho^{(i)}_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho^{(i)}_t}{\theta^2}
\Bigg).
\end{multline}
This must equal \cref{eq:dean-ito-time} for every $\phi$, so the two integrands
agree, which gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:dean-single}
  \partial_t\varrho^{(i)}_t \!=&\!  -\pd{}{\r}\tcdot\Bigl[\varrho^{(i)}_t
    \!\Bigl(\tfrac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F\!+v_0\p\,k_i\Bigr)\Bigr]
 \!-\! \pd{}{\theta}\Bigl[\varrho^{(i)}_t\Bigl(\tfrac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma\!+\!\lambda_i\Bigr)\Bigr]
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha p_\beta\,
    \pd{^2\varrho^{(i)}_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho^{(i)}_t}{\theta^2}.
\end{align}
The last line holds the It\^o
terms. The self-propulsion noise $v_0\p\,k_i$ and its It\^o term carry the same amplitude
$v_0^2/\frev$, and this shared amplitude is what ties the active stress to the noise. We
write the coefficient as $v_0^2/(2\frev)=2\zeta/\xi_0$, which defines the $\zeta$ of
\cref{eq:active-force}.

Next we sum \cref{eq:dean-single} over $i$, with the weight $\rho_0^{-1}$ of
\cref{eq:joint-density-definition}. Since $\F$, $\Gamma$ and $\p$ are fields that do not
depend on $i$, each drift term becomes the same term for $\varrho_t$. The noise terms, on
the other hand, keep their rod index, and we collect them in
$\bs\chi=\rho_0^{-1}v_0\sum_i\varrho^{(i)}_t\,\p\,k_i$ and
$\psi=\rho_0^{-1}\sum_i\varrho^{(i)}_t\lambda_i$. The joint density therefore obeys
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:dean-sum}
  \partial_t\varrho_t = {}& -\frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\pd{}{\r}\tcdot(\varrho_t\F)
  - \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\pd{}{\theta}(\varrho_t\Gamma)
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0}\,p_\alpha p_\beta\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{\theta^2}
  \nonumber\\
  &- \pd{}{\r}\tcdot\bs\chi - \pd{}{\theta}\psi .
\end{align}
The force and torque of \cref{eq:force-torque} still depend on the positions of all
rods, but we can express them through $\varrho_t$. To this end, consider the pair free
energy of \cref{eq:pair-free-energy} and its functional derivative,
\begin{align}
  \calF[\varrho] =& \frac{\rho_0^2}2\!\int\!\d\r\d\r'\,\d\theta\d\theta'\,
  V(\r\!-\!\r'\!,\theta,\theta')\,\varrho(\r,\theta)\,\varrho(\r'\!,\theta'),
  \nonumber
  \\
  \frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}=&\rho_0^2\int\!\d\r'\d\theta'\,
V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')\,\varrho_t(\r',\theta')
\nonumber\\
=& \rho_0\sum_jV(\r-\r_j,\theta,\theta_j).
\label{eq:dean-functional-derivative}
\end{align}
Both factors of $\varrho$ contribute equally to the derivative, because of the pair
symmetry $V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')=V(\r'-\r,\theta',\theta)$, and the last step uses
\cref{eq:joint-density-definition}.
Comparing with \cref{eq:force-torque}, we find $\F=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\r\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$
and $\Gamma=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\theta\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$, and substituting
these into \cref{eq:dean-sum} gives the first two lines of \cref{eq:final-langevin}.

The noise terms $\bs\chi$ and $\psi$ are not yet functions of $\varrho_t$, because each of
them carries its own $k_i$ or $\lambda_i$. The noises are Gaussian, however, so it
suffices to match their correlators. Since different rods have independent noises, only
$i=j$ survives the average of $\bs\chi\bs\chi$, and two delta functions at the same rod
collapse to one,
$\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta)\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r',\theta')
=\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta)\,\delta(\r-\r')\delta(\theta-\theta')$. Hence
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dean-noise-sum}
  \la\chi_\alpha(\r,\theta,t)\,\chi_\beta(\r',\theta',t')\ra
  = \frac{v_0^2}{\frev\rho_0}\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)\,p_\alpha(\theta)p_\beta(\theta)\,\Delta,
\end{equation}
with $\Delta\equiv\delta(t-t')\delta(\r-\r')\delta(\theta-\theta')$. This correlator
is linear in $\varrho_t$, and the field $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p\,k$, with $k$ of
\cref{eq:noise-correlators}, has exactly the same one. In the same way, the
rotational noise $\psi$ has the correlator
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dean-noise-sum-psi}
  \la\psi(\r,\theta,t)\,\psi(\r',\theta',t')\ra
  = \frac{2\Lambda}{\rho_0}\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)\,\Delta,
\end{equation}
and so has $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda$.
Both identities hold at fixed $\varrho_t$, which is all an It\^o increment needs. We can
therefore replace $\bs\chi$ by $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p\,k$ and $\psi$ by
$\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda$, which gives the last two lines of \cref{eq:final-langevin}.

\subsection{The fluctuating active nematic equations}
\label{app:dean-angle}

The field equations follow from \cref{eq:final-langevin} by integration over $\theta$, and
for this we need the $\theta$ dependence of $\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$.
To find it, we use the parametrization \cref{eq:free-energy-parametrisation},
$\calF[\varrho]=F[\rho,\Q]$, in which $\rho$ and $\Q$ are themselves functionals of
$\varrho$ through \cref{eq:density-definition}. The chain rule
\cref{eq:chain-rule} then follows from 
\begin{align}
  \frac{\delta\rho(\r')}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}=\delta(\r-\r'),
  \quad
  \frac{\delta\Q(\r')}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}=\hat\Q(\theta)\,\delta(\r-\r'),
\end{align}
 so
$\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$ holds only the angular harmonics $0$ and $2$. As a result, the
interaction terms of the density equation integrate to $\rho$ and $\Q$ alone. In the $\Q$
equation, by contrast, they leave angular moments, which we write as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:average-def}
\la X\ra_\varrho\equiv\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)X(\theta).
\end{equation}

We start with the density. Integrating \cref{eq:final-langevin} over $\theta$ removes every
$\theta$ derivative, and the self-propulsion term needs only
$\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,p_\alpha p_\beta=\tfrac12(Q_\ab+\rho\,\delta_\ab)$, which
follows from $\p\p=\tfrac12(\hat\Q+\I)$. Before any approximation, the density therefore
obeys
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-full}
  \partial_t\rho = {}& \frac1{\xi_0}\nabla\tcdot\Bigl(\rho\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}
    + \Q\!:\!\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\Q}\Bigr)
  \nonumber\\
  &+\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\bigl(\nabla^2\rho+\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta Q_\ab\bigr)
  - \nabla\tcdot\bEta_\rho,
\end{align}
where the noise is the angular integral of the noise $\bs\chi$ of
\cref{eq:dean-sum},
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-chi}
  \bEta_\rho=\int\!\d\theta\,\bs\chi
  =\frac{v_0}{\rho_0}\sum_i\delta(\r-\r_i)\,\p(\theta_i)\,k_i .
\end{equation}
It is the sum of the self-propulsion noises of the rods at $\r$, and replacing
$\bs\chi$ by $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p\,k$ gives \cref{eq:eta-rho-def}. Each term
of \cref{eq:rho-full} has a simple meaning. With
\cref{eq:free-energy-choice}, the first term,
$\rho\nabla(\delta F/\delta\rho)=B\rho\nabla\rho=\nabla p$, is the gradient of the exact
pressure \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, and the second is $-\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$. The $\zeta$
term, in turn, splits into the diffusion $\zeta\nabla^2\rho/\xi_0$ and the divergence of
the active force \cref{eq:active-force}.

\Cref{eq:rho-full} is still more detailed than we need, because the large modulus $B$
keeps $\rho$ close to one. In the steady state $\rho-1$ is of order
$\zeta/B=1.4\times10^{-4}$ (\cref{eq:rho-analytic}), and even its fluctuations have a
standard deviation of only $1.9\%$ (\cref{eq:pedestal}). We therefore keep the leading
order in $\rho-1$ at fixed pressure, counting $B(\rho-1)$ as of order one. This removes
the diffusion, which is smaller than $B\nabla^2\rho/\xi_0$ by $\zeta/B$, and it reduces
$\nabla p=B\rho\nabla\rho$ to $B\nabla\rho$, so that \cref{eq:rho-full} becomes
\cref{eq:model-rho}. The noise correlator \cref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation}, on the other
hand, needs neither the truncation nor a closure. It follows from
\cref{eq:eta-rho-chi}, the correlator \cref{eq:dean-noise-sum} of $\bs\chi$ and the
angular integral used above,
\begin{align}
  &\la\eta^\rho_\alpha(\r,t)\,\eta^\rho_\beta(\r',t')\ra
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\int\!\d\theta\,\d\theta'\,
  \la\chi_\alpha(\r,\theta,t)\,\chi_\beta(\r',\theta',t')\ra
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{v_0^2}{\frev\rho_0}\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,p_\alpha p_\beta\;
  \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t')
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}\bigl(Q_\ab+\rho\,\delta_\ab\bigr)\,
  \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t').
  \nonumber
\end{align}

The $\Q$ equation follows in the same way, except that we multiply
\cref{eq:final-langevin} by $\hat\Q(\theta)$ before integrating. The $\theta$ derivatives
now survive, but one integration by parts turns them into $\hat\Q'\equiv\partial_\theta\hat\Q$,
and the identity $\hat\Q''=-4\hat\Q$ removes the second derivatives. In components, with
summation over repeated indices (recall \cref{eq:average-def})
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:Q-full}
  \partial_t Q_\ab = {}& \frac1{\xi_0}\partial_i\Bigl(Q_\ab\,\partial_i\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}
  + \langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_\mn\rangle_\varrho\,\partial_i\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_\mn}\Bigr)
  \nonumber\\
  &- \frac1{\xir}\langle\hat Q'_\ab\hat Q'_\mn\rangle_\varrho\,\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_\mn}
  - 4\Lambda Q_\ab
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\bigl(\nabla^2Q_\ab
  + \partial_i\partial_j\langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_{ij}\rangle_\varrho\bigr)
  \nonumber\\
  &- \partial_i\eta^Q_{\ab i} + f^Q_\ab .
\end{align}
Here the first line comes from the translational friction, the second from the rotational
friction and diffusion, the third from self-propulsion, and the last from the two noises.
Like $\bEta_\rho$, they are angular integrals of the noises of \cref{eq:dean-sum},
now weighted by $\hat\Q$,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:Q-noise-chi}
  \eta^Q_{\ab i}=\int\!\d\theta\,\hat Q_\ab\,\chi_i,
  \qquad
  f^Q_\ab=\int\!\d\theta\,\hat Q'_\ab\,\psi.
\end{align}
The second follows from $-\int\!\d\theta\,\hat\Q\,\partial_\theta\psi$ by one
integration by parts in $\theta$, which turns the minus sign into a plus. Replacing
$\bs\chi$ by $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p\,k$ and $\psi$ by $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda$ gives the
$\bEta^\Q$ of \cref{sec:model-closure} and
$f^Q_\ab=\int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\hat Q'_\ab\,\lambda$. With these
definitions every noise carries a plus sign: $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$ enter
\cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} as $-\nabla\tcdot\bEta$, with the plus sign inside the
flux, exactly as the noise of \cref{eq:final-langevin} does, and $\f^Q$ enters with a plus
sign.

Unlike the density equation, \cref{eq:Q-full} is not closed, because the moments
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$ and $\langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho$ are not functions of
$\rho$ and $\Q$. We close it with the lowest-order closure of \cref{app:closure}, which sets
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho\simeq\rho\J$ and $\langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho\simeq4\rho\J$, with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:J-definition}
  J_\abmn=\tfrac12(\delta_{\alpha\mu}\delta_{\beta\nu}+\delta_{\alpha\nu}\delta_{\beta\mu}
  -\delta_\ab\delta_\mn) 
\end{equation}
The tensor $\J$ leaves the symmetric traceless $\delta F/\delta\Q$
unchanged, and it gives $\J\!:\!\nabla\nabla\rho=\nabla\nabla\rho-\tfrac12\I\nabla^2\rho$.
With this closure, and still without any approximation in $\rho-1$, \cref{eq:Q-full}
becomes
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:Q-closed}
  \partial_t Q_\ab =
  \frac{1}{\xi_0}\partial_i\Bigl(Q_\ab\,\partial_i\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}\Bigr)
  +\frac{1}{\xi_0}\partial_i\Bigl(\rho\,\partial_i\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_\ab}\Bigr)
  \\
  -\frac{4\rho}{\xir}\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_\ab} -4\Lambda Q_\ab
  +\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\Bigl(\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta\rho
  +\nabla^2\bigl(Q_\ab-\tfrac12\rho\,\delta_\ab\bigr)\Bigr)
  \\
  - \partial_i\eta^Q_{\ab i}+ f^Q_\ab .
\end{multline}
The density now enters in two ways: as the prefactor $\rho$ of the two
$\delta F/\delta\Q$ terms, and through its second derivatives in the $\zeta$ term. The
truncation of the density equation removes both. The derivative terms,
$(\zeta/\xi_0)(\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta\rho-\tfrac12\delta_\ab\nabla^2\rho)$, are
smaller than the retained $(B/\xi_0)\,\partial_i(Q_\ab\partial_i\rho)$ by a factor of order
$\zeta/B$, and the prefactor $\rho$ can be set to one. The rotational friction then gives
$(4/\xir)\H$, and the translational friction $-\nabla^2\H/\xi_0$. Rotational diffusion, $-4\Lambda\Q$, and the
self-propulsion term $(\zeta/\xi_0)\nabla^2\Q$ then have the same form as the terms $-a\Q$
and $K\nabla^2\Q$ of $\H$. They therefore combine with it into $(4/\xir)\Ha$, with the $a'$
and $K'$ of \cref{eq:H-active}, and with $\delta F/\delta\rho=B(\rho-1)$ the result is
\cref{eq:model-Q}.

The correlators of the two $\Q$ noises follow from their definitions
\cref{eq:Q-noise-chi} exactly as for $\bEta_\rho$, with the correlators
\cref{eq:dean-noise-sum,eq:dean-noise-sum-psi} of $\bs\chi$ and $\psi$ now weighted by
$\hat\Q$ or $\hat\Q'$. For the noises of \cref{eq:model-Q} they read
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:Q-noise-correlators}
  \langle\eta^Q_{\ab i}\,\eta^Q_{\mn j}\rangle &= \frac{2\zeta\Delta'}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \bigl[\langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_\mn\hat Q_{ij}\rangle_\varrho
  + \langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_\mn\rangle_\varrho\delta_{ij}\bigr],
  \nonumber\\
  \langle\eta^Q_{\ab i}\,\eta^\rho_j\rangle &= \frac{2\zeta\Delta'}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \bigl[\langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_{ij}\rangle_\varrho + Q_\ab\,\delta_{ij}\bigr],
  \nonumber\\
  \langle\!f^Q_\ab\,f^Q_\mn\rangle &= \frac{2\Lambda\Delta'}{\rho_0}\,
  \langle\hat Q'_\ab\hat Q'_\mn\rangle_\varrho,
\end{align}
with $\Delta'\equiv\delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t')$ and the moments taken at $\r$. Since
$\psi$ and $\bs\chi$ are independent, $\f^Q$ is uncorrelated with $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$.
\Cref{app:closure} evaluates the moments; at lowest order, for example, the last
correlator is $(8\Lambda\rho/\rho_0)\,\J\,\Delta'$.

The linearized equations \cref{eq:linearized} follow from \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} in
the frame comoving with the core, where $\partial_t\to\partial_t-\u\tcdot\nabla$. We
substitute $\rho=\rho_\ss+\delta\rho$ and $\Q=\Q_\ss+\delta\Q$ and keep the first order in
$\delta\rho$ and $\delta\Q$. For the nematic force field this gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:dH-active}
  \delta\Ha = {}& -a'\delta\Q
  - b\bigl[(\Q_\ss\!:\!\Q_\ss)\,\delta\Q + 2(\Q_\ss\!:\!\delta\Q)\,\Q_\ss\bigr]
  \nonumber\\
  &+ K'\nabla^2\delta\Q ,
\end{align}
while the transport term of \cref{eq:model-Q} gives
$(B/\xi_0)\nabla\tcdot(\Q_\ss\nabla\delta\rho+\delta\Q\nabla\rho_\ss)$. Its second part
combines with the frame term $\nabla\tcdot(\delta\Q\,\u)$ into the advection by
$\bs w_\ss$ in \cref{eq:linearized-Q}.
The noise amplitudes are evaluated on the steady state, so the linearized equations
carry additive noise, and for them the It\^o and Stratonovich interpretations coincide
trivially.

A comparison of rates shows that the frame velocity term matters only in the $\Q$ equation. In the
density equation $\u\tcdot\nabla\delta\rho$ competes with
$(B/\xi_0)\nabla^2\delta\rho$, and for $\delta\rho$ varying over a length $L$ their
ratio is $\xi_0|\u|L/B$. Our steady
state has $|\u|=0.24~\micron/\minute$, so the ratio is $2.7\times10^{-4}$ at $L=20~\micron$, the half-width of our box
(\cref{app:comoving}), and we drop it. In the $\Q$ equation, by contrast, the advection
$\nabla\tcdot(\delta\Q\,\bs w_\ss)$ competes with the much slower elastic relaxation
$(4K'/\xir)\nabla^2\delta\Q$. Since $|\bs w_\ss|$ reaches $0.96~\micron/\minute$, their
ratio $|\bs w_\ss|\,\xir L/(4K')$ is already $0.19$ at $L=\elld$, so we keep it.

\subsection{Definitions of the nematic order parameter}
\label{app:dean-normalisation}
We use the unnormalized $\Q$ of \cref{eq:density-definition} because it is linear in
$\varrho_t$. Its functional derivative is therefore simple, whereas the normalized
$\Q/\rho$ carries an additional term,
$\delta(\Q/\rho)/\delta\varrho=(\hat\Q-\Q/\rho)\,\delta(\r-\r')/\rho$, which would crowd the
final equations.

The price of this choice is small. The strength of the nematic order acquires a factor
$\rho^{-1}$, $S=\rho^{-1}\sqrt{(\Q\!:\!\Q)/2}$, and the closure acquires bare factors of
$\rho$. However, the two definitions differ only at order $\rho-1$, so they coincide
in the incompressible limit of large $B$. The truncation of \cref{app:dean-angle} drops
this order anyway, and the Pad\'e profile \cref{eq:pade-approximation} is accordingly
written at $\rho=1$.

\section{Maximum-entropy closure}
\label{app:closure}
% dean.tex 2090-2262 (construction), 2263-2450 (realizability). Written 2026-10-02.
% Numbers at the fourth-generation set (N150, B = 9e4 steady state of the Lyapunov run),
% not dean.tex's (87.6% there is the old set). Checks: claude_experiments/appD_check.wls.

The $\Q$ equation \cref{eq:Q-full} and the correlators \cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators} of
its noises contain the angular moments $\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$,
$\langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho$ and $\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$.
These depend on the whole angular distribution, which $\rho$ and $\Q$ do not determine, so
closing the equations of \cref{sec:model-closure} needs a rule that assigns a
distribution to every pair $(\rho,\Q)$. We take the distribution that has the given
$\rho$ and $\Q$ and otherwise carries as little information as possible, the one of
maximum entropy. Below we construct it, compute its moments exactly and expand them
at weak nematic order. The expansion fails to give a valid noise covariance at the strong
order of the defect steady state, as \cref{app:realizability} shows, and the exact moments
repair it.

At a point $\r$, consider all angular distributions $\varrho(\theta) = \varrho_t(\theta \,|\, \r)$ with
$\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho=\rho$ and $\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\,\hat\Q=\Q$. Maximizing the
entropy $\int\!\d\theta\varrho\ln(1/\varrho)$ under these constraints, with a scalar and a
tensor Lagrange multiplier, gives $\varrho\propto\exp[\bs\lambda\!:\!\hat\Q(\theta)]$.
Since $\hat\Q$ contains only $\cos2\theta$ and $\sin2\theta$, this is a von Mises
distribution in $2\theta$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:von-mises}
  \varrho(\theta)=\frac{\rho\,\ee^{\kappa\cos2(\theta-\theta_0)}}{2\pi I_0(\kappa)},
\end{equation}
where $\theta_0$ is the angle of the director, $\kappa\ge0$ sets how sharply the rods are
aligned, and $I_n$ is the modified Bessel function \citeTODO{Bessel function}. Because the distribution depends on
$\theta$ only through $2\theta$, it gives $\p$ and $-\p$ the same weight, and all its odd
harmonics vanish. The even ones are
$\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\,\ee^{2\ii n\theta}=\rho\,m_n\ee^{2\ii n\theta_0}$, with the
Bessel ratios $m_n=I_n(\kappa)/I_0(\kappa)$. The first of them gives
$\Q=\rho\,m_1\hat\Q(\theta_0)$. With the strength of the nematic order
$S=\rho^{-1}\sqrt{(\Q\!:\!\Q)/2}$ of \cref{app:dean-normalisation}, $\kappa$ therefore
follows from $\Q$ through
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kappa-S}
  \frac{I_1(\kappa)}{I_0(\kappa)}=S .
\end{equation}
The left side increases monotonically from $0$ to $1$, so this equation has exactly one
root for every $S<1$.

The other moments follow from the same harmonics. Each is an isotropic tensor function
of $\Q$, so it can be built from the $\J$ of \cref{eq:J-definition} and three further
tensors,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:tensor-basis}
  T_{\abmn ij}(\Q) &= Q_\ab J_{\mn ij}+Q_\mn J_{\ab ij}+Q_{ij}J_\abmn,
  \nonumber\\
  \U(\Q) &= 2\bigl[\Q\Q-\tfrac12(\Q\!:\!\Q)\,\J\bigr],
  \nonumber\\
  \W(\Q) &= \Q\Q\Q-\tfrac14(\Q\!:\!\Q)\,\bT(\Q),
\end{align}
where $\Q\Q$ and $\Q\Q\Q$ are outer products, $(\Q\Q)_\abmn=Q_\ab Q_\mn$. The tensor $\bT$
is linear in $\Q$, while $\U$ and $\W$ are quadratic and cubic. The subtractions make
$\U$ and $\W$ totally symmetric and traceless, so they are pure fourth and sixth
harmonics. For $\Q=\hat\Q(\theta_0)$, for example, $U_{1111}=\cos4\theta_0$ and
$W_{111111}=\tfrac14\cos6\theta_0$. Integrating against \cref{eq:von-mises} then gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:exact-moments}
  \langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho &= \rho\,\J+\frac{g_2}{2\rho}\,\U(\Q),
  \nonumber\\
  \langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho &= 4\rho\,\J-\frac{2g_2}{\rho}\,\U(\Q),
  \nonumber\\
  \langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho &= \tfrac12\,\bT(\Q)+\frac{g_3}{\rho^2}\,\W(\Q),
\end{align}
with the coefficients
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:g-coefficients}
  g_2=\frac{m_2}{m_1^2}=\frac{I_0I_2}{I_1^2},
  \qquad
  g_3=\frac{m_3}{m_1^3}=\frac{I_0^2I_3}{I_1^3},
\end{equation}
both functions of $S$ through \cref{eq:kappa-S}. The coefficients of $\J$ and $\bT$ are
pure numbers. The shape of the distribution therefore enters only through $g_2$ and
$g_3$, and the single function $g_2$ controls both second moments.

The exact moments are not polynomials in $\Q$, which makes them awkward in the field
equations, but at weak order they simplify. Expanding the Bessel functions at small
$\kappa$ and eliminating $\kappa$ with \cref{eq:kappa-S} gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:g-small-S}
  g_2=\tfrac12+\tfrac16S^2+O(S^4),
  \qquad
  g_3=\tfrac16+\tfrac18S^2+O(S^4).
\end{equation}
Since $\U=O(S^2)$ and $\W=O(S^3)$, while the leading terms are of order $1$
and $S$, the $\U$ and $\W$ terms of \cref{eq:exact-moments} are smaller than the
leading ones by a relative $O(S^2)$. Dropping them leaves the lowest-order closure
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:leading-closure}
  &\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho\simeq\rho\,\J,
  \quad
  \langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho\simeq4\rho\,\J,
  \quad \langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho\simeq\tfrac12\,\bT(\Q).
\end{align}
The first two close \cref{eq:Q-full} into \cref{eq:Q-closed}, and hence give
\cref{eq:model-Q}. All three, inserted into \cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators}, give the
lowest-order noise correlators. The defect, however, is not weakly ordered. At $S=0.9$
the coefficients are $g_2=0.82$ and $g_3=0.55$, against the small-$S$ values $1/2$ and
$1/6$, so the dropped terms are not small there. The lowest-order closure is
therefore not accurate around the defect. We nevertheless keep the closed equation
\cref{eq:model-Q}, as continuum theories derived from particle models
usually do \cite{baskaran2008hydrodynamics,bertin2013,grossmann2016mesoscale,peshkov2012nonlinear}. The noise
correlators are a different matter, because there the closure produces covariance
matrices with a negative eigenvalue, which makes the stochastic equations ill defined.
\Cref{app:realizability} shows this and builds the noise from the exact moments instead.

\section{Solution of the noiseless density equation}
\label{app:density-poisson}
% dean.tex 820-903. Written 2026-10-02.
For a $+1/2$ defect the density equation of \cref{eq:simplified-noiseless} can be
integrated at once, because its source is itself a Laplacian, whatever the profile of the
nematic order. That profile follows from $\Ha=0$, so we find it first. We use polar
coordinates $(r,\phi)$ centred on the core, with $\phi$ measured from the $+x$ axis. At a
$+1/2$ defect the director makes the angle $\theta=\phi/2$ with this axis, so it turns by
half a turn around the core, and the ansatz of \cref{eq:pade-approximation} is
$\Q=S(r)\hat\Q(\phi/2)$. Since the components of $\hat\Q(\theta)$ are $\cos2\theta$ and
$\sin2\theta$, this ansatz has the components $Q_{xx}=S\cos\phi$ and
$Q_{xy}=S\sin\phi$. Both are angular harmonics of order one, on which the Laplacian acts
as $S''+S'/r-S/r^2$, so $\nabla^2\Q=(S''+S'/r-S/r^2)\,\hat\Q(\phi/2)$. At the same time
$\Q\!:\!\Q=2S^2$ turns the cubic term of \cref{eq:H-active} into $2bS^3\hat\Q(\phi/2)$.
With $S_0$ and $\elld$ of \cref{eq:S0-elld}, the condition $\Ha=0$ therefore reduces to the
radial equation
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:S-radial}
  S''+\frac{S'}{r}-\frac{S}{r^2}=\frac{1}{\elld^2}\Bigl(\frac{S^2}{S_0^2}-1\Bigr)S,
\end{align}
with $S(0)=0$ at the core and $S\to S_0$ far from it. \Cref{eq:S-radial} has no
closed-form solution, which is why \cref{sec:defect-steady} uses the Pad\'e profile. Far
from the core the equation gives $S\simeq S_0(1-\elld^2/2r^2)$, and the profile
\cref{eq:pade-approximation} has the same far field. It departs from the numerical
solution of \cref{eq:S-radial} by less than $10^{-2}\,S_0$, with the largest departure near
$r\simeq1.5\,\elld$. A higher-order approximant, rational in $x^2$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pade-accurate}
  f(x)=x\left[\tfrac{0.34011+0.04517\,x^2+0.00234\,x^4}
  {1+0.38387\,x^2+0.04751\,x^4+0.00234\,x^6}\right]^{1/2}\!\!\!,
\end{equation}
departs from it by less than $10^{-4}S_0$, a hundred times less. The difference matters
only where a small modulation is quoted to a few percent. With
\cref{eq:pade-accurate}, as with the exact profile, \cref{eq:total-gauss} gives
$\nu_0=2.94\%$ at $\elln=0.5~\micron$, whereas \cref{eq:pade-approximation} gives
$3.06\%$, because its $S$ rises slightly too fast near the core. We therefore quote
$\nu_0$ for the exact profile and use \cref{eq:pade-approximation} everywhere else for simplicity, where
its error is small against the effects we discuss.

With $\Q$ known, the first equation of \cref{eq:simplified-noiseless} reads
$B\nabla^2\rho=\nabla\tcdot\fa$. For the defect its source is simple, because the active
force \cref{eq:active-force-defect} points along $x$ and depends on $r$ alone. Thus
$\nabla\tcdot\fa=\partial_xf^\text{a}_x=-\zeta\,(S'+S/r)'\cos\phi$. The radial factor
$(S'+S/r)'$ equals $S''+S'/r-S/r^2$, the operator of \cref{eq:S-radial}, so
$\nabla\tcdot\fa=-\zeta\nabla^2(S\cos\phi)$, and the density obeys
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-harmonic}
  \nabla^2\Bigl[\rho+\frac{\zeta}{B}\,S(r)\cos\phi\Bigr]=0 .
\end{align}
We therefore write the density as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rho-h}
  \rho(r,\phi)=1-\frac{\zeta}{B}\,S(r)\cos\phi+h(r,\phi),\qquad \nabla^2h=0,
\end{equation}
with $h$ regular inside the wall, $r\le R$, and let the wall condition fix $h$. Every
condition below is proportional to $\cos\phi$, and the only regular harmonic functions
with this angular dependence are $r\cos\phi$ and constants, so $h=c+A\,r\cos\phi$.

The reservoir wall of \cref{sec:defect-steady}, $\rho(R,\phi)=1$, requires
$h(R,\phi)=(\zeta/B)S(R)\cos\phi$. This gives $c=0$ and $A=(\zeta/B)S(R)/R$, which is
\cref{eq:rho-analytic}. Since $S(0)=0$ and $\cos\phi$ averages to zero over the disk, the
density at the core and the mean density are then both one.

A reservoir lets cells cross the wall. One might instead leave the density free at the
wall, $\partial_r\rho=0$, but this condition is inconsistent. It exerts no pressure
gradient, so the active flux $\fa/\xi_0$ still crosses the wall, and once the
cross-coupling of \cref{app:comoving} breaks the antisymmetry of the defect, the inflow
and the outflow no longer cancel. Cells then enter or leave the box, and no steady state
is reached.

A wall that blocks the cells imposes zero normal flux,
$\nh\tcdot(B\nabla\rho-\fa)=0$, that is $B\,\partial_r\rho=f^\text{a}_r$ at $r=R$, with
the radial active force $f^\text{a}_r=-\zeta(S'+S/r)\cos\phi$. This requires
$A=-(\zeta/B)S(R)/R$, while the number of cells is conserved, so the mean density
stays one and $c=0$. Thus
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-noflux}
  \rho(r,\phi)=1-\frac{\zeta}{B}\Bigl[S(r)+\frac rR\,S(R)\Bigr]\cos\phi .
\end{align}
The cells that the active force drives against this wall pile up there, so the linear
term has the slope of the reservoir wall with the opposite sign, and the modulation at the
wall doubles. \Cref{fig:rho1d-dirichlet-neumann} compares the two walls in the
numerical solution. They change the density across the whole box, but under both walls it
stays of order $\zeta/B$, far below the scale of its fluctuations.

\section{Solving noiseless equations in comoving frame}
\label{app:comoving}
% dean.tex 2460-2597.
Our target is the fluctuations around the steady state, but with the cross-coupling the
defect has no steady state in the laboratory frame, because it moves
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}). We therefore have to compute its velocity $\u$ together with
the steady fields, and linearize the equations in the frame that moves with the core,
where the defect is at rest. Here we obtain $\rho_\ss$, $\Q_\ss$ and $\u$ in that
comoving frame.

Dropping the $\nabla\H$ terms as in \cref{sec:model-closure}, dropping all the noise
terms, and substituting
$\partial_t\to\partial_t-(\u\tcdot\nabla)$ in \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q}, we solve the following equations
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:comoving-equations}
\begin{align}
    0 ={}& \u\tcdot\nabla\rho + \frac1{\xi_0}\nabla\tcdot
    \bigl(B\nabla\rho - \fa\bigr),
    \\
    0 ={}& \nabla\tcdot\Bigl[\Q\Bigl(\u+\frac B{\xi_0}\nabla\rho\Bigr)\Bigr]
    + \frac4{\xir}\Ha .
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

We drop the advection term $\u\tcdot\nabla\rho$ from the density equation. At large $B$
the pressure gradient $B\nabla\rho$ balances $\fa$, so $\nabla\rho\propto1/B$. At distance
$r$ from the core, the dropped term is smaller than the flux divergence by
$\xi_0ur/B$, which is at most $3\times10^{-4}$ at the wall. $B$ then drops out of the problem: $\Q_\ss$ and $\u$ do not depend on
$B$, and $\rho_\ss-1\propto1/B$ (see \cref{fig:rho1d-dirichlet,fig:rho2d}).


The frame velocity $\u$ is not available in closed form. It is fixed implicitly, by
requiring that the defect core stay pinned at the origin, and enters the numerical
problem as the Lagrange multiplier conjugate to that constraint.

We solve in the square box of side $40~\micron$, and
impose on its walls the density condition of \cref{sec:defect-steady}, the reservoir
$\rho=1$.

For the nematic order we impose
$\nh\tcdot\nabla\Q=0$, so that the wall does not force any nematic axis.
We impose the shape of the defect only by pinning the core, $Q_1(0,0)=Q_2(0,0)=0$, and
by setting $Q_2(x,0)=0$ on the axis, with $x Q_1(x,0)>0$. These constraints turn
out to be enough to keep the solution a $+1/2$ defect.

The fields vary fastest near the core, where $S$ rises over the length $\elld$, and
slowly far from it. We therefore solve \cref{eq:comoving-equations} by finite differences
on a graded grid, which is finer near the core and coarser far from it. The unknowns are
$\rho$, $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ at every node, together with $u_x$ and $u_y$.

With the cross-coupling the core cannot stay at rest, so the multiplier $\u$ is nonzero.
It points along $+x$, as \cref{sec:defect-steady} explains. In our box
$u_x=0.24~\micron/\minute$, while $u_y=7\times10^{-12}~\micron/\minute$ vanishes to
round-off, as the mirror symmetry requires. The value of $u_x$ depends on the box size and
on the walls. The coupling also deforms the defect: the deformation is weak near the core, but away
from it the solution deviates from the Pad\'e profile used in \cref{sec:defect-steady}.
This can be seen in \cref{fig:strength,fig:defectangle,fig:nematicaxis}. The active
force is deformed too: the cross-coupling and the motion of the defect shift it along the
axis, away from \cref{eq:active-force-defect} (\cref{fig:activeforce}).

\section{Density variance with a short-length cutoff}
\label{app:variance}
% dean.tex 904-1211 ("Only density fluctuations"). Supports \cref{sec:defect-fluct}.
% Written 2026-10-02. Checks: claude_experiments/appF_check.wls (transforms, asymptotes,
% pedestal, inversion), fit_new.py (nu0 vs l_n at S0 = 0.9, l_d = 1), appF_corr.wls
% (lagged and two-point correlators with the Q_ss term).
The layering probability depends exponentially on the equal-point density variance,
so the few percent by which the defect modulates that variance decide the result of
\cref{sec:nucleation-2}. Here we derive the variance from \cref{eq:rho-fourier} at
$\delta\Q=0$, together with the correlation time and length that \cref{sec:defect-fluct}
quotes. Two features of the calculation need care: the variance of a continuum diverges,
so it depends on a short-length cutoff, and the nematic order makes the noise
anisotropic.

We take the Fourier conventions $\tilde f(\k)=\int\!\d^2\r\,\ee^{-\ii\k\cdot\r}f(\r)$
in space and $\tilde f(\omega)=\int\!\d t\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t}f(t)$ in time.
Taking the Fourier transform of \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss} over time and space, we get
\begin{multline}
  \la\tilde \eta^\rho_\alpha(\k,\omega)\tilde \eta^\rho_\beta(\k',\omega')\ra = \frac{4\pi\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}\,\delta(\omega+\omega')\,\times
  \\
  \times
  \left[(2\pi)^2 \delta_\ab \delta(\k+\k')\;+\;\tilde Q^\ss_\ab(\k+\k')\right].
\end{multline}
Then, with \cref{eq:rho-fourier}, we get
\begin{multline}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,\omega)\delta\tilde\rho(\k',\omega') \ra= \frac{4\pi\zeta\,\delta(\omega+\omega')}{\xi_0\rho_0\,[\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2]\![-\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k'^2]}\times
  \\
  \times
  \!\left[(2\pi)^2 k^2\,\delta(\k+\k')\;-\;k_\alpha k'_\beta\,\tilde Q^\ss_\ab(\k+\k')\right]
\end{multline}
Inverse-transforming to $(t,t')$ and integrating the $\delta(\omega+\omega')$ collapses one $\omega$-integral. Partial fractions on the remaining $\omega$-denominator,
\begin{multline}
    \frac{1}{[\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2]\,[-\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k'^2]} = 
    \\
    =\frac{1}{\gamma_0(k^2+k'^2)}\!\left[\frac{1}{\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2}\;+\;\frac{1}{-\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k'^2}\right],
\end{multline}
then we apply the  identity $\int\!\frac{\d\omega}{2\pi}\ee^{\ii\omega t}/(\ii\omega+a) = \theta(t)\ee^{-at}$ on each term. Thus we obtain $\la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,t)\delta\tilde\rho(\k',t') \ra$.
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,t)\delta\tilde\rho(\k',t')\ra = \frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\,\gamma_0(k^2+k'^2)}\times
  \\
  \times \left[\theta(t-t')\ee^{-\gamma_0 k^2(t-t')}+\theta(t'-t)\ee^{-\gamma_0 k'^2(t'-t)}\right]
  \\
  \times\!\left[(2\pi)^2 k^2\delta(\k+\k') - k_ik'_j\tilde\Q^\ss_{ij}(\k+\k')\right].
\end{multline}

At equal times, $t=t'$, the support of $\delta(\k+\k')$ sets $k^2=(k^2+k'^2)/2$ in the
first term, and the spectrum becomes
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-spectrum}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k)\,\delta\tilde\rho(\k')\ra
  = \frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\Bigl[{}&(2\pi)^2\delta(\k+\k')
  \nonumber\\
  &-\frac{2k_ik'_j\,\tilde Q^\ss_{ij}(\k+\k')}{k^2+k'^2}\Bigr].
\end{align}
The nematic field enters only through the second term. Before inverting over space for a
general nematic field, we consider two simple cases, no nematic order and uniform nematic
order.

\subsection{Isotropic case}
\label{app:variance-iso}
Without nematic order, $\Q_\ss=0$, only the first term of
\cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln} remains. On the support of
$\delta(\k+\k')$ we have $k'^2=k^2$, so the two exponentials combine into one, and
\begin{equation}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,t)\,\delta\tilde\rho(\k',t')\ra=(2\pi)^2\delta(\k+\k')\,
  \frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\,\ee^{-\gamma_0k^2|t-t'|}.
\end{equation}
Inverting over space gives a heat kernel,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rho-correlation-no-cutoff}
  \la\delta\rho(\r,t)\,\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\,
  \frac{\ee^{-|\r-\r'|^2/(4\gamma_0|t-t'|)}}{4\pi\gamma_0|t-t'|},
\end{equation}
and as $t'\to t$ it tends to $(\zeta/\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0)\,\delta(\r-\r')$. At equal times the density at two different points is
therefore uncorrelated, and the variance at one point diverges.

\subsection{The case with uniform nematic order}
\label{app:variance-uniform}
Let the nematic order be uniform, with strength $S$ and the director along $x$, so
that $Q^\ss_{xx}=-Q^\ss_{yy}=S$ and $Q^\ss_{xy}=0$. Its transform is
$\tilde\Q_\ss(\bs p)=(2\pi)^2\delta(\bs p)\,\Q_\ss$, so the second term of
\cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln} sits on $\k'=-\k$ as well, where it
becomes $(2\pi)^2\delta(\k+\k')\,(k_x^2-k_y^2)S$. Both terms then sit on $\k'=-\k$, where
$k'^2=k^2$, so the two exponentials combine into $\ee^{-\gamma_0k^2|t-t'|}$ and
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:rho-spectrum-uniform}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,t)\,\delta\tilde\rho(\k',t')\ra
  =(2\pi)^2\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\,\delta(\k+\k')\times
  \\
  \times\bigl[1+S\cos2\psi\bigr]\,\ee^{-\gamma_0k^2|t-t'|},
\end{multline}
with $\psi$ the angle between $\k$ and the director ($x$ axis). Uniform order therefore keeps the
spectrum diagonal in $\k$ and only multiplies it by the factor $1+S\cos2\psi$. Since
$\cos2\psi$ averages to zero over the directions of $\k$, this factor averages to one. A correlation taken at a single point integrates the
spectrum over all directions, so it does not see the order: the autocorrelation in time at
a fixed point is exactly the isotropic
$(\zeta/\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0)/(4\pi\gamma_0|t-t'|)$, for every time difference $t-t'$. The
equal-point variance is therefore that of the isotropic case as well.

Two distinct points, however, weight the directions of $\k$ unequally. Let $\r'-\r$
make the angle $\alpha$ with the director. The angular integral of
$\ee^{\ii\k\cdot(\r'-\r)}\cos2\psi$ gives $-2\pi J_2(k|\r'-\r|)\cos2\alpha$, and the
remaining radial integrals are elementary,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:corr-uniform}
  &\la\delta\rho(\r,t)\,\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\,\frac{1}{4\pi\gamma_0|t-t'|}
  \Bigl[\ee^{-u}-S\cos2\alpha\,g(u)\Bigr],
  \nonumber\\
  &g(u)=\frac{1-(1+u)\,\ee^{-u}}{u},\qquad u=\frac{|\r-\r'|^2}{4\gamma_0|t-t'|}.
\end{align}
The first term is the isotropic heat kernel. The second vanishes at $\r'=\r$, because
$g(u)\simeq u/2$ at small $u$, but at large separation, $|\r-\r'|^2\gg4\gamma_0|t-t'|$,
$g(u)\simeq1/u$, so it decays only
as $-\zeta S\cos2\alpha/(\pi\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0|\r-\r'|^2)$. This tail does not depend on the
time difference $|t-t'|$, and it comes from $k\to0$, where the direction factor is not analytic and the modes relax
arbitrarily slowly. Such power-law correlations are generic when the noise and the
relaxation of a conserved density have different
anisotropies~\cite{grinstein1990conservation,garrido1990longrange,mahdisoltani2021longrange}. At equal times the heat kernel collapses to a $\delta$ function and
only the tail is left at finite separation,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:corr-uniform-equal-time}
  \la\delta\rho(\r)\,\delta\rho(\r+\bs d)\ra
  =\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\Bigl[\delta(\bs d)-\frac{S\cos2\alpha}{\pi d^2}\Bigr].
\end{equation}
So the isotropic case leaves two points uncorrelated at equal times, while uniform order
anticorrelates them along the director and correlates them across it.

\subsection{Short-length cutoff}
\label{app:variance-cutoff}

As we already mentioned below \cref{eq:rho-correlation-no-cutoff}, at $t=t'$, the density autocorrelation is proportional to $\delta(\r-\r')$, or in other words, equal-point variance is infinite.
It comes from the flat isotropic spectrum, whose integral over all $\k$ is infinite.
The large wavevectors are worse for the anisotropic part of the density autocorrelation in  \cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln}, since the anisotropic part of the equal time variance 
grows only logarithmically as we consider larger and larger wavevectors
(\cref{app:variance-ln}), much more slowly than the isotropic part.
Therefore, 
without a cutoff
the modulation by the defect vanishes.

The solution is to introduce a short-length (or long-wavelength) cutoff. We implement this by suppressing long wavevectors in fourier space with Gaussian filter:
\begin{equation}
  \delta\tilde\rho(\k,\omega) =
  -\frac{\ii\,\k\cdot\tilde \bEta_\rho(\k,\omega)}{\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2} W(\k)
  \qquad W(\k)=\ee^{-k^2\elln^2/4}
\end{equation}
We choose Gaussian filder because it keeps the integrals below Gaussian. The $1/4$ prefactor in the exponent is a choice: this makes density autocorrelation length in the isotropic case equal to $\elln$, as we will see below.


The correlator \cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln} thus carries the factor
$\ee^{-(k^2+k'^2)\elln^2/4}$, which on the support of $\delta(\k+\k')$ becomes
$\ee^{-k^2\elln^2/2}$. The isotropic spectrum of \cref{app:variance-iso} then integrates to
a finite equal-point variance,
\begin{equation}
  \sigmarho=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\int\!\frac{\d^2\k}{(2\pi)^2}\,
  \ee^{-k^2\elln^2/2}=\frac{\zeta}{2\pi\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0\elln^2},
\end{equation}
which with $\xi_0\gamma_0=B$ is the pedestal \cref{eq:pedestal}. The filter $W$ depends on
$|\k|$ alone, so the angular argument of \cref{app:variance-uniform} still holds: uniform
nematic order leaves the pedestal unchanged, and the autocorrelation in time at a point
as well.

At unequal times the isotropic spectrum carries the relaxation factor
$\ee^{-\gamma_0k^2|t-t'|}$ besides the smoothing, and inverting over space gives
\begin{equation}
  \la\delta\rho(\r,t)\,\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}
  \int\!\frac{\d^2\k}{(2\pi)^2}\,\ee^{\ii\k\cdot(\r-\r')}\,\ee^{-a k^2},
\end{equation}
with $a=\gamma_0|t-t'|+\elln^2/2$. Relaxation over the time $|t-t'|$ therefore acts on the
correlator like a wider patch. The integral is Gaussian,
\begin{equation}
  \int\!\frac{\d^2\k}{(2\pi)^2}\,\ee^{\ii\k\cdot\bs d-ak^2}=\frac{\ee^{-d^2/4a}}{4\pi a},
\end{equation}
so the correlator is a Gaussian in $\r-\r'$ with variance $2a=\elln^2+2\gamma_0|t-t'|$
along each axis, and its normalization $1/4\pi a$ falls by the same factor as this
variance grows. Substituting $a$ and $\xi_0\gamma_0=B$ gives
\cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic}. At equal times its width is the correlation length
$\elln$, and its normalization has halved after the correlation time $\tau_c$ of
\cref{eq:tau-c}.

\subsection{The anisotropic piece}
\label{app:variance-aniso}
With the cutoff, the equal-point variance is the integral of
\cref{eq:rho-spectrum} over $\k$ and $\k'$, weighted by
$\ee^{\ii(\k+\k')\cdot\r}\ee^{-(k^2+k'^2)\elln^2/4}$. Its second term depends on the sum
$\bs p=\k+\k'$, which is conjugate to the slow variation of $\Q_\ss$, and on the internal
wavevector $\bs q=(\k-\k')/2$. We therefore change variables to
\begin{equation}
  \k=\tfrac12\bs p+\bs q,\qquad\k'=\tfrac12\bs p-\bs q,
\end{equation}
which has unit Jacobian, $\d^2\k\,\d^2\k'=\d^2\bs p\,\d^2\bs q$. In these variables
\begin{align}
  k_ik'_j&=\tfrac14p_ip_j-q_iq_j+\tfrac12(q_ip_j-p_iq_j),
  \nonumber\\
  k^2+k'^2&=\tfrac12p^2+2q^2 .
\end{align}
The last term of $k_ik'_j$ is antisymmetric and drops against the symmetric
$\tilde\Q_\ss$, and the smoothing factor splits into
$\ee^{-z}\ee^{-q^2\elln^2/2}$ with $z=p^2\elln^2/8$. The anisotropic
part of the variance thus reads
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:aniso-pq}
  \sigmarho(\r)-\sigmarho\big|_\text{pedestal}
  =-\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\int\!\frac{\d^2\bs p}{(2\pi)^2}\,\ee^{\ii\bs p\tcdot\r}\,
  \ee^{-z}\,\tilde Q^\ss_{ij}(\bs p)
  \\
  \times\int\!\frac{\d^2\bs q}{(2\pi)^2}\,
  \frac{\tfrac14p_ip_j-q_iq_j}{p^2/2+2q^2}\,\ee^{-q^2\elln^2/2}.
\end{multline}
The smoothing therefore suppresses the internal wavevector and touches $\bs p$ only
through the overall factor $\ee^{-z}$. In the inner integral the rest of the integrand
depends only on $q=|\bs q|$, so $q_iq_j$ averages over the directions of $\bs q$ to
$\tfrac12q^2\delta_{ij}$, and $\delta_{ij}$ vanishes against the traceless
$\tilde\Q_\ss$. Only the $p_ip_j$ part remains, and it leaves an elementary integral,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:aniso-inner}
  \int\!\frac{\d^2\bs q}{(2\pi)^2}\,\frac{\ee^{-q^2\elln^2/2}}{p^2/2+2q^2}
  =\frac{\ee^{z}E_1(z)}{8\pi},
\end{equation}
with $E_1$ the exponential integral. Its $\ee^{z}$ cancels the external
$\ee^{-z}$ exactly. Since $p_ip_j\tilde Q^\ss_{ij}$ is minus the Fourier
transform of $\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}$, the anisotropic variance is the double
divergence of the nematic field passed through a filter,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:aniso-kernel}
  &\sigmarho(\r)-\sigmarho\big|_\text{pedestal}
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{\zeta}{8\pi\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\!\int\!\frac{\d^2\bs p}{(2\pi)^2}\,
  \ee^{\ii\bs p\tcdot\r}\,\hat\Phi(p)\,\widetilde{\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}}(\bs p),
  \nonumber\\
  &\hat\Phi(p)=\tfrac12E_1\bigl(p^2\elln^2/8\bigr).
\end{align}
No expansion and no fitted scale enter it. The inverse transform of $\hat\Phi$ is the
kernel $\kappa$ of \cref{eq:kappa-real}, and dividing by the pedestal gives
\cref{eq:total-gauss}.

The filter interpolates between two branches,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:Phi-asymptotics}
  \hat\Phi(p)&\simeq\ln\frac1{p\elln}+\frac{3\ln2-\gamma_\mathrm{E}}2
  \quad (p\elln\ll1),
  \nonumber\\
  \hat\Phi(p)&\simeq\frac4{p^2\elln^2}\,\ee^{-p^2\elln^2/8}
  \quad (p\elln\gg1),
\end{align}
with $\gamma_\mathrm{E}$ Euler's constant. Variations of the nematic field on scales
larger than $\elln$ are therefore weighted logarithmically, and those below $\elln$
are cut off exponentially. In real space the same two branches appear as the shape of
$\kappa$: it is finite at the origin, $\kappa(0)=1/(\pi\elln^2)$, and beyond $\elln$ it
falls off as $1/(2\pi w^2)$, whose integral produces the logarithm.

\subsection{Dependence on the cutoff}
\label{app:variance-ln}
When $\elln$ is much smaller than the core size $\elld$, the nematic field varies only
on scales of $\elld$ and larger, so its transform is supported at $p\lesssim1/\elld$,
where the filter takes its logarithmic branch. The anisotropic term then reduces to
$(\zeta/8\pi\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0)\,\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}\ln(\elld/\elln)$,
plus a term of order one that depends on the shape of the profile. Relative to the
pedestal the modulation therefore grows as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:nu0-asymptote}
  \nu_0\simeq\elln^2\Bigl[\tfrac14\max\bigl|\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}\bigr|
  \ln\frac{\elld}{\elln}+c\Bigr]
\end{equation}
at small $\elln$, where the constant $c$ depends on the shape of the profile, and it
vanishes as $\elln\to0$. Because of the logarithm, $\nu_0$ falls more slowly than
$\elln^2$. At the cutoff of \cref{eq:cutoff}, however, $\elld/\elln$ is only $2$, so the
logarithm is of order one and comparable to the constant $c$. The asymptote is then not
accurate enough, and we evaluate \cref{eq:total-gauss} in full. \Cref{fig:ln_curve} shows
the result, with $\nu_0=2.94\%$ at $\elln=0.5~\micron$. As $\elln$ grows, the peak of the
modulation also moves away from the core, because the filter smooths the nematic field over
a larger region.

\section{Lyapunov solver for the coupled fluctuations}
\label{app:lyapunov}

% dean.tex 1521-2089.
The analytic variance \cref{eq:total-gauss} rests on $\delta\Q=0$, which holds only if
the active force $\zeta\nabla\tcdot\delta\Q$ is small against the density noise
(\cref{sec:defect-fluct}). To test this, and to obtain the variance without the
approximation, we solve the linearized equations \cref{eq:linearized} numerically for the
equal-time covariance of $(\delta\rho,\delta\Q)$ on the steady background of
\cref{app:comoving}. The solver implements the cutoff \cref{eq:cutoff} by
giving the noise a spatial correlation, chosen to reproduce the pedestal
\cref{eq:pedestal} (\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff}).

\Cref{app:lyapunov-method} turns the linearized equations into a Lyapunov equation for the
covariance and states when it has a valid solution. The next three subsections make the
discretized noise satisfy these conditions and reproduce the continuum: the noise covariance
needs the exact moments of the closure to be positive semi-definite
(\cref{app:realizability}), the drift and the noise need matching difference operators
(\cref{app:lyapunov-operators}), and the spatial correlation of the noise, which imposes the cutoff, needs a
calibration to the grid (\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff}). \Cref{app:lyapunov-walls} gives the
boundary conditions, and \cref{app:lyapunov-results} compares the solution with the analytic
variance and shows the fluctuations of the active force.

\subsection{Method}
\label{app:lyapunov-method}
% Written 2026-10-02 from dean.tex 1521-2047 and lyapunov_solver/ (lyapunov_solver.m,
% lyap_solve.py). Numbers: claude_experiments/appG_numbers.wls, appG_force.wls, and the
% job log jobscripts/logs/1773058_2.out (N150, B = 9e4).
The linearized equations \cref{eq:linearized} are a linear Ornstein--Uhlenbeck field
theory for $\psi=(\delta\rho,\delta Q_1,\delta Q_2)$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ou-model}
  \partial_t\psi=\A\psi+\bxi,
  \qquad
  \la\bxi(\r,t)\,\bxi(\r',t')^\T\ra=\mathbf N(\r,\r')\,\delta(t-t'),
\end{equation}
where $\A$ and $\mathbf N$ are linear operators on fields. $\A$ collects the drift of
\cref{eq:linearized}, including its derivatives, and $\mathbf N$ is the covariance of its
noise terms, which contains derivatives as well, because the flux noises $\bEta_\rho$ and
$\bEta^\Q$ enter through their divergence. The flux correlators are
\cref{eq:eta-rho-ss,eq:Q-noise-correlators}, evaluated with the exact moments of
\cref{app:realizability} to ensure covariance positivity. Because the noise is white in time, $\psi$ is a Markov process.
It\^o's lemma applied to $\psi\psi^\T$ then turns stationarity into an algebraic
condition on the equal-time covariance
$\mathbf C(\r,\r')=\la\psi(\r,t)\,\psi(\r',t)^\T\ra$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:lyapunov}
  \A\,\mathbf C+\mathbf C\,\A^\T+\mathbf N=0,
\end{equation}
with $\A$ acting on the first argument of $\mathbf C$ and $\A^\T$ on the second. Only the
delta function in time is needed for this step. The spatial form of $\mathbf N$ is free,
which is what lets \cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff} give the noise a finite correlation
length. \Cref{eq:lyapunov} is the Lyapunov equation of control theory, where it tests
stability: a linear system $\partial_t\psi=\A\psi$ is asymptotically stable exactly when, for
a positive definite $\mathbf N$, the equation has a positive definite solution
\cite{lyapunov1992general,zhou1996robust}. This solution,
$\mathbf C=\int_0^\infty\!\d t\,\ee^{\A t}\,\mathbf N\,\ee^{\A^\T t}$, adds up the noise
injected at every earlier time and carried to the present by the drift
\cite{zhou1996robust,simoncini2016computational}.

On a grid, \cref{eq:lyapunov} becomes a matrix equation. It has a unique positive
semi-definite solution if two conditions hold:
($i$)~every eigenvalue of the discretized $\A$ has a negative real part, so that the steady
state is linearly stable;
($ii$)~the discretized $\mathbf N$ is positive semi-definite, so that it is a covariance at
all.

We use a uniform grid of $N_\mathrm{int}=150$ interior nodes per direction on
$[-20,20]^2~\micron^2$, with spacing $\Delta x=40/151~\micron=0.265~\micron$, so the state
vector has $3N_\mathrm{int}^2=67\,500$ entries. Two-dimensional
operators are Kronecker products of one-dimensional ones.
The covariance is then a dense matrix, and we solve \cref{eq:lyapunov} directly. A real
Schur decomposition of the discretized $\A$ is followed by a recursive Bartels--Stewart
back-substitution \cite{bartels1972solution}, which keeps most of the work in matrix
products. The Schur decomposition also yields the spectrum of $\A$, which checks
condition ($i$): in our run every eigenvalue has a negative real part.

\subsection{Realizability of the noise covariance}
\label{app:realizability}
% Moved here from the closure appendix (2026-10-05); only the opening and the references
% to the run were rewritten.
The noise of the coupled equations comes from the angular distribution of the rods, through
the moments that the closure of \cref{app:closure} assigns to it. A covariance must be
positive semi-definite, and the lowest-order closure keeps it so only for weak order,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:realizability-condition}
  S\le S_\star \simeq 0.618,
\end{equation}
whereas around the defect the nematic order reaches $S_0=0.9$. We therefore build the
noise from the exact moments of the distribution, which give a valid covariance at every
$S$.

Condition ($ii$) is not automatic. The noise covariance $\mathbf N$ is assembled from the
local covariance of the noise fluxes at each point (\cref{app:lyapunov-operators}), so it is
positive semi-definite only if that local covariance is. A variance cannot be negative: if
some combination of the noises had a negative variance, no noise could have the
correlators \cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators}, and the stochastic equations
\cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} would be ill defined. For any genuine angular distribution
the local covariance is valid automatically, but for the lowest-order closure
\cref{eq:leading-closure} of \cref{app:closure} it is not.

We collect the three flux noises at a point, $\eta^\rho_i$, $\eta^Q_{11i}$ and
$\eta^Q_{12i}$, into a vector with six components $(a,i)$. Each is an angular integral of
the reversal noise, $\int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,u_{(ai)}(\theta)\,k$, with the
weights
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:u-vector}
  \bs u(\theta)=\bigl(\p,\ \hat Q_{11}\p,\ \hat Q_{12}\p\bigr).
\end{equation}
By \cref{eq:dean-noise-sum}, their covariance is $\bs M\Delta'$, where $\Delta'=\delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t')$ as in
\cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators}, with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gram-matrix}
  \bs M=\frac{4\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,\bs u\,\bs u^\T .
\end{equation}
This $6\times6$ matrix holds the density correlator \cref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation} and
the first two correlators of \cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators}. Since the weight $\varrho_t$ is
non-negative, $\bs M$ is a Gram matrix, and
$\bs v^\T\bs M\bs v=(4\zeta/\xi_0\rho_0)\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,(\bs v\tcdot\bs u)^2\ge0$
for every $\bs v$. The covariance of $\f^Q$ is a Gram matrix in the same way. Every
genuine distribution, and in particular the von Mises distribution with the exact
moments \cref{eq:exact-moments}, therefore gives a valid covariance.

There is no margin, however. Since $\hat\Q\tcdot\p=\p$ for every $\theta$, the fluxes
obey the two exact relations $\eta^Q_{\alpha\beta\beta}=\eta^\rho_\alpha$, so $\bs M$ has
two zero eigenvalues whatever the distribution. Equivalently, the six components of
$\bs u$ span only the four functions $\cos\theta$, $\sin\theta$, $\cos3\theta$ and
$\sin3\theta$. A covariance built from approximate moments can move these two
eigenvalues in either direction, and a negative eigenvalue makes it invalid.

This is what happens with the lowest-order closure. The products $u_{(ai)}u_{(bj)}$
contain only the harmonics $n=0,2,4,6$, so $\bs M$ depends on the distribution only
through $\rho$ and the complex harmonics
$z_n=\rho^{-1}\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\,\ee^{\ii n\theta}$ with $n=2,4,6$. The closure
\cref{eq:leading-closure} keeps $z_2=S\ee^{2\ii\theta_0}$, which $\Q$ fixes, and sets
$z_4=0$ through $\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho=\rho\J$ and $z_6=0$ through
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho=\tfrac12\bT$. Its $\bs M$ is valid only if some
non-negative distribution has these harmonics, which is a truncated trigonometric moment
problem. Once the phase of $z_2$ is rotated away, such a distribution exists if and only
if the Toeplitz matrix of the harmonics,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:toeplitz}
  \mathcal T=\begin{pmatrix}1&S&0&0\\S&1&S&0\\0&S&1&S\\0&0&S&1\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
is positive semi-definite \cite{grenander1958toeplitz}. Its eigenvalues are
$1+2S\cos(k\pi/5)$ with $k=1,\dots,4$, and the smallest, $1-2S\cos(\pi/5)$, turns
negative above
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:golden-bound}
  S_\star=\frac1{2\cos(\pi/5)}=\frac{\sqrt5-1}2\simeq0.618 .
\end{equation}
Above $S_\star$, no non-negative distribution has the moments of the closure, and its
$\bs M$ acquires a negative eigenvalue. We checked numerically that the smallest
eigenvalue of $\bs M$ changes sign exactly at $S_\star$, so the bound is sharp.

The defect steady state lies far above this bound. In the steady state of
\cref{app:comoving}, $S$ rises from $0.10$ next to the core to $0.90$ far from it,
and it exceeds $S_\star$ everywhere except within about $1.5~\micron$ of the core. Keeping the next lowest order term in \cref{eq:exact-moments} raises the bound only to $S\le0.775$. The lowest-order closure therefore gives no valid noise covariance for this defect.

The analytic calculation of \cref{sec:defect-fluct} avoids the problem, because it
sets $\delta\Q=0$ and keeps only the density noise. Its correlator
\cref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation} is exact and needs no closure. The coupled
equations for $\delta\rho$ and $\delta\Q$, on the other hand, need the full covariance of
$(\bEta_\rho,\bEta^\Q,\f^Q)$, and with the lowest-order closure they are ill defined
around the defect.

The von Mises distribution itself is a genuine density, so its exact moments
\cref{eq:exact-moments} give a Gram matrix at every $S$. The solver builds the noise
covariance from them, solving \cref{eq:kappa-S} once per node, while its drift, like the
steady state, keeps the lowest-order closure of \cref{eq:model-Q}.

\subsection{Consistent difference operators}
\label{app:lyapunov-operators}
% Written 2026-10-05 from dean.tex 1556-1910 (sec-fd-operators, sec-stencil-choice) and
% lyapunov_solver/lyapunov_solver.m (915-960 operators and the exact identity check,
% 1166-1183 drift, 1241-1291 noise assembly). Numbers: claude_experiments/fd_operators/
% check_operators.py and cutoff_run_resolution.py.
The equal-point variance of the density depends on how the derivatives in
\cref{eq:linearized-rho} are discretized. In the continuum the spectrum of the density
fluctuations is flat, because two factors of $k^2$ cancel: the noise enters as the divergence
of the flux $\bEta_\rho$, so its power carries one, and diffusion relaxes a mode of wavevector
$\k$ at the rate $(B/\xi_0)k^2$, which carries the other (\cref{sec:defect-fluct}). Every
wavevector below the cutoff contributes equally, so the variance is set by the shortest
wavelengths that are kept. On a grid each factor $k^2$ becomes the symbol of a difference
operator, the factor by which the operator multiplies a plane wave \cite{leveque2007finite}, and the spectrum
stays flat only if the noise and the drift have equal symbols.

The one-dimensional Laplacian shows what can go wrong (\cref{fig:staggered-grid}). On nodes
spaced by $\Delta x$, the drift uses the compact second difference
\begin{equation*}
  (Lu)_n=\frac{u_{n+1}-2u_n+u_{n-1}}{\Delta x^2}.
\end{equation*}
A first derivative can be taken at a node, with the central difference
$(Du)_n=(u_{n+1}-u_{n-1})/2\Delta x$. Applying $D$ twice, however, does not give $L$ (\cref{fig:staggered-grid}(a)):
\begin{equation*}
  (D^2u)_n=\frac{u_{n+2}-2u_n+u_{n-2}}{4\Delta x^2}\neq(Lu)_n .
\end{equation*}

The forward difference $G$ takes the first derivative between two nodes instead, on the face
$n+1/2$, $(Gu)_{n+1/2}=(u_{n+1}-u_n)/\Delta x$. Its negative transpose,
$(-G^\T F)_n=(F_{n+1/2}-F_{n-1/2})/\Delta x$, takes a face flux $F$ back to the nodes as a
divergence (\cref{fig:staggered-grid}(b)), so that whatever leaves one node enters its
neighbour. Applying $G$ and then $-G^\T$ gives the compact Laplacian exactly, $L=-G^\T G$. This is the staggered grid of computational fluid dynamics, which avoids the
decoupling of neighbouring nodes that $D^2$ produces \cite{ferziger2020computational}.

The density noise in \cref{eq:ou-model} is the divergence of the flux noise $\bEta_\rho$, so
its covariance contains a first difference on each side. If this divergence is taken with
$D$, the noise carries $|\hat D|^2$ where the drift carries $\hat L$. For a plane wave
$u_n=\ee^{\ii kn\Delta x}$ the two symbols are \cite{leveque2007finite}
\begin{align}
  \hat L(k)&=-\frac{4}{\Delta x^2}\sin^2\frac{k\Delta x}{2},
  \nonumber\\
  \label{eq:fd-symbols}
  |\hat D(k)|^2&=\frac{\sin^2k\Delta x}{\Delta x^2}=-\hat L(k)\cos^2\frac{k\Delta x}{2},
\end{align}
so the noise power of $D$ falls short of the drift by $\cos^2(k\Delta x/2)$. The
driving by the noise and the damping by the drift then disagree at short wavelengths. We fix
this by taking the divergence with $G$ instead, as is done in fluctuating hydrodynamics
\cite{donev2010accuracy,balboa2012staggered,kim2017stochastic}, so that the noise and the
drift share one operator and the spectrum is exactly flat.

The solver is built this way. In two dimensions, with $G_x$ and $G_y$ taking the nodes to the
faces between horizontal and vertical neighbours, the drift of the density is
$(B/\xi_0)L$ with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fd-identity}
  L=-G_x^\T G_x-G_y^\T G_y .
\end{equation}
The noise flux $\bEta_\rho$ lives on the faces, independent from face to face, with the
local covariance $M_\ab=(2\zeta/\xi_0\rho_0)(\delta_\ab+Q^\ss_\ab)$ of \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss}
sampled there, so the diagonal part of the density noise is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fd-noise}
  \mathbf N_{\rho\rho}=\frac1{\Delta x^2}\sum_{i=x,y}G_i^\T\,\mathrm{diag}(M_{ii})\,G_i ,
\end{equation}
where $1/\Delta x^2$ is the grid form of $\delta(\r-\r')$. For an isotropic noise the noise
matrix is then a multiple of the drift matrix, the spectrum is flat on the grid, and every
node, including those next to the wall, has the same variance. The solver builds both sides
of \cref{eq:fd-identity} in exact arithmetic and stops if they differ.

The other divergence terms of \cref{eq:linearized} follow the same construction. A drift term
$\nabla\tcdot(c\nabla\,\cdot\,)$, such as $(B/\xi_0)\nabla\tcdot(\Q_\ss\nabla\delta\rho)$,
becomes $-\sum_iG_i^\T\,\mathrm{diag}(c)\,G_i$ with $c$ on the faces, and the advection
$\nabla\tcdot(\delta\Q\,\bs w_\ss)$ is $-G^\T$ applied to the face flux $\bs w_\ss\,\delta\Q$,
with $\delta\Q$ averaged onto the faces. The noise of $\delta\Q$ and its correlation with
$\bEta_\rho$ are assembled in the same way. Only the mixed noise terms,
$M_{xy}=M_{yx}\propto Q^\ss_{xy}$, keep the central difference at the nodes. Their symbol is
proportional to $\sin(k_x\Delta x)\sin(k_y\Delta x)$, which is odd in $k_x$ and averages to
zero, as in the continuum, where uniform nematic order leaves the variance unchanged
(\cref{app:variance-uniform}). Derivatives taken on different faces would instead add a
spurious pedestal proportional to $Q^\ss_{xy}$.

Each diagonal term of \cref{eq:fd-noise} is a congruence, so it is positive semi-definite
wherever the local covariance is. With the exact moments of \cref{app:realizability} the
local covariance is valid at every node, and the solver checks it there with a
$6\times6$ eigensolve.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{The noise correlation length and its calibration}{The noise correlation length and its calibration}}
\label{app:lyapunov-cutoff}
% dean.tex 1200-1275 (sec-ln-vs-h, sec-cutoff-numerics), 2048-2089 (sec-noise-cutoff).
% Figure (l_eff/l_n against dx/l_n, 2026-10-06): plotgen/cutoff-calibration (run.sh).
% The 0.1% checks: cutoff_calibration_v2.wls (solver on a uniform state, plus the defect runs of
% set2_2_comove_optimized_2).
The density variance is set by the shortest wavelengths that the noise excites
(\cref{app:lyapunov-operators}), so the solver must impose the cutoff $\elln$ of
\cref{eq:cutoff}. A noise white in space would let the grid set the cutoff instead: the
solver then returns the pedestal $\zeta/(B\rho_0\Delta x^2)$, which is \cref{eq:pedestal}
with $\elln=\Delta x/\sqrt{2\pi}$, so the grid spacing would play the role of the width of a
cell. The monolayer, however, is made of cells of width $\elln$, and we build this
discreteness into the noise. Instead of a noise white in space, we use a noise correlated
over the length $\elln$.

We construct it from the white noise $\bxi$ of \cref{eq:ou-model} with the operator
$W=\exp(\ell_W^2\nabla^2/4)$. Since $\nabla^2$ multiplies a plane wave by $-k^2$, the symbol of
$W$ is the Gaussian $\tilde W(\k)=\ee^{-k^2\ell_W^2/4}$ of \cref{app:variance-cutoff}, and $W$
correlates the noise over the length $\ell_W$. The noise $W\bxi$ is still white in time, and
its covariance between the points $\r$ and $\r'$ is
\begin{equation*}
  (W\mathbf NW)(\r,\r')=\int\!\d^2\r_1\d^2\r_2\,W(\r,\r_1)\mathbf N(\r_1,\r_2)W(\r_2,\r'),
\end{equation*}
with $W(\r,\r_1)$ the kernel of $W$ and $\mathbf N(\r_1,\r_2)$ the covariance of
\cref{eq:ou-model}. For any field $\bs v$ the quadratic form of $W\mathbf NW$ equals that of
$\mathbf N$ evaluated on $W\bs v$, so the correlated covariance is positive semi-definite
whenever $\mathbf N$ is. Moreover, $W$ commutes with the drift $(B/\xi_0)\nabla^2$ of the
density, so the correlated noise gives the same density covariance as the density smoothed
with $W$, which is what \cref{app:variance-cutoff} computes.

On the grid we replace $\nabla^2$ in $W$ by the discrete Dirichlet Laplacian
$\nabla^2_{\Delta x}$, and the correlation then comes out too short. The discrete Laplacian
measures the curvature of a wave from three neighbouring nodes, so it underestimates the
curvature of waves only a few nodes long. Its symbol is $-k^2$ with each $k_i^2$ replaced by
$(4/\Delta x^2)\sin^2(k_i\Delta x/2)$. For the shortest wave of the grid, which alternates
from node to node, this gives $4/\Delta x^2$ instead of $\pi^2/\Delta x^2$. The grid version of $W$
therefore damps the shortest waves too little, and since these set the variance, the pedestal
comes out too high. The error depends on the ratio $\Delta x/\elln$, and it
disappears on fine grids, where all the waves that $W$ lets through are long compared with
$\Delta x$. 

A finer grid, however, is out of reach: the covariance is a dense matrix with
$(3N_\mathrm{int}^2)^2$ entries, so its memory grows as $N_\mathrm{int}^4$, and at
$N_\mathrm{int}=150$ it already takes $36$~GB. We therefore keep the grid and calibrate the
correlation length instead.

Quantitatively, the symbol of $W$ on the grid is
\begin{equation*}
  \exp\Bigl[-\frac{\ell_W^2}{\Delta x^2}\Bigl(\sin^2\frac{k_x\Delta x}2
  +\sin^2\frac{k_y\Delta x}2\Bigr)\Bigr],
\end{equation*}
which decays more slowly than $\ee^{-k^2\ell_W^2/4}$, since
$\sin^2(k\Delta x/2)<(k\Delta x/2)^2$. 
With $\ell_W=\elln$ the pedestal on the grid therefore exceeds \cref{eq:pedestal} by the factor
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:lattice-overshoot}
  \chi=2\pi\frac{\elln^2}{\Delta x^2}\,\mathcal I\Bigl(\frac{\elln}{\Delta x}\Bigr)^2
  \simeq1+\frac{\Delta x^2}{4\elln^2},
\end{equation}
with $\mathcal I(s)=\ee^{-s^2}I_0(s^2)$ and $I_0$ the modified Bessel function. On our
grid, $\Delta x/\elln=0.53$, the pedestal would come out $9\%$ too high.

The solver removes this factor by building $W$ from a length $\ell_\mathrm{eff}$
instead of $\elln$, chosen so that the pedestal on the grid equals \cref{eq:pedestal} at
the physical $\elln$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ell-eff}
  \mathcal I(\ell_\mathrm{eff}/\Delta x)=\frac{\Delta x}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\elln}.
\end{equation}
\Cref{fig:cutoff-calibration} shows the ratio $\ell_\mathrm{eff}/\elln$ that solves
\cref{eq:ell-eff} against $\Delta x/\elln$. On fine grids it approaches one,
$\ell_\mathrm{eff}/\elln\simeq1+\Delta x^2/8\elln^2$, and on our grid $\ell_\mathrm{eff}$ lies
$4\%$ above $\elln$. 


The calibration fixes the isotropic pedestal only. The anisotropic term of
\cref{eq:total-gauss} keeps a lattice correction of its own, so the peak modulation $\nu_0$
of the solver approaches its continuum value only as the grid is refined, which accounts for
part of the difference from the analytic $\nu_0$ (\cref{app:lyapunov-results}).

\subsection{Boundary conditions}
\label{app:lyapunov-walls}
A bounded domain needs three conditions at its wall, not one: on the steady state, on the
fluctuating field, and on the noise flux through the wall. The last two answer one
question, whether a cell may cross the wall, once for the fluctuation and once for the
noise, so they must agree. Two coherent choices remain for the fluctuations. A reservoir
pins the fluctuation and lets the noise flux through, like a window cut from a larger
colony. A sealed wall blocks both, like a container.

We use the reservoir. Its
wall nodes are removed, so $\delta\rho=\delta\Q=0$ there, the noise flux through the faces
next to the wall is kept, and $W$ is built on the Dirichlet Laplacian, so the correlated
noise vanishes at the wall too. The steady state of \cref{app:comoving} has the same density wall, $\rho_\ss=1$, so
the density sector is coherent throughout. Only the nematic wall differs: the steady state
leaves $\Q_\ss$ free at the wall, $\nh\tcdot\nabla\Q_\ss=0$, whereas the fluctuations are
anchored there, $\delta\Q=0$. The fluctuations see the steady state only through
$\rho_\ss$, $\Q_\ss$, $\u$ and $\bs w_\ss=\u+(B/\xi_0)\nabla\rho_\ss$, and we assume that
this difference does not reach the core.

\subsection{Results}
\label{app:lyapunov-results}
\Cref{fig:variance,fig:variance3d} compare the solver with \cref{eq:total-gauss}.
Away from the core the pedestal agrees with \cref{eq:pedestal}, as the calibration of
\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff} requires. Along the axis the modulation has the structure of the
analytic result: it is raised on the $-x$ side and lowered on the $+x$ side, by a few
percent within a couple of micrometers of the core. The two sides, however, are no longer
equal. The modulation is weaker and closer to the core on the $-x$ side, and stronger and
farther out on the $+x$ side. Part of this difference is the grid
(\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff}). The rest comes from $\delta\Q$, which the analytic result
drops, and from the drift, which makes the steady state unequal on the two sides
(\cref{app:comoving}). Across the axis, where \cref{eq:total-gauss} gives no modulation,
the solver gives a weak one, an order of magnitude below that along the axis.

The solver also returns the fluctuations of the nematic order, and with them those of the
active force $\fa=-\zeta\bs f$, $f_\alpha=\partial_\beta Q_\ab$, which $\delta\Q$ carries as
$\delta f_\alpha=\partial_\beta\delta Q_\ab$. This fluctuating force tests the approximation $\delta\Q=0$ of \cref{sec:defect-fluct}, and it is not small.
\Cref{fig:force-axis} compares its fluctuations with the mean force along the axis of the
defect. The fluctuations exceed the mean everywhere. Near the core they are a few times
larger, and away from it the difference grows, because the mean force decays with the
distance from the core while the fluctuations level off at a uniform value. The fluctuating
force is therefore strongest around the core, where the mean force is, but relative to the
mean it dominates most in the aligned regions far from the defect. The traction measured in
Ref.~\cite{han2025local} shows the same pattern, with fluctuations far above the mean around
defects that stay strong in aligned regions, where the mean traction nearly vanishes. The
traction also contains the pressure gradient, however, so this resemblance is qualitative.

The two components of the fluctuating force differ near the core. Along the axis
(\cref{fig:force-axis}), $f_x$ fluctuates most on either side of the core and less at the
core itself, whereas $f_y$ fluctuates most at the core. In the plane, the total variance
forms a ring around the core (\cref{fig:force-map}), and on this ring the variance of $f_x$
is much larger than that of $f_y$. Around the core the force thus fluctuates mostly along
the axis of the defect, the direction of the mean active force. At the core and far from it,
the two components fluctuate equally.

The density variance nevertheless changes little, because $\delta\Q$ evolves orders of
magnitude more slowly than the density relaxes over $\tau_c$. Over a correlation time of the
density the $\delta\Q$ force therefore acts like a static active force, and the density
responds to a static force only at order $\zeta/B$, as \cref{eq:rho-analytic} shows for the
mean, while $\sigma_\rho\propto B^{-1/2}$.
