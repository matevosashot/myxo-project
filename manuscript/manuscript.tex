\title{Fluctuating active nematics account for layer formation in \textit{Myxococcus xanthus} colonies}

\author{Ashot Matevosyan}
\author{Ricard Alert}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
In colonies of the bacterium \textit{Myxococcus xanthus},
cells leave the monolayer and form new layers, the first
step toward the fruiting bodies that starving colonies
build. Experiments show that these layers form
preferentially at $+1/2$ topological defects of the cell
orientation. However, the mean pressure at a defect falls
three to four orders of magnitude short of the pressure
needed for an extruded cell to grow into a new layer,
whereas the stress in the monolayer fluctuates strongly. We
therefore propose that layers form during large, and hence
rare, pressure fluctuations, and we compute the statistics
of these fluctuations. Treating layer formation as
nucleation gives this threshold, the critical pressure, so
the layering rate is proportional to the probability that
the pressure exceeds it. The pressure fluctuations depend
on the activity of the cells, so we systematically derive
fluctuating active-nematic equations for the monolayer by
coarse-graining self-propelled rods that reverse their
direction of motion, as in the derivation of Dean's
equation. As a result, one coefficient, which grows with
the distance a cell glides between reversals, sets both the
active stress and the noise amplitude, so neither can be
tuned without the other. The pressure fluctuates more
strongly close to a $+1/2$ defect, which makes pressures
above the critical pressure more likely there and so
enhances the layering rate. The enhancement peaks off the
defect core, and we compare its profile with the measured
layering rate.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
\textit{Myxococcus xanthus} is a soil bacterium that lives
and hunts in groups \cite{munoz2016}. While nutrients last,
the cells glide together across the substrate as a swarm, a
thin film that feeds on prey bacteria
(\cref{fig:myxo_life}(A)). When the nutrients run out, the
swarm instead aggregates into mounds, and these grow into
fruiting bodies of $10^5$ to $10^6$ cells, in which some of
the cells become spores (\cref{fig:myxo_life}(B)). A
fruiting body is built by stacking layers of cells on top
of one another, so its first step is the formation of a
second layer on top of the monolayer (red arrow in
\cref{fig:myxo_life}). We ask how this first new layer
forms.

The cells are rods $5$ to $7~\micron$ long and about
$0.5~\micron$ wide that glide along their long axis and
reverse their direction every few minutes
\cite{wu2009periodic,copenhagen2021}. Although each cell
moves in one direction at a time, it keeps reversing, and
neighboring rods interact in the same way whichever end
leads. At long time scales the monolayer is therefore an
active nematic
\cite{marchetti2013hydrodynamics,doostmohammadi2018active},
and since the substrate takes up the momentum of the cells
through friction, it belongs to dry active matter, in which
momentum is not conserved
\cite{marchetti2013hydrodynamics}. The local average axis
of the cells, the director, is undefined at isolated points
called topological defects. Because the director has no
head or tail, it can rotate by any integer multiple of
$\pi$ along a loop around a defect, so nematic defects
carry integer or half-integer charge
\cite{degennes1993physics,mermin1979topological}. The
elastic energy of a defect grows as the square of its
charge, so a $+1$ defect lowers its energy by splitting
into two $+1/2$ defects
\cite{degennes1993physics,duclos2017topological}.
Half-integer defects therefore dominate in two-dimensional
nematics \cite{doostmohammadi2018active,giomi2013defect},
and \textit{M.\ xanthus} monolayers show them too
\cite{copenhagen2021}: the comet-shaped $+1/2$ defect and
the three-armed $-1/2$ defect
(\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a,b)). In other living active
nematics, defects are where cells leave the plane:
epithelial cells are extruded at $+1/2$ defects
\cite{saw2017topological}, neural progenitor cells
accumulate there into three-dimensional mounds
\cite{kawaguchi2017topological}, and in colonies of
\textit{Pseudomonas aeruginosa}, cells of a fast-moving
mutant rotate upright and become trapped where two $+1/2$
defects collide \cite{meacock2021bacteria}. In chaining
\textit{Bacillus subtilis} biofilms, cells instead lift off
near $-1/2$ defects, where simulations show stress
accumulating \cite{yaman2019emergence}.

Copenhagen \textit{et al.}~imaged \textit{M.\ xanthus}
monolayers on a nutrient-rich substrate, where no fruiting
bodies form, and still saw new layers and holes appear
throughout the colony \cite{copenhagen2021}. Under
starvation, fruiting bodies develop so fast that the
emergence of new layers is hard to follow, whereas on
nutrients the colony stays a thin sheet in which single
layer-formation events can be tracked. We expect the same
layering mechanism to act when nutrients are scarce. Unlike
in colonies that grow by cell division, where the pressure
that growth builds up induces layering at a critical colony
size \cite{grant2014,you2019mono}, these layers form
independently of the colony size; they are induced by the
migration of the cells \cite{copenhagen2021}. Nor does cell
division drive them: over the one to three hours of
imaging, the colony does not grow substantially, because at
the temperature of the experiments the cells divide only
about every $10~\mathrm h$ \cite{copenhagen2021}. The new
layers form, however, about $200$ times more often near
$+1/2$ defects than far from them
(\cref{fig:defects-layering}(c), red), whereas holes open
about $80$ times more often near $-1/2$ defects. Here we
address the layers at $+1/2$ defects. Copenhagen \textit{et
al.}~explained the preference for $+1/2$ defects with an
active nematic model in which the friction with the
substrate is weaker along the cells than across them
\cite{copenhagen2021}. This friction anisotropy creates a
net inflow of cells at the defect. Cells were assumed to be
readily extruded, so this net inflow was interpreted as the
formation of a new layer. The measured
flows~\cite{copenhagen2021,han2025local} also converge at
$+1/2$ defects, but a converging flow is what layer
formation itself requires, since the cells that leave the
monolayer must be replaced \cite{han2025local}. The
measured inflow is therefore a consequence of layer
formation, not its cause.

The stress in these monolayers, however, fluctuates
strongly. The divergence of this stress is the traction
that the cells exert on the substrate. Han \textit{et
al.}~measured this traction around $\pm1/2$ defects and
found that its standard deviation is about ten times its
mean \cite{han2025local}. The fluctuations stay strong in
aligned regions away from defects, where the mean traction
nearly vanishes. Han \textit{et al.}~attributed these
fluctuations to local polar order, whose magnitude the
cells tune through their reversal rate, and found them
stronger at $+1/2$ than at $-1/2$ defects. They proposed
that the fluctuating polar forces build up local pressure,
which can trigger new layers. In this work, we extend the
active nematic theory to capture these fluctuations and the
stochasticity of layer formation. We propose that layers
form during large, and hence rare, pressure fluctuations
that originate in the self-propulsion and reversals of
single cells.

In the experiments, patches of cells are extruded
frequently, but most of them shrink back into the
monolayer, and only a few grow into a new
layer~\cite{copenhagen2021}. In the view of classical
nucleation theory, fluctuations would extrude patches of
cells at random, and a patch grows only if its area exceeds
a critical area set by an energy
barrier~\cite{debenedetti2020metastable}. We therefore
treat layer formation as a nucleation event. In our system,
however, the energy cost of a patch depends on the pressure
in the monolayer: a larger pressure decreases the critical
area (\cref{sec:nucleation-1}), so the nucleation rate is
higher where the pressure is high.

We thus model layering as two processes: a stochastic
evolution of the pressure over long times and long length
scales, and the nucleation of a layer over short times and
short length scales. On the scales of nucleation, the
pressure is then a slow variable and uniform in space, so
the layering rate at a given position and time depends on
the instantaneous pressure there. This separation of time
and length scales is a simplification of the model, because
the layering rate changes over distances comparable to the
size of a cell (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(c)).

A defect can thus favor layers through the statistics of
the pressure around it. We therefore compute the
distribution of the pressure fluctuations around a defect.
This requires a fluctuating version of the active nematic
equations, which contain a noise term. In equilibrium, the
fluctuation-dissipation theorem (FDT) fixes that noise by
tying it to the dissipative terms in the equations of
motion \cite{kubo1966fluctuation}. Even though an active
nematic is out of equilibrium and the theorem does not
apply, phenomenological theories add the noise to the
continuum equations as if the FDT held, or by hand, with a
free amplitude and a chosen tensor structure
\cite{ramaswamy2003active,mishra2010dynamic,shankar2018low,
marchetti2013hydrodynamics,basu2008thermal}. We derive the
noise systematically from the dynamics of the cells.

Kinetic theories derive continuum equations of active
matter from particle models, including self-propelled rods
that reverse, but they average the noise out on the way
\cite{bertin2006boltzmann,baskaran2008hydrodynamics,
grossmann2016mesoscale,peshkov2012nonlinear}. The
Dean--Kawasaki equation, an exact stochastic equation for
the density of interacting Langevin particles, keeps it
instead
\cite{dean1996,kawasaki1994stochastic,illien2025dean}. It
has been extended to run-and-tumble and active Brownian
particles \cite{tailleur2008statistical,solon2015active}
and to apolar active nematics of particles that step back
and forth at random and align with their neighbors as in
the Vicsek model \cite{bertin2013}. We extend the
Dean--Kawasaki equation to rods that interact through a
pair potential and reverse at a rate $\frev$, as
\textit{M.\ xanthus} does (\cref{fig:model-micro}), and
then take moments of their distribution over orientations,
so that the noise and the deterministic terms follow from
the same coarse-graining.

The result is a pair of stochastic equations for the
density and the nematic order parameter. Self-propulsion
and reversals together drive an extensile active stress.
The active stress coefficient grows with the distance a
cell glides between reversals, and the same coefficient
sets the amplitude of the noise. Neither can therefore be
tuned without the other. The coarse-graining also fixes how
this noise depends on the local nematic order. Fluctuating
cell forces are also known to produce extensile stress in
cell
monolayers~\cite{vafa2021fluctuations,killeen2022polar},
and for reversing rods Banerjee \textit{et
al.}~\cite{banerjee2026active} derived the active stress in
the same form as ours.

Solved around a $+1/2$ defect, these equations give a
density variance, and hence, through the stiffness of the
monolayer,\footnote{More generally, through the equation of
state of the monolayer, which relates its pressure to its
density (\cref{sec:pressure}).} a pressure variance. The
variance is largest just off the core, at the head of the
defect, the side where the director bends around it
(\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)). In the linearized theory
the pressure is Gaussian, so a larger variance makes
high pressures more likely and so raises the layering rate
(\cref{sec:nucleation-1}). In unpublished observations, the
authors of Refs.~\cite{copenhagen2021,han2025local} also
find that layering starts more often at the head of the
defect (private communication), as in the example of
\cref{fig:defects-layering}(b). Besides the radial profile,
which \cref{fig:defects-layering}(c) compares with the
measurement, the predicted layering rate therefore has an
angular profile around the defect, but there is no
quantitative measurement of the angular profile.
Fluctuations of microscopic origin can thus account for
where layers form, although the calculation does not
exclude other mechanisms.

This paper is organized as follows. The next section
describes layer formation as nucleation, which reduces the
layering rate to the statistics of the pressure.
\Cref{sec:model} then derives the stochastic equations from
the microscopic model, and \cref{sec:defect} solves them at
a $+1/2$ defect, first for the steady state and then for

compares it with the measurements of
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}. Finally, \cref{sec:discussion}
summarizes what is fitted and what is predicted, lists the
simplifications of the model, discusses the reversal rate
as a control parameter, and proposes experimental tests.
The appendices give all derivations in detail, so that the
paper is self-contained.

\section{Layer formation as a nucleation event}
\label{sec:nucleation-1}
% dean.tex 1284-1390


We model layer formation with nucleation theory, which
defines a critical area above which an extruded patch of
cells grows into a new layer. We begin by calculating the
energy cost of extruding a patch of cells.


We assume that cells adhere both to the substrate and to
other cells, and that water completely wets the colony. Let
$\Wc$ and $\Ws$ be the cell-cell and cell-substrate
adhesion energies per unit area, and $\gammaw$ the surface
tension of water. Suppose a patch of cells of area $A$ and
perimeter $\ell$ forms a new layer
(\cref{fig:nucleation}(a)). The cells of the patch need to
(\textit{i}) detach from the substrate, (\textit{ii})
detach from their neighbors, and (\textit{iii}) stretch the
surface of the water film over the edge of the patch. This
costs $\Ws A + \Wc\ell h + \gammaw\ell h$, where $h$ is the
height of the monolayer. At the same time, the bottom layer
relaxes to fill the space the patch left; cells move in
from the outer boundary of the monolayer, which retracts,
so the bottom layer as a whole does not regain the
substrate contact that the patch lost
(\cref{fig:nucleation}(a)). The energy gain comes from
(\textit{iv}) the adhesion of the patch to the cells
beneath it and (\textit{v}) the work done by the pressure,
$\Wc A + pA$. Here $p$ is the two-dimensional pressure of
the monolayer, a force per unit length.
\Cref{app:extrusion} derives $pA$ from the elastic energy
the bottom layer releases.

\begin{figure*}[t]

  % Source of (b): plotgen/fluctuating_barier/plot_fluctuating_barier.py

  \label{fig:nucleation}

The cell-cell adhesion $\Wc$ enters twice with opposite
signs: as a cost on the side faces and as a gain on the
bottom face. For a circular patch, $\ell=\sqrt{4\pi A}$,
and the energy cost of the new layer is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dE-of-A}
    \Delta E(A) = (\Ws-\Wc-p)\,A + (\Wc+\gammaw)\,h\sqrt{4\pi A},
\end{equation}
which is a function of the patch area $A$ and monolayer
pressure $p$. Here, $A$ is a continuous variable, and
\cref{eq:dE-of-A} is also defined below the cell area
$\Acell$. This is meaningful because the cells are not
rigid and can be partially extruded, so $A$ need not be a
multiple of $\Acell$.

The perimeter term is a cost and grows as $\sqrt A$. The
area term grows as $A$ and is a gain once $p>\Ws-\Wc$. At
such pressures, $\Delta E(A)$ rises at small $A$, reaches a
maximum and then falls. This is the barrier of classical
nucleation theory (\cref{fig:nucleation}(b)). Its maximum
is the critical nucleus, of area $A^*=\pi
h^2(\Wc+\gammaw)^2/(p+\Wc-\Ws)^2$. A larger patch lowers
its energy by growing, a smaller one by shrinking.


In classical nucleation theory, the probability that
fluctuations extrude a patch of area $A$ is proportional to
the Boltzmann factor $\ee^{-\Delta E(A)/E_0}$, where $E_0$
is the characteristic energy of the fluctuations.
Accordingly, the rate of layer formation is proportional to
$\ee^{-\Delta E(A^*)/E_0}$, where $\Delta E(A^*)$ is the
height of the barrier~\cite{debenedetti2020metastable}. For
thermal fluctuations, $E_0=\kB T$, with $\kB$ the Boltzmann
constant. Self-propelled bacteria, however, are far from
equilibrium, and $E_0$ would be set by the active
fluctuations in the monolayer.

The shape and the height of the barrier depend on the
pressure in the monolayer, and the pressure changes slowly
and stochastically in time; the following sections compute
its statistics. Layer formation can therefore be viewed as
an escape over a fluctuating barrier
(\cref{fig:nucleation}(b))~\cite{reimann1995thermally}.

In experiments, although layers form rarely, patches of
cells are extruded frequently~\cite{copenhagen2021}. One
source of this out-of-plane motion is the activity of the
cells. Another could be the roughness of the substrate, but
other sources are unclear. We therefore simplify the
process: we assume that only single cells are extruded, at
random and at a constant and uniform rate
$\calJ_{\!\Acell}$, and we neglect the extrusion of larger
patches. The rate $\calJ_{\!\Acell}$ thus stands in place
of the Boltzmann factor for the probability that
fluctuations extrude a patch. A layer then forms when the
critical nucleus is smaller than one cell, $A^*<\Acell$,
since the extruded cell then forms a patch larger than the
critical nucleus, and this patch grows, so the layering
rate is\footnote{The rates $\calJ_{\!\Acell}$ and $\calJ$
are rate densities with units
$\micron^{-2}\tcdot\minute^{-1}$. They are defined over the
monolayer, and $\calJ_{\!\Acell}\,\d A\,\d t$ is the
average number of cells extruded in the area $\d A$ during
the time interval $\d t$.}
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:layering-rate-approximation}
 \calJ \approx \calJ_{\!\Acell}  \Prob(A^* < \Acell) = \calJ_{\!\Acell}  \Prob(p>\pcrit),
\end{equation}
where $\Prob$ denotes the probability measure over the
fluctuations of the pressure. The condition $A^*<\Acell$ is
equivalent to $p>\pcrit$, with the critical pressure
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pcrit}
  \pcrit = (\Ws-\Wc) + \sqrt{\frac{\pi h^2}{\Acell}}\,(\Wc+\gammaw).
\end{equation}

\Cref{eq:layering-rate-approximation} is a simplification,
but a more detailed model of the extrusion of patches would
not be more faithful, because experimental evidence on this
process is lacking. We therefore focus on the effect of the
pressure in the monolayer on layer formation. A more
general model, outlined below, might nonetheless help to
understand \cref{eq:layering-rate-approximation} and its
limitations. Let $\calJ_p(\r)$ be the layering rate at the
point $\r$ when the pressure there is $p$. The average
layering rate at $\r$ is then
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:layering-rate-average}
  \calJ(\r) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \calJ_p(\r)\,\calP_\r(p)\,\d p,
\end{equation}
where $\calP_\r(p)$ is the probability density of the
pressure at $\r$. \Cref{eq:layering-rate-approximation}
underestimates this rate for two reasons: (\textit{i}) it
does not account for the extrusion of larger patches, which
grow into a new layer also when the critical nucleus is
larger than one cell, and (\textit{ii}) it counts partially
extruded cells, which have a higher rate, with the same
rate as one extruded cell. Hence
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:underestimates}
  \calJ(\r) \geq \int_{\pcrit}^\infty \calJ_p(\r)\,\calP_\r(p)\,\d p
  \geq \calJ_{\pcrit} \int_{\pcrit}^\infty \calP_\r(p)\,\d p ,
\end{align}
where the second inequality uses that $\calJ_p$ increases
monotonically with $p$. Since
$\calJ_{\pcrit}=\calJ_{\!\Acell}$, the lower bound is
\cref{eq:layering-rate-approximation}.

Some remarks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In \cref{eq:layering-rate-average}, the layering
  rate depends on position through the statistics of the
  pressure, $\calP_\r(p)$, and through the rate
  $\calJ_p(\r)$ at fixed pressure.
  \Cref{eq:layering-rate-approximation} instead takes a
  constant and uniform $\calJ_{\!\Acell}$, although we
  speculate that the single-cell extrusion rate could
  itself be enhanced near topological defects. Since the
  critical pressure does not depend on position either
  (\cref{eq:pcrit}), the layering rate of
  \cref{eq:layering-rate-approximation} varies in
  space only through the statistics of the pressure
  fluctuations around the defect.

  \item \Cref{eq:layering-rate-approximation} requires the
  pressure to change slowly compared with the extrusion of
  a patch, because escape over a fluctuating barrier
  follows the instantaneous barrier only in this slow
  limit~\cite{reimann1995thermally}; when the barrier
  changes fast, the crossing sees the average barrier
  instead~\cite{doering1992resonant}.

\item With the parameter values of
\cref{app:parameters-extrusion},
$\pcrit=35~\kPa\tcdot\micron$. The surface tension of water
dominates it. The mean pressure in the monolayer is far
below $\pcrit$: at a defect it is of the order of the
active stress, three to four orders of magnitude below
$\pcrit$ (\cref{app:parameters-choice}). Layers therefore
form predominantly during large, and hence rare,
fluctuations of the pressure.

\item The nucleus is idealized as a disk of one cell area,
which keeps the nucleation condition independent of the
orientation of the rod-shaped cells, and the pressure is
taken at a point rather than averaged over a cell. Other
choices would mainly shift $\pcrit$ or the width of the
pressure fluctuations. The layering profile depends on the
two only through their ratio (\cref{sec:nucleation-2}), so
such shifts are absorbed by the one parameter that we fit,
the stiffness of the monolayer against compression.
\end{itemize}


In the following sections we set up a microscopic model of
the monolayer and calculate the pressure fluctuations
around a defect. Then, in \cref{sec:nucleation-2}, we
compute the spatial profile of the layering rate
\eqref{eq:layering-rate-approximation}.


\section{Fluctuating active nematics from microscopic dynamics}
\label{sec:model}
% dean.tex 109-540

\subsection{The microscopic model}
\label{sec:model-micro}
% dean.tex 109-173 ("Microscopic equations of motion").
We start from Langevin equations for $N$ overdamped,
self-propelled rods
\cite{baskaran2008hydrodynamics,grossmann2016mesoscale},
each with position $\r_i$ and orientation $\theta_i$
(\cref{fig:model-micro}):
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:model}
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:langevin-position}
    \frac{\d\r_i}{\d t} &= \frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F_i + v_0\,\p(\theta_i) k_i,
    \\
    \label{eq:langevin-angle}
    \frac{\d\theta_i}{\d t} &= \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma_i + \lambda_i,
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
with $\p(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ the unit vector
along the rod axis and $k_i=\pm1$ a sign that gives the
direction of motion along that axis and flips at each
reversal. Here $\xi_0/\rho_0$ and $\xir/\rho_0$
are translational and rotational friction coefficients,
respectively ($\rho_0$ a reference density carried along
for later convenience), and $\lambda_i$ is Gaussian white
noise,
$\langle\lambda_i(t)\lambda_j(t')\rangle
=2\Lambda\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t')$, with $\Lambda$ the
rotational diffusion coefficient. The rods interact through
a pairwise potential $V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')$, so that
$V_\mathrm{tot}=\tfrac12\sum_{i,j=1}^N
V(\r_i-\r_j,\theta_i,\theta_j)$. The force and torque on
rod $i$ are the values at $(\r_i,\theta_i)$ of the fields
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:force-torque}
    \F(\r,\theta) &= -\pd{}{\r}\sum_{j=1}^N V(\r-\r_j,\theta,\theta_j),
    \nonumber\\
    \Gamma(\r,\theta) &= -\pd{}{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^N V(\r-\r_j,\theta,\theta_j).
\end{align}
The sums run over all $N$ rods, including
$j=i$,\footnote{Including $j=i$ lets us write the force and
torque in \cref{sec:model-coarse} as gradients of a single
functional of the density of all rods,
\cref{eq:pair-free-energy}.} so that the same two fields
act on every rod. The self term adds no force or torque: a
pair potential between identical rods satisfies
$V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')=V(\r'-\r,\theta',\theta)$, and at
$\r=\r'$ rotational invariance leaves it a function of
$\theta-\theta'$ alone, and this symmetry makes that
function even. Both derivatives in
\cref{eq:force-torque} therefore vanish on the $j=i$ term.

The second term of \cref{eq:langevin-position} is
self-propulsion at speed $v_0$ along the rod axis. Here
$k_i$ is a dichotomous noise: $k_i=\pm1$ with equal
probability, switching at rate $\frev$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:k-dichotomous}
    \la k_i(t)k_j(t')\ra = \delta_{ij}\,\ee^{-2\frev|t-t'|}.
\end{equation}
This models the reversals of \textit{Myxococcus xanthus}
gliding motility \cite{blackhart1985frizzy,guzzo2018gated}.
At time scales long compared with $\frev^{-1}$, we
approximate $k_i$ by Gaussian white noise, $\langle
k_i(t)k_j(t')\rangle \approx
\frev^{-1}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t')$. The coarse-graining of
\cref{sec:model-coarse} uses It\^o calculus and therefore
requires this approximation.
Density equations for particles that switch between
discrete states, as our rods do at each reversal, have been
derived without taking this
limit~\cite{bressloff2024global,spinney2025dean}.

The dichotomous noise also simplifies the reversals
themselves, because it makes them a Poisson process. The
measured intervals between reversals are more
regular~\cite{wu2009periodic}, since they come from a gated
oscillator with a refractory period, a time after each
reversal during which the cell cannot reverse
again~\cite{guzzo2018gated}. Both simplifications could be
lifted by keeping the direction of motion of each cell as a
dynamical variable~\cite{spinney2025dean}, together with
the time since its last reversal. We keep both as
deliberate simplifications, because the white-noise limit
closes the theory on the density and the nematic order
(\cref{sec:model-closure}).


Self-propulsion is a multiplicative noise. The noise is
$k_i$, and the amplitude it multiplies, $v_0\p(\theta_i)$,
is set by the orientation of the rod. This amplitude
depends only on $\theta_i$, which $k_i$ does not drive, so
the It\^o and Stratonovich readings of \cref{eq:model}
coincide (\cref{app:dean-approach}). The term
$v_0\p(\theta_i)k_i$ is also the only noise in
\cref{eq:langevin-position}, since the passive transport by
the pair force has no noise of its own.

\subsection{Coarse-graining that preserves fluctuations}
\label{sec:model-coarse}
% dean.tex 174-359 ("Dean's approach").
We take Dean's approach. Dean's equation \cite{dean1996} is
an \emph{exact} field-theoretic Langevin equation for the
density of interacting Langevin processes. Our model
\eqref{eq:model} adds an orientation, a self-propulsion and
a reversal noise. The same derivation then gives an exact
equation for the joint density over the state space
$(\r,\theta)$; the only approximation it relies on is the
Gaussian white-noise limit taken in the previous
subsection.
% SW-Q(P1,M1) [run 1]: "the only approximation it relies on is the Gaussian white-noise limit" (Sec. III B ¶1): readers then meet a second assumption in ¶5 (Eq. (12) used for a smooth density) and read it as a contradiction (R26 sev 2, R36 sev 2). a) scope it: "the only approximation in the derivation itself is ..." and add in ¶5 "a second assumption"; b) mention the smooth-density assumption already here; c) keep; other
% SW-A:

Write the single-particle density
$\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta) =
\delta(\r-\r_i(t))\,\delta(\theta-\theta_i(t))$, and let
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:joint-density-definition}
  \varrho_t(\r,\theta)=\rho_0^{-1}\sum_i\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta)
\end{align}
be the joint density over $(\r,\theta)$, made dimensionless
by $\rho_0$. The interaction is pairwise, so the force and
torque fields of \cref{eq:force-torque} are gradients of a
single functional of $\varrho_t$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pair-free-energy}
    \calF[\varrho] = \frac{\rho_0^2}2\!\int\!\d\r\d\r'\,\d\theta\d\theta'\,
    V(\r\!-\!\r'\!,\theta,\theta')\,\varrho(\r,\theta)\,\varrho(\r'\!,\theta'),
\end{equation}
namely the fields of \cref{eq:force-torque} can be written
as
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:force-torque-F}
  \F=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\r\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho ,
  \qquad
  \Gamma=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\theta\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho.
\end{align}
Applying It\^o's lemma to $\varrho^{(i)}_t$ and summing
over $i$ then gives a closed equation for $\varrho_t$ alone
(\cref{app:dean-approach}),
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:final-langevin}
    \begin{aligned}
    \partial_t\varrho_t(\r,\theta) = {}&
    \frac1{\xi_0}\pd{}{\r}\cdot\left(\varrho_t\pd{}{\r}\frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho}\right)
    + \frac1{\xir}\pd{}{\theta}\!\left(\varrho_t\pd{}{\theta}\frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho}\right)
    \\
    &+ \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha(\theta)p_\beta(\theta)\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
    + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{\theta^2}
    \\
    &- \pd{}{\r}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p(\theta)\,k(\r,\theta,t)\right)
    \\
    &- \pd{}{\theta}\!\left(\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda(\r,\theta,t)\right).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Its second line comes from the second-order derivatives in
It\^o's lemma. The last two lines of
\cref{eq:final-langevin} carry two noise fields,
$k(\r,\theta,t)$ and $\lambda(\r,\theta,t)$. Both are
Gaussian white noises in position, orientation and time,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:noise-correlators}
    \la k(\r,\theta,t)\,k(\r',\theta',t')\ra &= \frac{v_0^2}{\frev\rho_0}\;\Delta,
    \nonumber\\
    \la \lambda(\r,\theta,t)\,\lambda(\r',\theta',t')\ra &= \frac{2\Lambda}{\rho_0}\;\Delta,
\end{align}
with
$\Delta\equiv\delta(t-t')\delta(\r-\r')\delta(\theta-\theta')$,
and they are uncorrelated with each other. Their amplitudes
are inherited from the reversal noise $k_i$ and the
rotational noise $\lambda_i$ of \cref{eq:model}.
\Cref{app:dean-approach} derives that relation, together
with the multiplicative factor $\sqrt{\varrho_t}$ that
accompanies both fields.


\Cref{eq:final-langevin} is nonlinear even without
interactions. The noise is multiplicative: where there are
no rods, $\varrho_t$ cannot fluctuate. The product
$\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,k$ is an It\^o product, because
\cref{eq:final-langevin} follows from It\^o's lemma:
$\varrho_t$ is evaluated before each noise increment
\cite{dean1996}. Because each realization of $k$ and
$\lambda$ gives one random history of $\varrho_t$, the
statistics of the density, and of the pressure in
\cref{sec:nucleation-1}, follow from
\cref{eq:final-langevin} directly.

Continuum theories usually work instead with the
noise-averaged density. Averaging \cref{eq:final-langevin}
over the noise gives an equation for the mean density that
contains the pair correlation. The equation for the pair
correlation contains the three-point correlation, and so on
\cite{dean1996}. Dynamical density functional theory closes
this hierarchy with an equilibrium free energy
\cite{marconi1999dynamic,tevrugt2020classical}, and kinetic
theories of self-propelled rods close the analogous
hierarchy for the one-rod distribution
\cite{baskaran2008hydrodynamics}. Both give deterministic
equations for the ensemble-averaged density, because the
fluctuations are averaged out before the closure. The noise
drops out of the equation for the mean, but not of
\cref{eq:final-langevin}, which is the equation for one
realization.

The joint density $\varrho_t$ is a sum of delta functions,
an empirical density. Strictly speaking,
\cref{eq:final-langevin} is derived only for such
generalized functions, and rigorous results for it are few
\cite{konarovskyi2019dean,konarovskyi2020dean,
cornalba2023dean}. However, it becomes well-posed if the
noise is correlated in space \cite{fehrman2024well}, or if
each delta function is replaced by a kernel of finite width
\cite{cornalba2019regularized}. A density coarse-grained
over a finite region or time window also obeys a stochastic
equation \cite{archer2004dynamical}. Many applications
still use Dean's equation for smooth densities without a
rigorous proof
\cite{kruger2017gaussian,demery2016conductivity,
dean2014relaxation,demery2014generalized}, including the
fluctuating theories of active particles
\cite{tailleur2008statistical,solon2015active,bertin2013}.
We, too, assume that \cref{eq:final-langevin} holds for a
smooth $\varrho_t$, and use it in this form from
\cref{sec:model-closure} on.


\subsection{Free energy, closure, and the stochastic field equations}
\label{sec:model-closure}
The joint density $\varrho_t$ carries far more information
than we need. The rest of the paper works with its lowest
angular moments, the fluctuating density and nematic order
parameter, both dimensionless like $\varrho_t$,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:density-definition}
    \rho(\r,t) = \int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta),
    \quad
    \Q(\r,t) = \int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)\,\hat\Q(\theta),
\end{align}
with $\hat\Q(\theta)=2\p(\theta)\p(\theta)-\I$ the
single-rod nematic tensor.%
\footnote{%
The conventional nematic order parameter is normalized by
the local density,
$\Q=\rho^{-1}\!\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\hat\Q$, and is
bounded by unity. We use the unnormalized moment
\cref{eq:density-definition} instead; the two differ only
at order $\rho-1$, and a large bulk modulus keeps $\rho-1$
small (\cref{app:dean-normalisation}).} In this subsection
we reduce \cref{eq:final-langevin} to a closed pair of
stochastic equations for these two fields.

We do not know the interbacterial potential
${V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')}$ in any detail, so we do not
attempt to evaluate \cref{eq:pair-free-energy} from it.
Instead we replace \cref{eq:pair-free-energy} by a
coarse-grained free energy $\calF$ that depends on
$\varrho$ only through the two moments
\cref{eq:density-definition},
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:free-energy-parametrisation}
    \calF[\varrho] = F[\rho,\Q] = \int\!\d\r\;\mathfrak f\bigl(\rho(\r),\Q(\r),\partial_\r\Q(\r)\bigr),
\end{align}
which is both a physical assumption and a choice of
parametrization. It gives a chain rule,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:chain-rule}
    \frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}
    = \frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho(\r)}
    + \frac{\delta F}{\delta\Q(\r)}\!:\!\hat\Q(\theta).
\end{align}
This truncation of the $\theta$ dependence to a single
harmonic is what makes the interaction terms of
\cref{eq:final-langevin} integrate over $\theta$ to the two
moments alone in the equation for the density
(\cref{app:dean-angle}).

For the free energy density $\mathfrak f$ we take the sum
of a compressibility penalty and a Landau--de Gennes
nematic term,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:free-energy-choice}
    \mathfrak f(\rho,\Q,\partial_\r\Q) = {}& \frac B2(\rho-1)^2 + \frac a2(\Q\!:\!\Q)
    + \frac b4(\Q\!:\!\Q)^2
    \nonumber\\
    &+ \frac K2(\partial_\r\Q)\!:\!(\partial_\r\Q).
\end{align}
The first term enforces near-incompressibility of the
monolayer with a large bulk modulus $B$, the stiffness of
the monolayer against compression and the one parameter
that we fit (\cref{sec:nucleation-2}); the rest is the
standard nematic free energy, with $a<0$ favoring order and
$K$ penalizing distortion. The single $K$ is the
one-constant approximation of the Frank free energy, in
which the splay and bend moduli of the two-dimensional
nematic are taken equal
\cite{degennes1993physics,frank1958liquid}.


Integrating \cref{eq:final-langevin} over $\theta$ and
keeping the leading terms in $1/B$ at fixed $B(\rho-1)$
(\cref{app:dean-angle}) gives a continuity equation for the
density,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:model-rho}
  \partial_t\rho = -\nabla\cdot\Bigl[
    \frac1{\xi_0}\bigl(-B\nabla\rho + \Q\!:\!\nabla\H + \bfa\bigr)
    + \bEta_{\rho}(\r,t)\Bigr].
\end{align}
The first two terms in the bracket are passive, inherited
from the pair potential through the free energy. The
isotropic part $-B\nabla\rho$ resists compression, and the
nematic part $\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$ carries cells along
gradients of the nematic force field $\H(\r)=-\delta
F/\delta\Q= -a\Q - b(\Q\!:\!\Q)\Q + K\nabla^2\Q$
\cite{degennes1993physics}. The third term is the active
force density
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:active-force}
 \fa_\alpha(\r) = -\zeta\,\partial_\beta Q_\ab(\r)
  \quad\text{with}\quad
  \zeta = \frac{\xi_0v_0^2}{4\frev},
\end{align}
the divergence of the active nematic stress $-\zeta\Q$.
Since $\zeta>0$, the active stress is extensile. The
coefficient comes entirely from self-propulsion and
reversal, so fast-reversing colonies are less
active.\footnote{For reversing rods that also diffuse
rotationally,
Refs.~\cite{grossmann2016mesoscale,banerjee2026active} find
$\zeta={\xi_0v_0^2}/{(4\frev+2\Lambda)}$. This coefficient
reduces to that of \cref{eq:active-force} when the cells
reverse much faster than they turn, $\frev\gg\Lambda$, the
limit of fast reversals on which the white-noise
approximation of \cref{sec:model-micro} relies.}
%  \todo{$\Lambda/\frev=1.3$ with the parameters of App.~A.2:
% separate the white-noise simplification (valid for large $\frev$) from the parameters taken
% from experiment.}
Unlike the passive terms, $\bfa$ does not derive from the
free energy $F$. Since $\bfa\propto\nabla\cdot\Q$, it
vanishes in a uniform nematic field and is largest where
the field bends fastest, at a defect core
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}).

The last term is the noise of the density flux,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-def}
  \bEta_{\rho}(\r,t) = \int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t(\r,\theta)}\,
  \p(\theta)\,k(\r,\theta,t).
\end{equation}
Its autocorrelation follows from
\cref{eq:noise-correlators},
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation}
  \la \eta^{\rho}_\alpha(\r,t) \,\eta^{\rho}_\beta(\r',t') \ra
  \\
  =
  \frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \left[Q_\ab(\r)+\rho(\r) \delta_\ab\right]
 \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t') ,
\end{multline}
an isotropic piece proportional to $\rho$ and an
anisotropic piece proportional to $\Q$. Both scale with
$\zeta$ of \cref{eq:active-force}, so the noise strength is
set by the same reversal rate $\frev$ and self-propulsion
speed $v_0$ as the active force. The noise $\bEta_{\rho}$
sits inside the divergence, so $\int\!\d\r\,\rho$ is
constant in every realization of the noise, not only in the
mean. The rotational noise does not appear in
\cref{eq:model-rho}: every term it produces is a $\theta$
derivative, and $\int\!\d\theta\,\partial_\theta(\cdot)=0$.

\Cref{eq:model-rho} can be viewed as a force balance:
writing the flux of this continuity equation,
$\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\bs j=0$, as $\bs j=\rho\v$
defines the velocity field $\v(\r,t)$ of the cells, and
setting $\rho\approx1$ in the incompressible limit gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:velocity}
  \xi_0 \v = \bfp + \bfa + \xi_0 \bEta_{\rho},
\end{align}
where $\bfp$ is the passive force density, due to the
interactions between the cells. By
\cref{eq:force-torque-F,eq:chain-rule,eq:free-energy-choice},
a rod of orientation $\theta$ at $\r$ feels the force
$\F(\r,\theta)$, and averaging this force over the
orientations gives $\bfp$,
\begin{align}
  \F(\r,\theta) &= \rho_0^{-1}\bigl(-B\nabla\rho + \hat\Q(\theta)\!:\!\nabla\H\bigr),
  \label{eq:force-on-cells}
  \\
  \bfp = \la \rho_0 \F \ra_\varrho &\simeq -B\nabla\rho + \Q\!:\!\nabla\H ,
  \label{eq:passive-force}
\end{align}
with the average over orientations $\langle \cdot
\rangle_\varrho =
\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)\,(\cdot)$, so that
$\rho=\langle 1 \rangle_\varrho$ and $\Q=\langle \hat\Q
\rangle_\varrho$. The friction force density $\xi_0\v$ on
the cells is thus balanced by the passive force, the active
force and the noise. The passive force can also be written
as the divergence of a stress (\cref{sec:pressure}).


We also need a dynamical equation for the nematic order
parameter $\Q$. Multiplying \cref{eq:final-langevin} by
$\hat\Q(\theta)$ and integrating over $\theta$ does not
close the system: angular moments like
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho
=\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,\hat\Q\hat\Q$ appear, and
$\rho$ and $\Q$ alone do not determine them. We use the
lowest-order maximum-entropy closure,
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho \simeq \rho\,\J$ with
$\J$ the rank-four identity on symmetric traceless tensors.
The terms it drops are of order $S^2$ relative to those it
keeps, where $S=\sqrt{(\Q\!:\!\Q)/2}$ is the strength of
the nematic order. \Cref{app:closure} derives the
higher-order terms and the limitations of this closure. The
closure truncates the hierarchy of angular moments within
one realization of the noise, so the closed equations for
$\rho$ and $\Q$ keep the noise of \cref{eq:final-langevin}.

With the closure in place we obtain an equation for $\Q$
with two noises, $\bEta^\Q$ and $\f^\Q$,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:model-Q}
  \partial_t \Q = {}& -\nabla\cdot\Bigl[\frac1{\xi_0}
    \bigl(-\Q B \nabla\rho + \nabla \H\bigr) + \bEta^\Q\Bigr]
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac4{\xir} \Ha + \f^\Q.
\end{align}
Here $\Ha$ is the nematic force field with coefficients
renormalized by the rotational noise and the activity,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:H-active}
  \Ha &= -a'\Q - b(\Q\!:\!\Q)\Q + K'\nabla^2\Q,
  \nonumber\\
  a' &= a+\Lambda\xir, \qquad K' = K+{\zeta\xir}/({4\xi_0}).
\end{align}
Like $\H=-\delta F/\delta\Q$, the field $\Ha$ is minus the
functional derivative of the effective free energy, which
is $F$ with $a'$ and $K'$ in place of $a$ and $K$. The term
$4\Ha/\xir$ relaxes $\Q$ toward a minimizer of the
effective free energy, so it is $a'$ and $K'$, not the bare
$a$ and $K$, that set the defect profile solved for in
\cref{sec:defect-steady}.

The passive terms in the flux of $\Q$ have the same origin
as those of \cref{eq:model-rho}: the force
\cref{eq:force-on-cells} moves a rod of orientation
$\theta$ with the velocity $\rho_0\F(\r,\theta)/\xi_0$
(\cref{eq:langevin-position}). Averaged over angle, this
velocity gives the passive flux $\bfp/\xi_0$ of the
density, and weighted with $\hat\Q(\theta)$ it gives the
passive flux of $\Q$. The diffusive flux thus carries the
alignment of the rods through $\Q B\nabla\rho$, and the
nematic part of the force carries it through $\nabla\H$.

The two noises of \cref{eq:model-Q} enter differently.
$\bEta^\Q$ is a flux, the rank-three tensor
\begin{equation}
\eta^\Q_{\ab\gamma}=\int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\hat
Q_\ab(\theta)p_\gamma(\theta)k(\r,\theta,t),
\end{equation}
with $\gamma$ the direction of the flux. Both $\bEta_\rho$
and $\bEta^\Q$ carry the same reversal noise
$k(\r,\theta,t)$, so the two are correlated. The second
noise $\f^\Q$ is local and non-conservative. It carries the
rotational noise $\lambda(\r,\theta,t)$ alone and is
uncorrelated with $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$. It is the
only noise that changes the orientation of the rods.
\Cref{app:dean-angle} gives their correlators.

The reversal noise enters the equation for $\Q$ although a
reversal does not change the nematic tensor $\hat\Q$ of a
rod. It enters through the transport of alignment: each rod
carries its alignment as it runs along its axis, and
$\bEta^\Q$ is the random part of this transport, as
$\bEta_\rho$ is the random part of the transport of the
rods. Like $\bEta_\rho$, it sits inside a divergence, so it
moves alignment between neighboring points and leaves
$\int\!\d\r\,\Q$ unchanged in every realization. Such a
conserved noise is weak at long wavelengths, and
Bertin \textit{et al.}~\cite{bertin2013} discarded it as
irrelevant. We keep it, because it comes from the same
reversals as $\bEta_\rho$ and is correlated with it, and
because layers form at the scale of a single cell, at short
wavelengths.

The same equations follow from a theory that keeps the
direction of motion of each cell as a variable
(\cref{sec:model-micro}). Such a theory has a third field,
the local polar order $\bs
P(\r,t)
=\rho_0^{-1}\sum_ik_i\,\p(\theta_i)\,\delta(\r-\r_i(t))$,
with $k_i=\pm1$ the dichotomous variable of
\cref{eq:k-dichotomous}, and $\bs P$ has an equation of its
own~\cite{baskaran2008hydrodynamics,banerjee2026active,
spinney2025dean}. Reversals relax it at the rate $2\frev$,
and splay and bend of the director drive it. In the
white-noise limit (large $\frev$), $\bs P$ follows $\rho$
and $\Q$ instantaneously, and eliminating it gives back
\cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q}. The mean of $\bs P$
contains the polarity $\bs P_Q=-(v_0/4\frev)\nabla\cdot\Q$
that splay and bend induce, and the active force is the
friction force of this polarity, $\bfa=\xi_0v_0\bs P_Q$.
The fluctuation of $\bs P$ about its mean gives the density
noise, $\bEta_\rho=v_0(\bs P-\langle\bs P\rangle)$.

\subsection{Dropping the nematic part of the force}
\label{sec:model-elastic}

With the nematic part $\hat\Q(\theta)\!:\!\nabla\H$ of the
force on a rod, \cref{eq:force-on-cells}, the lowest-order
closure makes \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} ill posed: in
\cref{app:closure} we show that they become antidiffusive
wherever the nematic order is strong, $S>1/\sqrt2$. To
resolve this consistently, we drop this part, which removes
both $\nabla\H$ terms of the passive fluxes at once
(\cref{sec:model-closure}): $\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$ from the flux
of the density and $\nabla\H$ from the flux of $\Q$. The
passive force density then reduces to its isotropic part,
$\bfp=-B\nabla\rho$.

Both $\nabla\H$ terms are usually dropped in continuum

higher order in gradients than the active
ones~\cite{shankar2018low,srivastava2016negative}. Here, in
contrast, the closure forces the simplification on us.


\par

\Cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q}, without their $\nabla\H$
terms, are the fluctuating active nematic equations this
paper solves. Activity enters them in three places: the
force $\bfa$, the amplitude of the reversal noises
$\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$, and the renormalization of the
nematic force field $\Ha$ in \cref{eq:H-active}. The
transport terms leave the shape of a defect, which $\Ha$
sets, almost unchanged, but they couple the density and the
nematic field in both directions, through
$\bfa\propto\nabla\cdot\Q$ in one equation and through $\Q
B\nabla\rho$ in the other. This coupling sets a $+1/2$
defect in motion (\cref{sec:defect-steady}).

\subsection{Passive stress and pressure}
\label{sec:pressure}

The layering rate of \cref{sec:nucleation-1} depends on the
pressure $p$ in the monolayer, which we now define through
the free energy. By \cref{eq:force-torque-F,eq:chain-rule},
the passive force density is
$\bfp=\langle\rho_0\F\rangle_\varrho
=-\rho\nabla\mu_\rho-\Q\!:\!\nabla\bmu_\Q$, with the
chemical potentials $\mu_\rho=\delta
F/\delta\rho=\partial\mathfrak f/\partial\rho$ and
$\bmu_\Q=\delta F/\delta\Q=-\H$; at $\rho\simeq1$ it
reduces to \cref{eq:passive-force}. Expanding
$\partial_\alpha\mathfrak f$ in the derivatives of $\rho$,
$\Q$ and $\partial_\r\Q$ turns this force density into the
divergence of a stress, $\bfp=\nabla\cdot\bsigma$, through
the Gibbs--Duhem relation~\cite{callen1985thermodynamics}
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:gibbs-duhem}
  \rho\,\partial_\alpha\mu_\rho+\Q\!:\!\partial_\alpha\bmu_\Q &= -\partial_\beta\sigma_{\alpha\beta},
  \nonumber\\
  \sigma_{\alpha\beta} &= -\bigl(\rho\mu_\rho+\Q\!:\!\bmu_\Q-\mathfrak f\bigr)\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad-\partial_\alpha\Q\!:\!\frac{\partial\mathfrak f}{\partial(\partial_\beta\Q)} .
\end{align}

The free energy density \cref{eq:free-energy-choice} is the
sum of the compressibility penalty $\mathfrak
f_B(\rho)=\tfrac B2(\rho-1)^2$ and a nematic part
$\mathfrak f_\rn(\Q,\partial_\r\Q)$. Since $\mu_\rho$
depends on $\mathfrak f_B$ alone and $\bmu_\Q$ on
$\mathfrak f_\rn$ alone, the stress splits into an
isotropic and a nematic part,
\begin{align}
  \bsigma &= -p\,\I+\bsigma^\rn,
  \qquad p=\rho\mu_\rho-\mathfrak f_B,
  \nonumber\\
  \sigma^\rn_{\alpha\beta} &= -\bigl(\Q\!:\!\bmu_\Q-\mathfrak f_\rn\bigr)\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}
  -K\,\partial_\alpha\Q\!:\!\partial_\beta\Q ,
\end{align}
and \cref{eq:gibbs-duhem} splits into one relation for each
part. The first, $\rho\nabla\mu_\rho=\nabla p$, is the
Gibbs--Duhem relation of a simple
fluid~\cite{callen1985thermodynamics}. In the second,
$\Q\!:\!\nabla\bmu_\Q=-\nabla\cdot\bsigma^\rn$, the nematic
stress $\bsigma^\rn$ is the sum of an isotropic nematic
pressure and the anisotropic Ericksen
stress~\cite{degennes1993physics,doostmohammadi2018active}.
Since $\bmu_\Q=-\H$, its divergence is the nematic part of
the passive force,
$\nabla\cdot\bsigma^\rn=\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$.

Dropping the nematic part of the force
(\cref{sec:model-elastic}) drops $\bsigma^\rn$ and leaves
$-p\,\I$ as the passive stress, so that $\bfp=-\nabla p$.
With $\mathfrak f_B$, the pressure of the monolayer is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pressure-definition}
  p=\frac B2\left(\rho^2-1\right)\simeq B(\rho-1),
\end{equation}
where the last form is the leading order in $\rho-1$ that
\cref{eq:model-rho} keeps. This is the two-dimensional
pressure $p$ of \cref{eq:dE-of-A} (\cref{app:extrusion}).

\section{Fluctuations around a $+1/2$ defect}
\label{sec:defect}
% dean.tex 681-1266
Solving \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} at a $+1/2$ defect
takes three steps: find the steady state of the noiseless
equations (\cref{sec:defect-steady}); linearize the
stochastic equations around it; and solve the resulting
linear field theory for the equal-time covariance of the
fluctuations (\cref{sec:defect-fluct}).

The main text gives analytical solutions of simplified
equations, which agree well with the numerics discussed in
\cref{app:steady,app:lyapunov}.

\subsection{The steady state}
\label{sec:defect-steady}
% dean.tex 705-747, 819-894, 2460-2597
The density and nematic order fields are cross-coupled in
\cref{eq:model-Q}. This sets the $+1/2$ defect in motion
(see \cref{app:comoving}). We first solve a simplified
system: dropping the cross-coupling term in the
$\Q$-equation leaves a static problem
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:simplified-noiseless}
  0 = \nabla\cdot\left[B\nabla\rho - \bfa\right] , \qquad \Ha = 0.
\end{align}
We use polar coordinates $(r,\phi)$ centered on the core,
with $\phi$ measured from the $+x$ axis, the symmetry axis
of the defect, which points from its head to its tail
(\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)). At a $+1/2$ defect the
director then makes the angle $\theta=\phi/2$ with this
axis. The solution of the second equation is given by a
Pad\'{e} approximation
\cite{pismen1999vortices,pismen2006patterns}:
\begin{align}
  \Q(r,\phi) = S(r)\hat\Q(\phi/2),
  \qquad
  S(r) = S_0 f_\pade(r/\elld),
  \label{eq:Q-pade}\\
  \label{eq:pade-approximation}
  f_\pade(x) = x \sqrt{\frac{0.34 + 0.07 x^2}{1 + 0.41 x^2+0.07 x^4}},
  \\
  S_0=\sqrt{-a'/(2b)}, \qquad \elld=\sqrt{K'/(-a')}.
  \label{eq:S0-elld}
\end{align}
Two scales appear. The far-field nematic order $S_0$ is the
value $S(r)$ saturates to, and the core size $\elld$ is the
distance over which $S$ recovers from $S(0)=0$ at the
defect core. Both are built from the renormalized $a'$ and
$K'$ of \cref{eq:H-active}, not from the bare coefficients
of the free energy density \eqref{eq:free-energy-choice}.
We take $S_0=0.9$ and $\elld=1~\micron$, close to the
direct measurement of Ref.~\cite{han2025local}
(\cref{app:parameters-choice}).

Now, the first equation in \cref{eq:simplified-noiseless}
is a Poisson equation for the density. Its solution needs a
boundary condition, and that condition is a choice. Without
a boundary, the density would not return to one far from
the core. No boundary at a finite distance describes a
defect in an extended colony, and none of the possible
boundaries is more physical than the others. We take the
boundary to be a reservoir: on a circle of radius $R$ we
impose $\rho=1$, as if the region inside the boundary were
embedded in a monolayer held at this density. We take
$R=20~\micron$, comparable to the nematic correlation
length measured in Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}, beyond which
the director field of one defect gives way to that of
others. With this condition, the Poisson equation has the
exact solution
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-analytic}
  \rho(r,\phi) = 1 - \frac{\zeta}{B}\left[ S(r) - \frac rR\,S(R)\right]\cos \phi.
\end{align}
The boundary adds the linear term $r S(R)/R$ of
\cref{eq:rho-analytic}, which pulls the density back to one
at $r=R$. \Cref{app:density-poisson} derives
\cref{eq:rho-analytic} and compares it with a no-flux
boundary, which blocks the flux of cells. In either case
the density departs from uniformity only at order
$\zeta/B$.

Restoring the cross-coupling sets the defect in motion. To
find the direction of the motion we need the active force
at the defect. For the $+1/2$ defect, the Pad\'e profile
\cref{eq:pade-approximation} gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:active-force-defect}
 \fa_x(r, \phi) = -\zeta \left(S'(r) +S(r)/r\right),
  \qquad\fa_y = 0,
\end{align}
a vector field along the defect axis with no transverse
component. It points along $-x$, toward the head of the
defect (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)). The active force
moves the cells, not the defect. The cells build up a
density gradient until the flux driven by the gradient
cancels the active flux, $B\nabla\rho\simeq\bfa$
(cf.~\cref{eq:velocity}). Within the closure of
\cref{sec:model-closure}, however, the nematic order
couples to the density gradient and not to the active
force. The cross-coupling term of \cref{eq:model-Q} carries
the nematic order with the velocity $-B\nabla\rho/\xi_0$,
against the active force, so the defect moves along $+x$,
toward its tail (\cref{app:comoving}).

In experiments, $+1/2$ defects move the other way, toward
their head (Supplementary Note of
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}). The difference comes from the
closure approximation. Self-propulsion also transports
nematic order: with reversals, each rod diffuses along its
own axis and carries its alignment with it. At the tail of
the defect the rods lie along the axis and carry their
alignment into the core, whereas at the head they lie
across the axis, so this transport moves the defect toward
its head. The lowest-order closure drops it, although it is
comparable to the cross-coupling term $QB\nabla\rho$ in
\cref{eq:model-Q}. In \cref{app:drift}, we recover the term
we dropped and we show that the transport outweighs the
cross-coupling, and the defect moves toward its head as in
experiments.


A drifting defect has no steady state in the laboratory
frame. \Cref{app:comoving} therefore solves the noiseless
\cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} numerically in a frame
comoving with the core, retaining the cross-coupling. The
closed form \cref{eq:rho-analytic} fixes $\Q$ to the Pad\'e
profile \cref{eq:pade-approximation} and solves only the
density equation, whereas the numerics solve both fields
together and let $\Q$ depart from that profile.
\Cref{fig:rho1d-dirichlet} shows the resulting density
along the defect axis against \cref{eq:rho-analytic}.

\subsection{Linearization and the fluctuation spectrum}
\label{sec:defect-fluct}
% dean.tex 748-818, 895-1266
In the frame comoving with the defect, let
$\delta\rho(\r,t)=\rho(\r,t)-\rho_\ss(\r)$ and
$\delta\Q(\r,t)=\Q(\r,t)-\Q_\ss(\r)$ be the fluctuations
about its steady state (\cref{sec:defect-steady}).
Linearizing \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} in this frame
around the steady state gives (\cref{app:dean-angle})
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:linearized}
\begin{align}
    &\partial_t\delta\rho =-\nabla\cdot\Bigl[
    \frac1{\xi_0}\bigl(-B\nabla\delta\rho -\zeta\,\nabla\cdot\delta\Q \bigr) + \bEta_\rho(\r,t)\Bigr],
    \label{eq:linearized-rho}
    \\
    &\partial_t\delta\Q = \frac B{\xi_0}\nabla\cdot(\Q_\ss\nabla\delta\rho)
    + \nabla\cdot(\delta\Q\,\bs w_\ss) + \frac4{\xir}\delta\Ha
    \nonumber\\
    &\qquad\qquad
    {}- \nabla\cdot\bEta^\Q(\r,t)+ \f^\Q(\r,t),
    \label{eq:linearized-Q}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
with $\u$ the velocity of the comoving frame, $\bs
w_\ss\equiv\u+(B/\xi_0)\nabla\rho_\ss$, so that $\delta\Q$
is transported with velocity $-\bs w_\ss$, and $\delta\Ha$
the linearization of \cref{eq:H-active} at $\Q_\ss$. The
three noises are those of \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q}
with their amplitudes evaluated on the steady state. The
dependence of the amplitudes on $\delta\rho$ and $\delta\Q$
would add products of a fluctuation and a noise, which are
of second order in the fluctuations, so the linearized
equations carry additive noise. \Cref{eq:linearized}
defines a linear stochastic field theory on the background
$(\rho_\ss,\Q_\ss)$. \Cref{app:lyapunov} obtains the
equal-time covariance of $\delta\rho$ and $\delta\Q$
numerically, by solving a Lyapunov equation.

The main text presents the analytically tractable case, in
which we neglect nematic order fluctuations ($\delta\Q=0$)
and only the density fluctuates. This drops from
\cref{eq:linearized-rho} the force
$-\zeta\nabla\cdot\delta\Q$, which is not small: it
fluctuates more strongly than the mean active force
(\cref{app:lyapunov-results}). But $\delta\Q$ relaxes
nearly four orders of magnitude more slowly than the
density at the scale of a cell, so the density follows this
force quasi-statically, as it follows the mean active force
in \cref{eq:rho-analytic}, and shifts only by about
$(\zeta/B)\,\delta\Q$, far less than the fluctuations that
$\bEta_\rho$ drives.

\Cref{eq:linearized-Q} then drops out; $\bEta_\rho$ is the
only source of the fluctuations. The steady density departs
from unity only at order $\zeta/B$
(\cref{eq:rho-analytic}), so we use a flat density profile
in the noise autocorrelation
\eqref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation}:
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-ss}
  \la \eta^{\rho}_\alpha(\r,t) \,\eta^{\rho}_\beta(\r',t') \ra
  \\
  =\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \left[Q^\ss_\ab(\r)+\delta_\ab\right]
 \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t').
\end{multline}
The defect then enters \cref{eq:linearized-rho} in exactly
one place, through the $\Q_\ss$ term in
\cref{eq:eta-rho-ss}, so it reaches $\delta\rho$ through
the noise amplitude alone, not through the relaxation rate.

Taking the Fourier transform of \cref{eq:linearized-rho}
over space and time gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-fourier}
  \delta\tilde\rho(\k,\omega) =
  -\frac{\ii\,\k\cdot\tilde \bEta_\rho(\k,\omega)}{\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2},
  \qquad \gamma_0 \equiv B/\xi_0,
\end{align}
with $\k$ the wavevector and $k=|\k|$. The noise is
conservative, so its power carries a factor $k^2$ that
cancels the $k^2$ of the relaxation rate $\gamma_0k^2$. The
spectrum is then flat in $\k$, and the equal-time variance
$\langle\delta\rho(\r,t)\delta\rho(\r',t)\rangle_\ss$
diverges as $\r'\to\r$.

The divergence comes from treating the monolayer as a
continuum down to arbitrarily short length scales. The
theory therefore needs a cutoff, and the system supplies
one: there is no length shorter than the width of a cell.
We therefore set
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cutoff}
    \elln = 0.5~\micron.
\end{equation}
We implement the cutoff by suppressing wavevectors above
$1/\elln$ with a Gaussian filter, multiplying
\cref{eq:rho-fourier} by $\ee^{-k^2\elln^2/4}$. Everything
below carries that factor.

Consider first the isotropic disordered case $\Q_\ss=0$.
Inverting the smoothed \cref{eq:rho-fourier} over space and
time gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic}
  \la\delta\rho(\r,t)\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra =
  \frac{\zeta}{2\pi B\rho_0 \elln^2}
  \frac{
      \exp\!\!\left(\!-\frac{|\r-\r'|^2}{2 \elln^2+4 \gamma_0 |t-t'|}\right)
      }{1 + 2 \gamma_0 |t-t'| / \elln^2},
\end{align}
so that the equal-point, equal-time variance is the
constant pedestal
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:pedestal}
  \sigmarho(\r) = \sigmarho\big|_\text{pedestal}
  = \frac{\zeta}{2\pi B\rho_0\elln^2}.
\end{align}
The square root of the pedestal, \cref{eq:pedestal}, is the
standard deviation of the density, $\sigma_\rho \sim
B^{-1/2}$, whereas the scale of the modulation of the
average density in \cref{eq:rho-analytic} is $B^{-1}$.
Thus, in the large-$B$ limit the average density profile is
overwhelmed by the fluctuations.

\Cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic} also gives the two
scales over which a density fluctuation stays coherent: a
correlation length equal to the cutoff $\elln$ and a
correlation time
\begin{align}
 \label{eq:tau-c}
 \tau_c = \frac{\elln^2}{2 \gamma_0},
\end{align}
the time a fluctuation of size $\elln$ takes to diffuse
away. With the parameters of \cref{app:parameters},
$\tau_c\simeq0.4~\mathrm{ms}$, far shorter than the
correlation time of the reversal noise,
$1/(2\frev)=5~\minute$ (\cref{eq:k-dichotomous}), so at the
scale $\elln$ the white-noise limit of
\cref{sec:model-micro} does not hold.
\Cref{eq:layering-rate-approximation}, moreover, requires
the pressure to be a slow variable, which changes slowly
compared with the extrusion of a cell
(\cref{sec:nucleation-1}). With white noise, however, the
pressure in the model changes within $\tau_c$, whereas the
bottom layer needs about
$\Acell/(2\gamma_0)\simeq5~\mathrm{ms}$ to relax around an
extruded cell. Real cells push in one direction for minutes
between reversals and cross $\elln$ in about half a minute,
so the pressure they build would change slowly enough. The
time scales thus separate for real cells but not in the
model, as a consequence of the white-noise limit, which we
keep as a deliberate simplification
(\cref{sec:model-assumptions}).


Restoring the anisotropic piece of \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss},
the one that carries $\Q_\ss$, gives the total density
variance (\cref{app:variance})
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:total-gauss}
  \frac{\sigmarho(\r)}{\sigmarho|_\text{pedestal}}
  = 1 + \bigl\langle S\cos2(\theta_\text{nb}-\theta_{\bs d})\bigr\rangle_\Omega .
\end{equation}
The average runs over the neighbors $\r+\bs d$ of the point
$\r$, $\langle g\rangle_\Omega=\int\!\d^2\bs
d\,\Omega(d)\,g(\r+\bs d)$. Here $S$ is the strength of the
steady nematic order at the neighbor, $\theta_\text{nb}$
the angle of its director with the $x$ axis, and
$\theta_{\bs d}$ the angle of $\bs d$ with the same axis
(\cref{fig:neighbors}(a)). A neighbor thus counts with $+S$
when its rods point at $\r$ and with $-S$ when they lie
across the line to $\r$. The weight depends only on the
distance $d$ to the neighbor,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:omega-weight}
  \Omega(d)=\frac{\elln^2}{2\pi d^4}\Bigl[2-\ee^{-X}\bigl(X^2+2X+2\bigr)\Bigr],
  \quad
  X=\frac{2d^2}{\elln^2}.
\end{align}
It is positive and normalized over the plane,
$\int\!\d^2\bs d\,\Omega(d)=1$, and since $\Omega(0)=0$,
the point $\r$ itself does not count
(\cref{fig:neighbors}(b)).

\Cref{eq:total-gauss} is the central result of
\cref{sec:defect}. The defect modulates the variance around
a uniform pedestal. The pedestal is the variance of a
uniform monolayer, fixed by the noise amplitude $\zeta$,
the stiffness $B$ and the resolution $\elln$. We evaluate
\cref{eq:total-gauss} for two nematic fields $\Q_\ss$: the
static Pad\'e profile \cref{eq:Q-pade} and the steady state
of the moving defect obtained numerically in
\cref{sec:defect-steady}, whose director lines
\cref{fig:nematicaxis} compares. \Cref{fig:variance} shows
both results along the axis of the defect and across it. We
call the point where the variance is largest
$\r_\mathrm{max}$, and its relative height above the
pedestal,
$\nu_0\equiv[\sigmarho(\r_\mathrm{max})/\sigmarho(\infty)]-1$,
the peak modulation.


The variance differs between head and tail because a cell
runs back and forth along its own axis. It moves density
into and out of the points ahead of it and behind it, but
not those beside it, and this sets the sign with which a
neighbor counts in \cref{eq:total-gauss}
(\cref{fig:neighbors}(a)). In a uniform nematic, the
neighbors that raise the variance above the pedestal and
those that lower it below the pedestal balance. Around the
defect, however, the director lines bend around the core
and break this balance, in favor of the neighbors that
raise the variance on the head side of the defect and of
those that lower it on its tail side. The caption of
\cref{fig:aimed-rods} explains how, panel by panel.

The weight $\Omega$ reaches over a distance proportional to
$\elln$, so the peak modulation $\nu_0$ decreases with
decreasing cutoff $\elln$ and vanishes at $\elln=0$
(\cref{app:variance-neighbors}). The defect therefore
modulates the variance only because the density is resolved
at the width of a cell, which makes $\elln$, the shortest
length of the model, a physical parameter. Strictly, a
continuum theory is not applicable, because the variance
changes over length scales comparable to the cutoff
length $\elln$. Even for an ideal gas, the density resolved
over less than one particle per cutoff area is far from
Gaussian~\cite{velenich2008brownian}, and here
$\rho_0\elln^2\simeq0.06$. Copenhagen \textit{et
al.}~\cite{copenhagen2021} nevertheless described the flows
around these defects successfully with a continuum
active-nematic theory. Fitted to the velocity measured
along the defect axis, the theory reproduced it near the
defects at length scales smaller than the length of one
cell.
% copenhagen2021 p. 213 and Fig. 4f: midline fits give a healing length of about 2.5 um at both
% +-1/2 defects; the +1/2 fit departs from the data only ahead of the defect (x < -10 um).
We likewise apply the continuum theory, with its Gaussian
statistics, down to $\elln$ as a deliberate simplification
(\cref{sec:model-assumptions}), and compare its prediction
with experiment in \cref{sec:nucleation-2}.

In \cref{fig:variance} we also show the density variance
from the full numerical solution of \cref{eq:linearized},
retaining nematic order fluctuations ($\delta\Q\ne0$,
\cref{app:lyapunov}), and \cref{fig:variance3d}(a) shows
its two-dimensional profile. The solver imposes the cutoff
differently: instead of suppressing high wavevectors, it
correlates the noise in space over the length $\elln$
(\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff}), which gives the same variance
when $\delta\Q=0$. Since the width of a cell sets $\elln$
(\cref{eq:cutoff}), the correlated noise also models the
discreteness of the cells. The analytic result of
\cref{eq:total-gauss} already captures its main features.

Evaluated on the steady state of the moving defect instead
of the Pad\'e profile (see \cref{fig:nematicaxis}),
\cref{eq:total-gauss} follows the numerical solution
closely, along the axis and across it (dotted in
\cref{fig:variance}), so the slow force of $\delta\Q$
indeed changes the variance little. Most of the small
remaining difference comes from the grid of the solver, and
it shrinks as the grid is refined
(\cref{app:lyapunov-results}). The motion of the defect
deforms the nematic field, and this deformation makes the
modulation unequal between head and tail
(\cref{fig:aimed-rods}(b,d)) and also lifts the modulation
above zero at the core. Seen from the core, the rods on the
tail side point at it and raise the variance at the core,
and those on the head side lie across the line to it and
lower the variance there (\cref{fig:aimed-rods}(c)). On the
static Pad\'e profile the two halves cancel, but in the
moving defect the tail side, where the order is stronger,
outweighs the head side. Both the head--tail asymmetry and
the raised core thus come from the motion of the defect,
whose direction the lowest-order closure of
\cref{sec:model-closure} sets (\cref{sec:defect-steady}).
Relaxing the closure approximation should therefore change
the modulation quantitatively, but we believe not
qualitatively.


A modulation of two to three percent is the entire
position\nobreakdash-dependent signal in the density
variance profile. In \cref{sec:nucleation-2} we show that
it modulates the layering rate by two orders of magnitude.

\section{Layer formation rate from the pressure fluctuations}
\label{sec:nucleation-2}
% dean.tex 1392-1487
\Cref{sec:nucleation-1} reduced the layering rate
\eqref{eq:layering-rate-approximation} to the probability
that the pressure at a point exceeds the critical pressure
$\pcrit$, and \cref{sec:model,sec:defect} gave the variance
of the density around a $+1/2$ defect
(\cref{sec:defect-fluct}). We can now compute the layering
rate and compare its profile with the measurements.

By \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, a density fluctuation
$\delta\rho$ changes the pressure by $B\,\delta\rho$. In
the linear theory, $\delta\rho$ is Gaussian with the
variance of \cref{eq:total-gauss}, so the pressure $p$ is
Gaussian too, with
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sigma-p}
    \sigma_p^2(\r) = B^2\sigmarho(\r),
    \qquad
    \sigma_{p,\infty}^2 \equiv\sigma_p^2(\infty)= \frac{B\zeta}{2\pi\rho_0\elln^2},
\end{equation}
the second expression its far-field value, from the
isotropic pedestal \cref{eq:pedestal}. As we argued for the
average density, in the large-$B$ limit the mean pressure
is $\langle p(\r) \rangle \ll \sigma_p(\r)$. We therefore
set it to zero.%
\footnote{Far in the tail of the distribution, the mean
shifts the exponent by $\pcrit|\langle
p\rangle|/\sigma_{p,\infty}^2\lesssim0.14$, which changes
the probability by less than $15\%$, far less than the
modulation of the variance does. The nematic stresses of
\cref{sec:pressure}, dropped with the nematic part of the
force in \cref{sec:model-elastic}, are of the same order as
$\langle p\rangle$: on the Pad\'e profile
\cref{eq:pade-approximation}, with the bare $a$, $b$ and
$K$ (\cref{app:parameters}), every component stays below
about $6~\Pa\tcdot\micron$.} The probability in
\cref{eq:layering-rate-approximation} is then a Gaussian
tail, and the layering rate is
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:p-layer}
    \calJ(\r) &\simeq\calJ_{\!\Acell} \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\,z(\r)}\ee^{-z(\r)^2/2},
    \quad
    z(\r)= \frac{\pcrit}{\sigma_p(\r)}.
\end{align}


The comparison with experiment is not to an absolute
layering rate but to a \emph{ratio}: Copenhagen \textit{et
al.}~\cite{copenhagen2021} report the distribution of
distances between defects and new layers, normalized by the
same distribution for random points in the monolayer. In
our case, the ratio of layering rates, up to the slowly
varying prefactor $1/(\sqrt{2\pi}\,z)$ of
\cref{eq:p-layer}, is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:R-ratio}
    \frac{\calJ(\r)}{\calJ(\infty)} \simeq
    \exp\!\left[\frac{z_\infty^2}2\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{p,\infty}^2}{\sigma_p^2(\r)}\right)\right],
    \quad z_\infty \equiv \frac{\pcrit}{\sigma_{p,\infty}}.
\end{equation}
Only the \emph{relative} modulation of the variance
matters, and the large factor $z_\infty^2/2$, with
$z_\infty\simeq21$ for the parameters of
\cref{app:parameters} and the bulk modulus $B$ fitted
below, amplifies it. A modulation of a few percent can
therefore produce an enhancement of two orders of
magnitude. The numerical variance of \cref{eq:linearized},
on the steady state of the moving defect, peaks at
$\nu_0\simeq2.4\%$, off the core at the head of the defect
(\cref{fig:variance}), and \cref{eq:R-ratio} then gives the
peak enhancement
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:R-value}
    \frac{\calJ(\r_\mathrm{max})}{\calJ(\infty)}
    = \exp\!\left[\frac{z_\infty^2}2\,\frac{\nu_0}{1+\nu_0}\right] \simeq 1.7\times10^2 ,
\end{equation}
since the layering rate, like the variance, is largest at
$\r_\mathrm{max}$. Here we used the parameters of
\cref{app:parameters} and chose the bulk modulus $B$ so
that this peak enhancement is close to $2\times10^2$, the
enhancement that Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} measures near
$+1/2$ defects (its Fig.~3f).

rather than a measured constant, and it fixes only the

the layer height, span $0.05$ to $10^4~\kPa\tcdot\micron$.

the moduli measured for bacterial colonies and of the order
of the stiffness of single cells
(\cref{app:parameters-choice}). The rest of the profile is
not fitted; it follows from the steady state $\Q_\ss$ of
the defect and the cutoff $\elln$, as \cref{eq:total-gauss}
shows.

The absolute rate of layering, in contrast, is beyond the
reach of the model, because it requires a model of the
extrusion of patches, which would give the single-cell
extrusion rate $\calJ_{\!\Acell}$ that
\cref{sec:nucleation-1} leaves unmodeled. With the
parameters of \cref{app:parameters}, $z_\infty\simeq21$, so
the Gaussian tail of \cref{eq:p-layer} makes the layering

exponent $z_\infty^2/2$ that amplifies a modulation of a
few percent into an enhancement of two orders of magnitude
also makes the absolute rate so sensitive that a small
variation in $\sigma_{p,\infty}$ or $\pcrit$ changes it by
many orders of magnitude. Both quantities carry the
approximations of the model: the white-noise limit of the
reversals, Gaussian statistics at the scale of a cell, and
the idealized nucleus. We speculate, moreover, that the
single-cell extrusion rate could itself be enhanced near
topological defects, which
\cref{eq:layering-rate-approximation} neglects; such an
enhancement would raise the layering rate there further.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Layers in \textit{M.\ xanthus} monolayers form
preferentially at $+1/2$ defects~\cite{copenhagen2021}. We
described layer formation as nucleation: the energy cost of
extruding a patch of cells sets a critical area, above
which an extruded patch grows into a new layer, and this
cost depends on the fluctuating pressure in the monolayer.
In our simplified picture, only single cells are extruded,
and a layer forms when the pressure exceeds the critical
pressure $\pcrit$, above which the critical area is smaller
than one cell. Since $\pcrit$ and the single-cell extrusion
rate are the same everywhere in the model, a defect
favors layers only because the pressure fluctuates
differently around it. We derived these fluctuations by
coarse-graining self-propelled rods that reverse, keeping
their noise in Dean's equation. The density variance
(\cref{fig:variance,fig:variance3d}) is raised by a few
percent at the head of a $+1/2$ defect
(\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)) and lowered at its tail.
In the far tail of the pressure distribution, this small
change is amplified exponentially, so that the layering
rate at the head of the defect rises by two orders of
magnitude, like the measured layering rate near $+1/2$
defects (\cref{fig:defects-layering}(c),
\cref{eq:R-value}).

The comparison has one fitted parameter, the bulk modulus
$B$. The mean pressure stays of the order of the active
stress $\zeta$ for any $B$, whereas the pressure variance
grows as $B\zeta$ (\cref{eq:sigma-p}). The fluctuations can
therefore reach the threshold when the mean cannot. The
shape of the variance profile, in contrast, is not fitted.
Due to self-propulsion, each cell is displaced along its
own axis, so the noise in the flux of cells is stronger
along the director than across it (\cref{eq:eta-rho-ss}).
Following the director field of the defect, this
anisotropic noise places the peak of the variance off the
core and makes the modulation decay over a few micrometers
(\cref{eq:total-gauss}).

\subsection{Model simplifications}
\label{sec:model-assumptions}

We have aimed at the simplest description that still
captures the mechanism. It rests on a coarse-grained
Landau--de Gennes free energy with a single Frank constant
in place of the unknown pair potential of the cells
(\cref{sec:model-closure}), on Dean's equation applied to a
smooth density (\cref{sec:model-coarse}), and on a friction
with the substrate that is the same along and across the
cells (\cref{sec:model-micro}), unlike the anisotropic
friction of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}. Several other
simplifications are crude, but improving them would make
the model more elaborate, so we keep them deliberately.

The first is the lowest-order closure of the angular
moments in the field equations. It is inaccurate where the
nematic order is strong, away from the defect core
(\cref{app:closure}), and the higher-order terms it drops
reverse the direction in which the defect drifts
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}), on which the head--tail
asymmetry of the variance depends
(\cref{sec:defect-fluct}). The closure also requires us to
drop the nematic part of the force on the cells, so that
the pressure is the only passive stress
(\cref{sec:model-elastic,sec:pressure}). This breaks the
Onsager symmetry of the passive transport: the
pressure-driven flow still carries the alignment of the
cells, but gradients of the nematic force field no longer
move the cells. Despite the broken symmetry, the model
stays consistent: since it is active, its free energy need
not decrease in time, and since the passive transport has
no noise of its own, no fluctuation--dissipation relation
ties it to the noise. The equations without the nematic
part of the force also remain linearly stable in a uniform
nematic state (\cref{app:closure}).

Like the closure, the white-noise limit of the reversals
serves to close the theory on the density and the nematic
order (\cref{sec:model-micro}). The limit requires the
cells to reverse faster than the density relaxes, but at
the scale of a cell the density relaxes within a
millisecond, whereas the cells reverse every few minutes.
As a result, the pressure in the model changes before the
bottom layer can relax around an extruded cell, although
\cref{eq:layering-rate-approximation} requires it to change
slowly compared with the extrusion of a cell
(\cref{sec:nucleation-1}). Real cells, in contrast, push in
one direction for minutes, so the pressure they build would
change slowly enough (\cref{sec:defect-fluct}).

The linear theory makes the density Gaussian, and we use
this Gaussian even far in its tail, at the scale $\elln$ of
a cell. The central limit theorem would make the density
Gaussian only over an area that holds many cells, whereas
$\elln^2$ holds on average less than
one~\cite{velenich2008brownian}. Even over an area with
many cells, it describes fluctuations near the mean, not
far in the tail.

Finally, the layering criterion idealizes the nucleus as a
disk of one cell area, which keeps it independent of the
orientation of the rod-shaped cells, and it assumes that
single cells are extruded at a uniform rate and grow into a
layer whenever the pressure at a point exceeds $\pcrit$
(\cref{sec:nucleation-1}).

The white-noise limit, the Gaussian tail and the idealized
nucleus keep the theory closed and linear and the layering
criterion simple, so that the variance around the defect,
and from it the layering rate, can be computed analytically
and interpreted. They affect the absolute rate of layering
most, which depends exponentially on $(\pcrit/\sigma_p)^2$,
with $\sigma_p$ the standard deviation of the pressure
(\cref{eq:sigma-p}). The ratio of the layering rates at the
defect and far from it is less sensitive, and this is why
we compare only this ratio with experiment, although it too
rests on the Gaussian tail of the pressure distribution.

\subsection{Outlook and future work}
\label{sec:overlook-and-future-work}

The nucleation criterion is only one way to turn the
statistics of the pressure into a layering rate. In growing
biofilms and colonies, a rod instead tilts out of the plane
once the compression exceeds a threshold that grows with
its length~\cite{beroz2018verticalization,you2019mono}, and
in three-dimensional models of cell monolayers extrusion
follows a local build-up of
stress~\cite{monfared2023mechanical}; such criteria could
be applied to the same density and pressure fluctuations.
% beroz2018verticalization pp. 955-956; you2019mono (arXiv:1811.08875v2) abstract and pp. 3-5;
% monfared2023mechanical pp. 6-7.

In the coarse-grained equations the same coefficient
$\zeta=\xi_0v_0^2/(4\frev)$ of \cref{eq:active-force} sets
the active stress and the amplitude of the density noise,
so the reversal rate controls both. The factor
$z_\infty^2=\pcrit^2/\sigma_{p,\infty}^2$ in the exponent
of \cref{eq:R-ratio} is therefore inversely proportional to
$\zeta$ at fixed $B$ and $\elln$ (\cref{eq:sigma-p}), and
for a fixed defect profile the logarithm of the enhancement
is proportional to $\frev/v_0^2$. Slower reversals then
make layers more frequent everywhere, but less concentrated
at defects. Layers do become more frequent when cells
reverse less often. Han \textit{et al.}~\cite{han2025local}
observed this with a \textit{frzE} mutant, whose cells
reverse about once every two hours instead of every few
minutes~\cite{blackhart1985frizzy}. Its colonies have a
larger area covered by multilayers, and traction
fluctuations more than twice those of reversing cells. A
non-reversing strain also forms multilayers at high cell
density~\cite{peruani2012collective}. Such rare or absent
reversals lie even farther outside the white-noise limit,
so these observations support the trend rather than the
quantitative prediction. That layering then also becomes
less concentrated at defects has not been measured, and
layering around defects at several reversal rates would
test it.

In future work we will improve the crudest simplifications
of \cref{sec:model-assumptions}. With the approach of
Refs.~\cite{bressloff2024global,spinney2025dean}, the model
can keep the dichotomous noise of the reversals, with its
finite correlation time, instead of its white-noise limit,
which would also change the amplitude of the fluctuations.
We also plan to model the extrusion of patches of cells
instead of assuming single cells extruded at the rate
$\calJ_{\!\Acell}$, for example with a Langevin equation
for the area of an extruded patch that moves over the
fluctuating barrier of \cref{fig:nucleation}(b).

Further experiments can test the model. We plan to measure
the angular profile of layer formation around $+1/2$
defects and compare it with the profile that the model
predicts (\cref{fig:variance3d}), with its maximum off the
core, at the head of the defect. Traction measurements
could also test the stress fluctuations of the model, but
the current ones are not accurate enough for a quantitative
comparison.

A topological defect may thus select where new layers form
through the fluctuations of the pressure rather than
through its mean, and the self-propulsion and reversals of
single cells set those fluctuations.

\section{Parameters}
\label{app:parameters}
% dean.tex 543-680 (field equations), 1338-1369 (critical pressure), 2451-2459 (relaxation rate);
% values: claude_experiments/params_new.py, fit_new.py
The model has two groups of parameters. The parameters of
\cref{app:parameters-extrusion} enter only the critical
pressure, whereas the parameters of
\cref{app:parameters-choice} set the steady state and the
variance. The two groups meet in the fitted bulk modulus:
at fixed enhancement, \cref{eq:sigma-p,eq:R-value} give
$B\propto\rho_0\pcrit^2\elln^2\nu_0/[(1+\nu_0)\zeta]$.

The surface tension dominates the critical pressure. We
take $\gammaw=65~\kPa\tcdot\micron$, measured by pendant
drop for the growth medium of
\textit{M.\ xanthus}~\cite{bourque2025active}. Clean water
has $72~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{vargaftik1983}, and the
supernatant of a wild-type culture, which holds the
surfactant the cells excrete~\cite{dworkin1983b,islam2020},
has about $60~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{islam2020}. Since
the surface tension exceeds $\Ws$ by more than an order of
magnitude, the wetting film, and not the adhesion, holds
the monolayer flat.

We set $\Wc=0$. Neighboring cells attract each other
through their overlapping water
menisci~\cite{black2025capillary}, but this capillary
cohesion is the surface energy of the water film, which
\cref{eq:dE-of-A} already counts. We neglect the cohesion
of wild-type cells through extracellular
fibrils~\cite{shimkets1986correlation,
behmlander1994biochemical,pelling2005nanoscale}.

For the adhesion to the substrate we rely on other
bacteria. Their contact-angle data give at most
$3.8~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{bayoudh2006quantification},
whereas detaching one \textit{E.\ coli} cell from glass
costs only $0.035~\kPa\tcdot\micron$ when spread over
$\Acell$~\cite{duanisassaf2024factors}, and we take
$\Ws=2~\kPa\tcdot\micron$ within this range. The size of a
cell sets the remaining two parameters: $h=0.5~\micron$,
the thickness of one cell layer~\cite{han2025local}, and
$\Acell=3~\micron^2$, the footprint of a rod $6~\micron$
long and $0.5~\micron$ wide. With these values,
\cref{eq:pcrit} gives $\pcrit=35.3~\kPa\tcdot\micron$.


\subsection{Parameters of the field equations}
\label{app:parameters-choice}
% Source: jobscripts/set2_2_comove_optimized_2/params.md; numbers: claude_experiments/newset_numbers.py

\Cref{tab:field-params} collects the parameters of the
field equations. Most of them have been measured, but the
reported values spread widely, because they depend on the
experimental conditions and change in time. In the
experiments of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}, sparse cells
glide at $0.92~\micron/\minute$ and reverse at
$0.17~\minute^{-1}$, whereas isolated cells glide at about
$2~\micron/\minute$ and reverse every
$6.8~\minute$~\cite{blackhart1985frizzy}, and cells of a
non-reversing strain glide at
$3.1~\micron/\minute$~\cite{peruani2012collective}. Under
starvation, cells glide faster and reverse less often as
development proceeds: over $15~\mathrm h$ the mean speed
rises from $1.7$ to $2.7~\micron/\minute$, while the
reversal frequency falls from $0.10$ to
$0.045~\minute^{-1}$~\cite{jelsbak2002pattern,
liu2019selfdriven}. Since $\zeta=\xi_0v_0^2/(4\frev)$,
these two changes alone raise $\zeta$ sixfold. Cells in
large groups also reverse less often than isolated
cells~\cite{shi1996cell}, and the density rises as the
cells aggregate. Where the reports differ, we therefore
choose a value within their range.

We take the density $\rho_0=0.25~\micron^{-2}$ from
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}, and the friction coefficient
$\xi_0=5~\Pa\tcdot\minute/\micron$ from
Ref.~\cite{han2025local}, which estimates it in two ways
(its Supplementary Fig.~S12 and Sec.~II.C).

The reported values of $\zeta/\xi_0$ span
$0.5$--$5~\micron^2/\minute$~\cite{han2025local,
copenhagen2021}. Instead of picking one, we use
\cref{eq:active-force}, which ties $\zeta$ to the motility
of single cells:
$v_0=1~\micron/\minute$~\cite{copenhagen2021} and
$\frev=0.1~\minute^{-1}$~\cite{liu2019selfdriven} give
$\zeta/\xi_0=v_0^2/(4\frev)=2.5~\micron^2/\minute$, and
hence $\zeta=12.5~\Pa\tcdot\micron$.

The defect core has been measured in two ways. Fits of the
flow around $+1/2$ defects give $\elld\simeq2.5~\micron$ in
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}, with $S_0\simeq1$ from a
separate measurement (its Supplementary Note), and
$\elld\simeq2.0~\micron$ in Ref.~\cite{han2025local}.
Ref.~\cite{han2025local} also measures the nematic field
directly, from the texture of bright-field images, and
finds $\elld\simeq0.8~\micron$, with $S$ rising to $0.9$
before it decays far away from the defect. We set
$\elld=1~\micron$, close to the direct measurement, and
$S_0=0.9$.

We take the rotational diffusion coefficient
$\Lambda=0.13~\minute^{-1}$, which
Ref.~\cite{liu2019selfdriven} infers from the decorrelation
of isolated cells. The rotational friction has not been
measured, so we estimate it from $\xi_0$ as for a rigid rod
of length $L$ with uniform friction, $\xir=\xi_0L^2/12$,
which gives $\xir=15~\Pa\tcdot\micron\tcdot\minute$ for
$L=6~\micron$. With these values the rotational noise of
\cref{eq:langevin-angle} is far from equilibrium: at
$T=20^\circ\mathrm C$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rotational-fdt}
  \frac{\Lambda\xir}{\rho_0\kB T}\simeq1.9\times10^3,
\end{equation}
with $\kB$ the Boltzmann constant, whereas in equilibrium
the fluctuation-dissipation theorem would make this ratio
one~\cite{kubo1966fluctuation}.

\Cref{eq:S0-elld} gives only two relations among the
coefficients $a'$, $b$ and $K'$ of the nematic free energy,
and we fix the third with the rate at which the nematic
order relaxes. A small perturbation of a uniform $\Q$ with
$\Q\!:\!\Q=2S_0^2$, relaxing through the local term
$\partial_t\Q=4\Ha/\xir$ of \cref{eq:model-Q}, obeys
(cf.~\cref{eq:dH-active})
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:relaxation-rate}
  \partial_t\delta\Q=\frac4{\xir}\bigl[-(a'+b\,\Q\!:\!\Q)\,\delta\Q
  -2b\,(\Q\!:\!\delta\Q)\,\Q\bigr],
\end{equation}
so that a perturbation parallel to $\Q$ decays at the rate
$\gamma_Q=16bS_0^2/\xir=-8a'/\xir$.

The model bounds this rate from below. The bare free energy
\cref{eq:free-energy-choice} is stable only for $K>0$,
which by \cref{eq:H-active} requires
$K'>\zeta\xir/(4\xi_0)$, and with $K'=-a'\elld^2$ from
\cref{eq:S0-elld} this gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gamma-bound}
  \gamma_Q=\frac{8K'}{\xir\elld^2}>\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\elld^2}=5~\minute^{-1}.
\end{equation}
We choose $\gamma_Q=10~\minute^{-1}$, so the nematic field
relaxes over $6$ seconds. The coefficients $a'$, $b$ and
$K'$, and the bare $a$ and $K$ of
\cref{eq:free-energy-choice}, then follow from the
relations in \cref{tab:field-params}.

This leaves the bulk modulus $B$, the only parameter of the
model that we fit. We set $B=90~\kPa\tcdot\micron$, for
which the peak of the layering rate is $1.7\times10^2$
times its far-field value, close to the enhancement of
about $2\times10^2$ that Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}
measures near $+1/2$ defects (\cref{sec:nucleation-2}). No
direct measurement exists to compare it with, but related
moduli, multiplied by the layer height $h$, span five
orders of magnitude. At the soft end, colonies of
\textit{Serratia marcescens} on agar have shear and
compressive moduli of $10^2$--$10^4~\Pa$, or
$0.05$--$5~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{kochanowski2024}. The
turgor pressure of \textit{E.\ coli}, $29~\kPa$,
corresponds to
$15~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{deng2011direct}, and the
effective cell modulus of $375~\kPa$ that fits the
planar-to-bulk transition of growing \textit{E.\ coli}
colonies corresponds to
$190~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{grant2014}. Indentation of
live \textit{M.\ xanthus} cells gives a local Young's
modulus of $0.25~\mathrm{MPa}$, or about
$125~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{pelling2005nanoscale},
although this is the stiffness of the cell envelope, not
the compressibility of the monolayer. At the stiff end, the
cell wall has a Young's modulus of $25$--$50~\mathrm{MPa}$,
or about
$10^4~\kPa\tcdot\micron$~\cite{yao1999thickness,
deng2011direct}. The fitted $B$ thus lies between the
turgor pressure and the effective cell modulus, close to
the envelope stiffness of \textit{M.\ xanthus}. It is
stiffer than the moduli measured for bacterial colonies and
of the order of the stiffness of single cells.

\section{Elastic energy released by the bottom layer}
\label{app:extrusion}
% dean.tex 1293-1337

The pressure work $pA$ in \cref{eq:dE-of-A} is the elastic
energy that the compressed cells release when a patch
leaves the monolayer. We derive it from the compression
term $\mathfrak f_B=\tfrac B2(\rho-1)^2$, the only term of
\cref{eq:free-energy-choice} that depends on $\rho$.

Consider a region of area $A_d$ that is compressed
uniformly to $\rho=1+\epsilon$, so that it stores the
energy $\mathfrak f_BA_d=\tfrac B2\epsilon^2A_d$. A patch
of area $A$ inside this region then forms the new layer and
takes $\rho_0(1+\epsilon)A$ cells with it. As the bottom
layer spreads back over the whole of $A_d$
(\cref{fig:nucleation}(a)), its strain falls to
$\epsilon_f=\epsilon-(1+\epsilon)A/A_d$. The extruded
cells, in contrast, are unconfined in the new layer and
relax to zero strain. The elastic energy therefore changes
by $\Delta E_e=\tfrac B2(\epsilon_f^2-\epsilon^2)A_d$,
which we write as
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dEe-full}
    \Delta E_e = \frac B2\left[-2\epsilon - 2\epsilon^2
    + (1+\epsilon)^2\frac A{A_d}\right]A.
\end{equation}

Of this energy, the bottom layer releases $pA-\tfrac
B2(1+\epsilon)^2A^2/A_d$, where $p=\tfrac B2(\rho^2-1)$ is
the exact pressure of \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, while
the extruded cells release their own strain energy
$\mathfrak f_BA$. For a patch much smaller than the
compressed region, $A\ll A_d$, the term in $A^2/A_d$ is
negligible, and
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dEe}
    \Delta E_e \simeq -(p+\mathfrak f_B)A \simeq -pA.
\end{equation}
The $p$ of \cref{eq:dE-of-A} is thus the pressure of
\cref{eq:pressure-definition}.

Dropping $\mathfrak f_B$ in the last step of \cref{eq:dEe}
is consistent at leading order, the order at which
\cref{eq:dE-of-A} works, because $\mathfrak f_B$ is
quadratic in $\epsilon$ and $p$ is linear. At the pressure
where a layer forms, $p=\pcrit$ (\cref{eq:pcrit}), however,
$\epsilon=\pcrit/B\simeq0.39$ at leading order, and the
dropped term adds about $20\%$ to $pA$. This correction
lowers the pressure at which a layer forms. Like the other
simplifications of the nucleus in \cref{sec:nucleation-1},
the disk of one cell area and the pressure taken at a
point, it is absorbed by the fit of $B$.


\section{Coarse-graining}
\label{app:dean}
% dean.tex 174-526 (derivation), 681-811 (truncation rule, comoving frame, linearization);
% checks: claude_experiments/appC_check.wls, newset_numbers.py

In this appendix we obtain Dean's equation for the joint
density of positions and orientations, then integrate out
the orientation to reach the field equations for $\rho$ and
$\Q$ of \cref{sec:model-coarse,sec:model-closure}, and
finally explain why we work with the unnormalized order
parameter.

\subsection{Derivation of Dean's equation}
\label{app:dean-approach}
We derive \cref{eq:final-langevin} with It\^o's lemma, so
\cref{eq:model} is read in the It\^o sense, although $k_i$
is the white limit of a colored noise and such a limit
converges to the Stratonovich
interpretation~\cite{wong1965convergence}. Here the two
interpretations coincide. The drift that separates them is
$\tfrac12 g\,\partial g$, where $g$ is the amplitude of a
noise and the derivative is taken along the variables that
this noise drives. It vanishes for both noises: the
amplitude $v_0\p(\theta_i)$ of $k_i$ depends only on
$\theta_i$, which $k_i$ does not drive, and $\lambda_i$ is
additive.

We follow the rod through a smooth test function
$\phi(\r,\theta)$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dean-ito-single}
  \phi(\r_i,\theta_i)=\int\!\d\r\,\d\theta\,\phi(\r,\theta)\,\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta),
\end{equation}
where $\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta) =
\delta(\r-\r_i(t))\,\delta(\theta-\theta_i(t))$ is the
single-particle density. Differentiating both sides in time
gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dean-ito-time}
  \frac{\d\phi}{\d t} = \int\!\d\r\,\d\theta\,\phi(\r,\theta)\,\pd{}{t}\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta),
\end{equation}
whose right-hand side contains $\partial_t\varrho^{(i)}_t$.
We evaluate the left-hand side a second time with the It\^o
formula,
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:dean-ito}
  \d\phi(\r_i,\theta_i) = \pd{\phi}{\r}\cdot\d\r_i + \pd{\phi}{\theta}\,\d\theta_i
  \\
  + \frac12\pd{^2\phi}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}\d r_\alpha \d r_\beta
  + \pd{^2\phi}{r_\alpha\partial \theta}\d r_\alpha \d \theta
  + \frac12\pd{^2\phi}{\theta^2}\d \theta \d\theta,
\end{multline}
with the derivatives of $\phi$ evaluated at the rod. In the
white-noise limit, the quadratic variations of the
increments of \cref{eq:model} are $\d r_\alpha\d
r_\beta=(v_0^2/\frev)\,p_\alpha p_\beta\,\d t$, $\d \theta
\d\theta=2\Lambda\,\d t$ and $\d\r\,\d\theta=0$. With
$\d\r_i$ and $\d\theta_i$ from \cref{eq:model}, the
left-hand side of \cref{eq:dean-ito-time} therefore becomes
\begin{multline}
  \frac{\d\phi}{\d t} = \pd{\phi}{\r}\cdot\left[
    \frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F_i + v_0\,\p(\theta_i) k_i
  \right]
  + \pd{\phi}{\theta}\left[
    \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma_i + \lambda_i
  \right]
  \\
  + \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha(\theta_i) p_\beta(\theta_i)\,\pd{^2\phi}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\phi}{\theta^2},
\end{multline}
where $\F_i = \F(\r_i,\theta_i)$ and $\Gamma_i =
\Gamma(\r_i,\theta_i)$, with the fields of
\cref{eq:force-torque}. Every term on the right is again a
function evaluated at the rod, so \cref{eq:dean-ito-single}
applies to each of them:
\begin{multline}
\frac{\d\phi}{\d t} = \int\!\d\r\,\d\theta\,\varrho^{(i)}_t
\Bigg(\pd{\phi}{\r}\cdot\left[
    \frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F + v_0\,\p k_i
  \right]
  \\
  + \pd{\phi}{\theta}\left[
    \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma + \lambda_i
  \right]
  \\
 + \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha p_\beta\,\pd{^2\phi}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\phi}{\theta^2}
\Bigg),
\end{multline}
with $\F$, $\Gamma$ and $\p$ now evaluated at
$(\r,\theta)$. Integrating by parts, once in the
first-order terms and twice in the second-order ones, moves
the derivatives from $\phi$ onto $\varrho^{(i)}_t$, as in
\cref{eq:dean-ito-time}. There are no boundary terms,
because $\varrho^{(i)}_t$ vanishes away from the rod and is
periodic in $\theta$, and $p_\alpha p_\beta$, which depends
only on $\theta$, passes through the $\r$ derivatives
unchanged,
\begin{multline}
  \frac{\d\phi}{\d t} =
  \int\d\r\d\theta\, \phi(\r,\theta) \Bigg(
    -\pd{}{\r}\cdot\Bigl[\varrho^{(i)}_t
    \Bigl(\frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F+v_0\p\,k_i\Bigr)\Bigr]
  \\
  - \pd{}{\theta}\Bigl[\varrho^{(i)}_t\Bigl(\tfrac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma+\lambda_i\Bigr)\Bigr]
  + \frac{v_0^2 p_\alpha p_\beta}{2\frev}\,
    \pd{^2\varrho^{(i)}_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho^{(i)}_t}{\theta^2}
\Bigg).
\end{multline}
Since this equals \cref{eq:dean-ito-time} for every $\phi$,
the integrands agree,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:dean-single}
  \partial_t\varrho^{(i)}_t \!=&\!  -\pd{}{\r}\cdot\Bigl[\varrho^{(i)}_t
    \!\Bigl(\tfrac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F\!+v_0\p\,k_i\Bigr)\Bigr]
 \!-\! \pd{}{\theta}\Bigl[\varrho^{(i)}_t\Bigl(\tfrac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma\!+\!\lambda_i\Bigr)\Bigr]
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha p_\beta\,
    \pd{^2\varrho^{(i)}_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho^{(i)}_t}{\theta^2}.
\end{align}
The It\^o term of self-propulsion, in the last line,
carries the same amplitude $v_0^2/\frev$ as the noise
$v_0\p\,k_i$, and this shared amplitude ties the active
stress to the noise (\cref{app:dean-angle}).

Summing \cref{eq:dean-single} over $i$, with the weight
$\rho_0^{-1}$ of \cref{eq:joint-density-definition}, turns
each drift term into the same term for $\varrho_t$, because
$\F$, $\Gamma$ and $\p$ do not depend on $i$. The noise
terms keep their rod index, and we collect them in
$\bs\chi=\rho_0^{-1}v_0\sum_i\varrho^{(i)}_t\,\p\,k_i$ and
$\psi=\rho_0^{-1}\sum_i\varrho^{(i)}_t\lambda_i$. The joint
density therefore obeys
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:dean-sum}
  \partial_t\varrho_t = {}& -\frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\pd{}{\r}\cdot(\varrho_t\F)
  - \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\pd{}{\theta}(\varrho_t\Gamma)
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac{v_0^2}{2\frev}\,p_\alpha p_\beta\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
  + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{\theta^2}
  \nonumber\\
  &- \pd{}{\r}\cdot\bs\chi - \pd{}{\theta}\psi .
\end{align}
The force and torque of \cref{eq:force-torque} follow from
$\varrho_t$ through the pair free energy of
\cref{eq:pair-free-energy} and its functional derivative,
\begin{align}
  \calF[\varrho] =& \frac{\rho_0^2}2\!\int\!\d\r\d\r'\,\d\theta\d\theta'\,
  V(\r\!-\!\r'\!,\theta,\theta')\,\varrho(\r,\theta)\,\varrho(\r'\!,\theta'),
  \nonumber
  \\
  \frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}=&\rho_0^2\int\!\d\r'\d\theta'\,
V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')\,\varrho_t(\r',\theta')
\nonumber\\
=& \rho_0\sum_jV(\r-\r_j,\theta,\theta_j).
\label{eq:dean-functional-derivative}
\end{align}
Both factors of $\varrho$ contribute equally, by the pair
symmetry
$V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')=V(\r'-\r,\theta',\theta)$, and
the last step uses \cref{eq:joint-density-definition}. With
\cref{eq:force-torque},
$\F=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\r\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$ and
$\Gamma
=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\theta\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$,
which turns \cref{eq:dean-sum} into the first two lines of
\cref{eq:final-langevin}.

The noises $\bs\chi$ and $\psi$ still carry the individual
$k_i$ and $\lambda_i$. They are Gaussian, so it suffices to
match their correlators. Different rods have independent
noises, so only $i=j$ survives the average of
$\bs\chi\bs\chi$, and two delta functions at the same rod
collapse to one,
$\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta)\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r',\theta')
=\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,
\theta)\,\delta(\r-\r')\delta(\theta-\theta')$. Hence
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dean-noise-sum}
  \la\chi_\alpha(\r,\theta,t)\,\chi_\beta(\r',\theta',t')\ra
  = \frac{v_0^2}{\frev\rho_0}\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)\,p_\alpha(\theta)p_\beta(\theta)\,\Delta,
\end{equation}
with
$\Delta\equiv\delta(t-t')\delta(\r-\r')\delta(\theta-\theta')$.
The field $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p\,k$, with $k$ of
\cref{eq:noise-correlators}, has the same correlator. In
the same way, the rotational noise $\psi$ has the
correlator
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:dean-noise-sum-psi}
  \la\psi(\r,\theta,t)\,\psi(\r',\theta',t')\ra
  = \frac{2\Lambda}{\rho_0}\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)\,\Delta,
\end{equation}
and so has $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda$. Both identities
hold at fixed $\varrho_t$, which is all an It\^o increment
needs, so replacing $\bs\chi$ by $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p\,k$
and $\psi$ by $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda$ gives the last
two lines of \cref{eq:final-langevin}.

\subsection{The fluctuating active nematic equations}
\label{app:dean-angle}

Integrating \cref{eq:final-langevin} over $\theta$ needs
the $\theta$ dependence of $\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$. In
the parametrization \cref{eq:free-energy-parametrisation},
$\calF[\varrho]=F[\rho,\Q]$, the fields $\rho$ and $\Q$ are
functionals of $\varrho$ through
\cref{eq:density-definition}, and the chain rule
\cref{eq:chain-rule} follows from
\begin{align}
  \frac{\delta\rho(\r')}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}=\delta(\r-\r'),
  \quad
  \frac{\delta\Q(\r')}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}=\hat\Q(\theta)\,\delta(\r-\r'),
\end{align}
 so $\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$ holds only the angular
 harmonics $0$ and $2$. The interaction terms of the
 density equation therefore integrate to $\rho$ and $\Q$
 alone, whereas in the $\Q$-equation they leave angular
 moments, which we write as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:average-def}
\la X\ra_\varrho\equiv\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)X(\theta).
\end{equation}
For example, the density and the nematic order of
\cref{eq:density-definition} read
$\rho=\langle1\rangle_\varrho$ and
$\Q=\langle\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$.

Integrating \cref{eq:final-langevin} over $\theta$ removes
every $\theta$ derivative, and the self-propulsion term
needs only $\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,p_\alpha
p_\beta=\tfrac12(Q_\ab+\rho\,\delta_\ab)$, which follows
from $\p\p=\tfrac12(\hat\Q+\I)$. Before any approximation,
and with $\zeta=\xi_0v_0^2/(4\frev)$ of
\cref{eq:active-force}, the density obeys
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-full}
  \partial_t\rho = {}& \frac1{\xi_0}\nabla\cdot\Bigl(\rho\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}
    + \Q\!:\!\nabla\frac{\delta F}{\delta\Q}\Bigr)
  \nonumber\\
  &+\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\bigl(\nabla^2\rho+\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta Q_\ab\bigr)
  - \nabla\cdot\bEta_\rho,
\end{align}
where the noise is the angular integral of the noise
$\bs\chi$ of \cref{eq:dean-sum},
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-chi}
  \bEta_\rho=\int\!\d\theta\,\bs\chi
  =\frac{v_0}{\rho_0}\sum_i\delta(\r-\r_i)\,\p(\theta_i)\,k_i .
\end{equation}
Replacing $\bs\chi$ by $\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p\,k$ gives
\cref{eq:eta-rho-def}. With \cref{eq:free-energy-choice},
the first term of \cref{eq:rho-full}, $\rho\nabla(\delta
F/\delta\rho)=B\rho\nabla\rho=\nabla p$, is the gradient of
the exact pressure \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, and the
second is $-\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$, which is minus the divergence
of the nematic stresses (\cref{sec:pressure}). The $\zeta$
term splits into the diffusion $\zeta\nabla^2\rho/\xi_0$
and the divergence of the active force
\cref{eq:active-force}.

The large modulus $B$ keeps $\rho$ close to one. In the
steady state $\rho-1$ is of order $\zeta/B$
(\cref{eq:rho-analytic}). We therefore keep the leading
order in $\rho-1$, counting $B(\rho-1)$ as of order one.
This truncation removes the diffusion
$(\zeta/\xi_0)\nabla^2\rho$, whose ratio to
$B\nabla^2\rho/\xi_0$ is $\zeta/B$, and it reduces
$B\rho\nabla\rho$ to $B\nabla\rho$, so that
\cref{eq:rho-full} becomes \cref{eq:model-rho}. These
truncated equations give the variance of
\cref{sec:defect-fluct}. Layers, however, form far out in
the tail of the density distribution, where
$\rho-1\simeq\pcrit/B\simeq0.39$ (\cref{app:extrusion}), so
the layering rate is a Gaussian extrapolation of these
equations, one of the deliberate simplifications of
\cref{sec:model-assumptions}. The noise correlator
\cref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation}, in contrast, needs
neither the truncation nor a closure. It follows from
\cref{eq:eta-rho-chi}, the correlator
\cref{eq:dean-noise-sum} of $\bs\chi$ and the angular
integral used above,
\begin{align}
  &\la\eta^\rho_\alpha(\r,t)\,\eta^\rho_\beta(\r',t')\ra
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\int\!\d\theta\,\d\theta'\,
  \la\chi_\alpha(\r,\theta,t)\,\chi_\beta(\r',\theta',t')\ra
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{v_0^2}{\frev\rho_0}\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,p_\alpha p_\beta\;
  \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t')
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}\bigl(Q_\ab+\rho\,\delta_\ab\bigr)\,
  \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t').
\end{align}

For the $\Q$-equation we multiply \cref{eq:final-langevin}
by $\hat\Q(\theta)$ before integrating. One integration by
parts turns the surviving $\theta$ derivatives into
$\hat\Q'\equiv\partial_\theta\hat\Q$, and the identity
$\hat\Q''=-4\hat\Q$ removes the second derivatives. In
components, with summation over repeated indices and the
moments of \cref{eq:average-def},
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:Q-full}
  \partial_t Q_\ab = {}& \frac1{\xi_0}\partial_\gamma\Bigl(Q_\ab\,\partial_\gamma\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}
  + \langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_\mn\rangle_\varrho\,\partial_\gamma\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_\mn}\Bigr)
  \nonumber\\
  &- \frac1{\xir}\langle\hat Q'_\ab\hat Q'_\mn\rangle_\varrho\,\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_\mn}
  - 4\Lambda Q_\ab
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\bigl(\nabla^2Q_\ab
  + \partial_\mu\partial_\nu\langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_\mn\rangle_\varrho\bigr)
  \nonumber\\
  &- \partial_\gamma\eta^\Q_{\ab\gamma} + f^\Q_\ab .
\end{align}
The four lines come from the translational friction, the
rotational friction and diffusion, self-propulsion and the
two noises. Like $\bEta_\rho$, the noises are angular
integrals of those of \cref{eq:dean-sum}, now weighted by
$\hat\Q$,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:Q-noise-chi}
  \eta^\Q_{\ab\gamma}=\int\!\d\theta\,\hat Q_\ab\,\chi_\gamma,
  \qquad
  f^\Q_\ab=\int\!\d\theta\,\hat Q'_\ab\,\psi.
\end{align}
The second follows from
$-\int\!\d\theta\,\hat\Q\,\partial_\theta\psi$ by one
integration by parts in $\theta$, which turns the minus
sign into a plus. Replacing $\bs\chi$ by
$\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p\,k$ and $\psi$ by
$\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda$ gives the $\bEta^\Q$ of
\cref{sec:model-closure} and
$f^\Q_\ab=\int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\hat
Q'_\ab\,\lambda$. With these definitions every noise
carries a plus sign: $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$ enter
\cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} as $-\nabla\cdot\bEta$, with
the plus sign inside the flux as in
\cref{eq:final-langevin}, and $\f^\Q$ enters with a plus
sign.

\Cref{eq:Q-full} is not closed, because the moments
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$ and
$\langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho$ are not functions of
$\rho$ and $\Q$. We close it with the lowest-order closure
of \cref{app:closure}, which sets
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho\simeq\rho\J$ and
$\langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho\simeq4\rho\J$, with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:J-definition}
  J_\abmn=\tfrac12(\delta_{\alpha\mu}\delta_{\beta\nu}+\delta_{\alpha\nu}\delta_{\beta\mu}
  -\delta_\ab\delta_\mn).
\end{equation}
The tensor $\J$ leaves the symmetric traceless $\delta
F/\delta\Q$ unchanged, and it gives
$\J\!:\!\nabla\nabla\rho
=\nabla\nabla\rho-\tfrac12\I\nabla^2\rho$. With this
closure \cref{eq:Q-full} becomes
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:Q-closed}
  \partial_t Q_\ab =
  \frac{1}{\xi_0}\partial_\gamma\Bigl(Q_\ab\,\partial_\gamma\frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho}\Bigr)
  +\frac{1}{\xi_0}\partial_\gamma\Bigl(\rho\,\partial_\gamma\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_\ab}\Bigr)
  \\
  -\frac{4\rho}{\xir}\frac{\delta F}{\delta Q_\ab} -4\Lambda Q_\ab
  +\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\Bigl(\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta\rho
  +\nabla^2\bigl(Q_\ab-\tfrac12\rho\,\delta_\ab\bigr)\Bigr)
  \\
  - \partial_\gamma\eta^\Q_{\ab\gamma}+ f^\Q_\ab .
\end{multline}
The truncation of the density equation removes the two
places where the density now enters. The derivative terms
of the $\zeta$ term,
$(\zeta/\xi_0)(\nabla\nabla\rho-\tfrac12\I\nabla^2\rho)$,
are smaller than the retained $(B/\xi_0)\,\nabla\cdot(\Q
\nabla\rho)$ by a factor of order $\zeta/B$, and the
prefactor $\rho$ of the two $\delta F/\delta\Q$ terms can
be set to one. The rotational friction then gives
$(4/\xir)\H$, and the translational friction
$-\nabla^2\H/\xi_0$. Rotational diffusion, $-4\Lambda\Q$,
and the self-propulsion term $(\zeta/\xi_0)\nabla^2\Q$ have
the form of the terms $-a\Q$ and $K\nabla^2\Q$ of $\H$ and
combine with it into $(4/\xir)\Ha$, with the $a'$ and $K'$
of \cref{eq:H-active}. With $\delta F/\delta\rho=B(\rho-1)$
the result is \cref{eq:model-Q}.

The correlators of the two $\Q$ noises follow from their
definitions \cref{eq:Q-noise-chi} exactly as for
$\bEta_\rho$, with the correlators
\cref{eq:dean-noise-sum,eq:dean-noise-sum-psi} of $\bs\chi$
and $\psi$ now weighted by $\hat\Q$ or $\hat\Q'$. For the
noises of \cref{eq:model-Q} they read
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:Q-noise-correlators}
  \langle\eta^\Q_{\ab\gamma}\,\eta^\Q_{\mn\epsilon}\rangle &= \frac{2\zeta\Delta'}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \bigl[\langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_\mn\hat Q_{\gamma\epsilon}\rangle_\varrho
  + \langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_\mn\rangle_\varrho\delta_{\gamma\epsilon}\bigr],
  \nonumber\\
  \langle\eta^\Q_{\ab\gamma}\,\eta^\rho_\epsilon\rangle &= \frac{2\zeta\Delta'}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \bigl[\langle\hat Q_\ab\hat Q_{\gamma\epsilon}\rangle_\varrho + Q_\ab\,\delta_{\gamma\epsilon}\bigr],
  \nonumber\\
  \langle\!f^\Q_\ab\,f^\Q_\mn\rangle &= \frac{2\Lambda\Delta'}{\rho_0}\,
  \langle\hat Q'_\ab\hat Q'_\mn\rangle_\varrho,
\end{align}
with $\Delta'\equiv\delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t')$ and the
moments taken at $\r$. Since $\psi$ and $\bs\chi$ are
independent, $\f^\Q$ is uncorrelated with $\bEta_\rho$ and
$\bEta^\Q$. \Cref{app:closure} evaluates the moments, and
\cref{eq:leading-closure} gives them at lowest order.

The linearized equations \cref{eq:linearized} follow from
\cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} in the frame comoving with
the core, where $\partial_t\to\partial_t-\u\cdot\nabla$. We
substitute $\rho=\rho_\ss+\delta\rho$ and
$\Q=\Q_\ss+\delta\Q$ and keep the first order in
$\delta\rho$ and $\delta\Q$. For the nematic force field
this gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:dH-active}
  \delta\Ha = {}& -a'\delta\Q
  - b\bigl[(\Q_\ss\!:\!\Q_\ss)\,\delta\Q + 2(\Q_\ss\!:\!\delta\Q)\,\Q_\ss\bigr]
  \nonumber\\
  &+ K'\nabla^2\delta\Q ,
\end{align}
whereas the transport term of \cref{eq:model-Q} gives
$(B/\xi_0)\nabla\cdot(\Q_\ss\nabla\delta\rho+\delta\Q\nabla\rho_\ss)$.
Its second part combines with the frame term
$\nabla\cdot(\delta\Q\,\u)$ into the advection by $\bs
w_\ss$ in \cref{eq:linearized-Q}. The noise amplitudes are
evaluated on the steady state, so the linearized equations
carry additive noise, and for them the It\^o and
Stratonovich interpretations coincide trivially.

In the density equation the frame velocity term is
negligible. There $\u\cdot\nabla\delta\rho$ competes with
$(B/\xi_0)\nabla^2\delta\rho$, and for $\delta\rho$ varying
over a length $L$ their ratio is $\xi_0|\u|L/B$. Our steady
state has $|\u|=0.24~\micron/\minute$, so the ratio is
$2.7\times10^{-4}$ at $L=20~\micron$, the half-width of our
box (\cref{app:comoving}), and we drop the frame velocity
term.

\subsection{Definitions of the nematic order parameter}
\label{app:dean-normalisation}
We use the unnormalized $\Q$ of
\cref{eq:density-definition} because it is linear in
$\varrho_t$, so its functional derivative is simple,
whereas the normalized $\Q/\rho$ carries the additional
term
$\delta(\Q/\rho)/\delta\varrho
=(\hat\Q-\Q/\rho)\,\delta(\r-\r')/\rho$, which would crowd
the final equations. In exchange, the strength of the
nematic order acquires a factor $\rho^{-1}$,
$S=\rho^{-1}\sqrt{(\Q\!:\!\Q)/2}$, which at $\rho=1$ is the
$S$ of \cref{sec:model-closure}, and the closure acquires
factors of $\rho$. The two definitions differ only at order
$\rho-1$, so they coincide in the incompressible limit of
large $B$. The truncation of \cref{app:dean-angle} drops
this order, and the Pad\'e profile
\cref{eq:pade-approximation} is accordingly written at
$\rho=1$.

\section{Maximum-entropy closure}
\label{app:closure}
% dean.tex 2090-2262 (construction), 2263-2450 (realizability); checks: claude_experiments/appD_check.wls

The $\Q$-equation \cref{eq:Q-full} and the correlators
\cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators} of its noises contain the
angular moments $\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$,
$\langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho$ and
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$, which depend on
the whole angular distribution and not only on $\rho$ and
$\Q$. We close them with the distribution of maximum
entropy at given $\rho$ and $\Q$, compute its moments
exactly and expand them in the strength of the nematic
order. Truncated at low order, the expansion leads to
pathologies at the strong order of the defect: it gives a
noise covariance with a negative eigenvalue
(\cref{app:realizability}) and makes the transport driven
by $\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$ ill posed (see below).

At a point $\r$, consider all angular distributions
$\varrho(\theta) = \varrho_t(\theta \,|\, \r)$ with
$\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho=\rho$ and
$\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\,\hat\Q=\Q$. Maximizing the
entropy $\int\!\d\theta\varrho\ln(1/\varrho)$ under these
constraints, with a scalar and a tensor Lagrange
multiplier, gives
$\varrho\propto\exp[\bs\lambda\!:\!\hat\Q(\theta)]$. Since
$\hat\Q$ contains only $\cos(2\theta)$ and $\sin(2\theta)$,
this is a von Mises distribution in $2\theta$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:von-mises}
  \varrho(\theta)=\frac{\rho\,\ee^{\kappa\cos2(\theta-\theta_0)}}{2\pi I_0(\kappa)},
\end{equation} 
where $\theta_0$ is the angle of the director, $\kappa\ge0$
sets how sharply the rods are aligned, and $I_n$ is the
modified Bessel function~\cite{olver2010nist}. Because the
distribution depends on $\theta$ only through $2\theta$, it
gives $\p$ and $-\p$ the same weight, and all its odd
harmonics vanish. The even ones are
$\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\,\ee^{2\ii
n\theta}=\rho\,m_n\ee^{2\ii n\theta_0}$, with the Bessel
ratios $m_n=I_n(\kappa)/I_0(\kappa)$. The first of them
gives $\Q=\rho\,m_1\hat\Q(\theta_0)$. With the strength of
the nematic order $S=\rho^{-1}\sqrt{(\Q\!:\!\Q)/2}$ of
\cref{app:dean-normalisation}, $\kappa$ therefore follows
from $\Q$ through
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kappa-S}
  \frac{I_1(\kappa)}{I_0(\kappa)}=S .
\end{equation}
The left side increases monotonically from $0$ to $1$, so
this equation has exactly one root for every $S<1$.

The other moments follow from the same harmonics. Each is
an isotropic tensor function of $\Q$, so it can be built
from the $\J$ of \cref{eq:J-definition} and three further
tensors,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:tensor-basis}
  T_{\abmn ij}(\Q) &= Q_\ab J_{\mn ij}+Q_\mn J_{\ab ij}+Q_{ij}J_\abmn,
  \nonumber\\
  \U(\Q) &= 2\bigl[\Q\Q-\tfrac12(\Q\!:\!\Q)\,\J\bigr],
  \nonumber\\
  \W(\Q) &= \Q\Q\Q-\tfrac14(\Q\!:\!\Q)\,\bT(\Q),
\end{align}
where $\Q\Q$ and $\Q\Q\Q$ are outer products,
$(\Q\Q)_\abmn=Q_\ab Q_\mn$. The tensor $\bT$ is linear in
$\Q$, whereas $\U$ and $\W$ are quadratic and cubic,
respectively. The subtractions make $\U$ and $\W$ totally
symmetric and traceless, so they are pure fourth and sixth
harmonics. For $\Q=\hat\Q(\theta_0)$, for example,
$U_{1111}=\cos4\theta_0$ and
$W_{111111}=\tfrac14\cos6\theta_0$. Integrating against
\cref{eq:von-mises} then gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:exact-moments}
  \langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho &= \rho\,\J+\frac{g_2}{2\rho}\,\U(\Q),
  \nonumber\\
  \langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho &= 4\rho\,\J-\frac{2g_2}{\rho}\,\U(\Q),
  \nonumber\\
  \langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho &= \tfrac12\,\bT(\Q)+\frac{g_3}{\rho^2}\,\W(\Q),
\end{align}
with the coefficients
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:g-coefficients}
  g_2=\frac{m_2}{m_1^2}=\frac{I_0I_2}{I_1^2},
  \qquad
  g_3=\frac{m_3}{m_1^3}=\frac{I_0^2I_3}{I_1^3},
\end{equation}
both functions of $S$ through \cref{eq:kappa-S}. The
coefficients of $\J$ and $\bT$ do not depend on the shape
of the distribution, which therefore enters only through
$g_2$ and $g_3$, and the single function $g_2$ controls
both second moments.

The exact moments are not polynomials in $\Q$, but at weak
order they simplify. Expanding the Bessel functions at
small $\kappa$ and eliminating $\kappa$ with
\cref{eq:kappa-S} gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:g-small-S}
  g_2=\tfrac12+\tfrac16S^2+O(S^4),
  \qquad
  g_3=\tfrac16+\tfrac18S^2+O(S^4).
\end{equation}
Since $\U=O(S^2)$ and $\W=O(S^3)$, whereas the leading
terms are of order $1$ and $S$, the $\U$ and $\W$ terms of
\cref{eq:exact-moments} are of order $S^2$ relative to the
leading ones. Dropping them leaves the lowest-order closure
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:leading-closure}
  &\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho\simeq\rho\,\J,
  \quad
  \langle\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\rangle_\varrho\simeq4\rho\,\J,
  \quad \langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho\simeq\tfrac12\,\bT(\Q).
\end{align}
The first two close \cref{eq:Q-full} into
\cref{eq:Q-closed}, and hence into \cref{eq:model-Q}, and
all three, inserted into \cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators},
give the lowest-order noise correlators. The defect,
however, is not weakly ordered. At $S=0.9$ the coefficients
are $g_2=0.82$ and $g_3=0.55$, against the small-$S$ values
$1/2$ and $1/6$, so the lowest-order closure is not
accurate around the defect. We nevertheless keep the closed
equation \cref{eq:model-Q}, without its $\nabla\H$ terms,
as continuum theories derived from particle models usually
do~\cite{baskaran2008hydrodynamics,bertin2013,
grossmann2016mesoscale,peshkov2012nonlinear}. For the noise
correlators, in contrast, the closure produces covariance
matrices with a negative eigenvalue, which makes the
stochastic equations ill defined, so
\cref{app:realizability} builds the noise from the exact
moments.

% Source: report_full_solution/report.tex (sec:wellposed), report_full_solution/dispersion.py
The closure also decides which passive terms the closed
equations can keep. The first line of \cref{eq:Q-full} is
the transport of alignment by the force on a rod,
\cref{eq:force-on-cells}, whose nematic part
\cref{sec:model-elastic} drops. Its isotropic part enters
with the coefficient $\Q$, the same coefficient with which
the nematic part $\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$ enters the flux of the
density in \cref{eq:model-rho}, so the two cross terms are
Onsager partners.

To see what the closure does to this transport, we
linearize about a uniform state with $\rho=1$ and order
$\Q_0$ of strength $S$, keeping both $\nabla\H$ terms. At
large $B$ and short wavelengths the pressure builds up
until its gradient balances the nematic part of the force
on the cells, $B\,\delta\rho\simeq\Q_0\!:\!\delta\H$, so
the cells barely move. Their alignment is still
transported: the nematic part carries it forward with the
second moment $\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$, and the
balancing pressure gradient carries it back with
$\Q_0\Q_0$, the product of two first moments. The first
line of \cref{eq:Q-full} becomes
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:antidiffusion}
  \partial_t\delta\Q&\simeq-\frac1{\xi_0}\bigl(\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho-\Q_0\Q_0\bigr)\!:\!\nabla^2\delta\H
  \nonumber\\
  &\simeq-\frac K{\xi_0}\bigl(\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho-\Q_0\Q_0\bigr)\!:\!\nabla^4\delta\Q ,
\end{align}
with $\delta\H\simeq K\nabla^2\delta\Q$ at short
wavelengths. The difference in the brackets is the
covariance of $\hat\Q$ over the rods, which cannot be
negative, so for any distribution of the rods this
transport is a stable fourth-order diffusion. The
lowest-order closure replaces
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$ by $\J$, which is too
small at strong order. For a change of the strength of the
order, $\delta\Q\propto\Q_0$, the coefficient then becomes
$1-2S^2$, so the return flux outweighs the forward one for
$S>1/\sqrt2$. Such perturbations grow at the rate
$(K/\xi_0)(2S^2-1)k^4$, which increases without bound with
the wavenumber $k$, so the equations are ill posed.

The failure is of the same kind as that of the noise
covariance in \cref{app:realizability}: the closure breaks
the positivity of a matrix of second moments, here above
$S=1/\sqrt2$ and there already above $S\simeq0.62$. Without
the nematic part of the force (\cref{sec:model-elastic}),
neither the forward flux nor the balancing pressure
gradient arises.
% Check: claude_experiments/round_2026_10_26/stability_paper.py (imports report_full_solution/dispersion.py)
Every perturbation of the uniform state then decays,
whatever its wavenumber, so the closed equations of the
main text are linearly stable about a uniform nematic
state. Keeping only one of the two $\nabla\H$ terms would
be inconsistent, because both come from the same force on a
rod.

\section{\texorpdfstring{The steady state of the defect}{The steady state of the defect}}
\label{app:steady}
The fluctuations of \cref{sec:defect-fluct} are linear
deviations from the steady state of the defect, which
solves the noiseless equations. We find the density of the
static defect in closed form (\cref{app:density-poisson}),
the steady state of the moving defect numerically
(\cref{app:comoving}), and the direction of its motion from
the closure of the $\Q$-equation (\cref{app:drift}).

\subsection{Solution of the noiseless density equation}
\label{app:density-poisson}
% dean.tex 820-903
The nematic profile follows from $\Ha=0$. The ansatz
$\Q=S(r)\hat\Q(\phi/2)$ of \cref{eq:pade-approximation} has
the components $Q_{xx}=S\cos\phi$ and $Q_{xy}=S\sin\phi$.
Both are angular harmonics of order one, on which the
Laplacian acts as $S''+S'/r-S/r^2$, and $\Q\!:\!\Q=2S^2$
turns the cubic term of \cref{eq:H-active} into
$-2bS^3\hat\Q(\phi/2)$. With $S_0$ and $\elld$ of
\cref{eq:S0-elld}, the condition $\Ha=0$ therefore reduces
to the radial equation
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:S-radial}
  S''+\frac{S'}{r}-\frac{S}{r^2}=\frac{1}{\elld^2}\Bigl(\frac{S^2}{S_0^2}-1\Bigr)S,
\end{align}
with $S(0)=0$ at the core and $S\to S_0$ far from it. It
has no closed-form solution. Far from the core it gives
$S\simeq S_0(1-\elld^2/2r^2)$, the far field of the Pad\'e
profile \cref{eq:pade-approximation}, which departs from
the numerical solution of \cref{eq:S-radial} by less than
$10^{-2}\,S_0$, most near $r\simeq1.5\,\elld$. A
higher-order approximant of $f_\pade$, with $x=r/\elld$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pade-accurate}
  f(x)=x\left[\tfrac{0.34011+0.04517\,x^2+0.00234\,x^4}
  {1+0.38387\,x^2+0.04751\,x^4+0.00234\,x^6}\right]^{1/2}\!\!\!,
\end{equation}
departs from it by less than $10^{-4}S_0$. In all our
calculations we use \cref{eq:pade-accurate} in place of
\cref{eq:pade-approximation}.

With $\Q$ known, the first equation of
\cref{eq:simplified-noiseless} reads
$B\nabla^2\rho=\nabla\cdot\bfa$. The active force
\cref{eq:active-force-defect} points along $x$ and depends
on $r$ alone, so
$\nabla\cdot\bfa=\partial_x\fa_x
=-\zeta\,(S'+S/r)'\cos\phi$. The radial factor $(S'+S/r)'$
equals $S''+S'/r-S/r^2$, the operator of
\cref{eq:S-radial}, so
$\nabla\cdot\bfa=-\zeta\nabla^2(S\cos\phi)$ whatever the
profile $S$, and the density obeys
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-harmonic}
  \nabla^2\Bigl[\rho+\frac{\zeta}{B}\,S(r)\cos\phi\Bigr]=0 .
\end{align}
We therefore write the density as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rho-h}
  \rho(r,\phi)=1-\frac{\zeta}{B}\,S(r)\cos\phi+h(r,\phi),\qquad \nabla^2h=0,
\end{equation}
with $h$ regular for $r\le R$ and fixed by the boundary
condition. Every condition below is proportional to
$\cos\phi$, so $h=c+A\,r\cos\phi$, the only regular
harmonic function with this angular dependence up to a
constant.

The reservoir boundary $\rho(R,\phi)=1$ of
\cref{sec:defect-steady} gives $c=0$ and
$A=(\zeta/B)S(R)/R$, which is \cref{eq:rho-analytic}. Since
$S(0)=0$ and $\cos\phi$ averages to zero over the disk, the
density at the core and the mean density are both one.

Leaving the density free at the boundary,
$\partial_r\rho=0$, is not consistent. This condition
exerts no pressure gradient, so the active flux
$\bfa/\xi_0$ crosses the boundary, and once the
cross-coupling term $(B/\xi_0)\nabla\cdot(\Q\nabla\rho)$ of
\cref{eq:model-Q} breaks the antisymmetry of the defect,
inflow and outflow no longer cancel. Cells then enter or
leave the disk, and no steady state is reached.

A no-flux boundary imposes $\nh\cdot(B\nabla\rho-\bfa)=0$,
that is $B\,\partial_r\rho=\fa_r$ at $r=R$, with the radial
active force $\fa_r=-\zeta(S'+S/r)\cos\phi$. This gives
$A=-(\zeta/B)S(R)/R$, and since the number of cells is
conserved, the mean density stays one and $c=0$. Thus
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-noflux}
  \rho(r,\phi)=1-\frac{\zeta}{B}\Bigl[S(r)+\frac rR\,S(R)\Bigr]\cos\phi .
\end{align}
The cells that the active force drives against this
boundary pile up there, so the linear term has the opposite
slope. Both boundaries change the density across the whole
disk, but only at order $\zeta/B$, far below the scale of
its fluctuations, and \cref{fig:rho1d-dirichlet-neumann}
shows the same in the numerical solution of
\cref{app:comoving}.

\label{app:comoving}

With the cross-coupling the defect moves
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}), so we compute its velocity $\u$
together with $\rho_\ss$ and $\Q_\ss$, in the frame that
moves with the core.

Without the $\nabla\H$ terms (\cref{sec:model-elastic}) and
the noises, and with
$\partial_t\to\partial_t-\u\cdot\nabla$,
\cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} give the steady-state
equations
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:comoving-equations}
\begin{align}
    0 ={}& \u\cdot\nabla\rho + \frac1{\xi_0}\nabla\cdot
    \bigl(B\nabla\rho - \bfa\bigr),
    \\
    0 ={}& \nabla\cdot\Bigl[\Q\Bigl(\u+\frac B{\xi_0}\nabla\rho\Bigr)\Bigr]
    + \frac4{\xir}\Ha .
    \label{eq:comoving-equations-Q}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

We drop the advection term $\u\cdot\nabla\rho$ of the
density equation. At large $B$ the pressure gradient
balances the active force, $B\nabla\rho\simeq\bfa$, so at
distance $r$ from the core the dropped term is smaller than
the flux divergence by $\xi_0ur/B$. $B$ then drops out of
the problem: $\Q_\ss$ and $\u$ do not depend on $B$, and
$\rho_\ss-1\propto1/B$ (\cref{fig:rho1d-dirichlet}).


The frame velocity $\u$ has no closed form, and
\cref{app:drift} estimates it only at first order in the
cross-coupling. It enters the numerical problem as an extra
unknown, fixed by the condition that the core stays at the
origin. We solve \cref{eq:comoving-equations} by finite
differences in the square box of side $40~\micron$, for
$\rho$, $Q_1=Q_{xx}$ and $Q_2=Q_{xy}$ at every node of the
grid, and for $u_x$ and $u_y$. On the boundaries of the box
we impose the reservoir $\rho=1$ of
\cref{sec:defect-steady} and $\nh\cdot\nabla\Q=0$, so that
the boundary forces no nematic axis. Starting from the
Pad\'e profile, we impose the shape of the defect only by
pinning the core, $Q_1(0,0)=Q_2(0,0)=0$, and by setting
$Q_2(x,0)=0$ on the axis, with $x Q_1(x,0)>0$. These
constraints are enough to keep the solution a $+1/2$
defect.

In this box $u_x=0.24~\micron/\minute$, along $+x$ as
\cref{sec:defect-steady} explains, and
$|u_y|<10^{-10}~\micron/\minute$. The value of $u_x$
depends on the boundary conditions and on the size of the
box (\cref{eq:drift-radial}). The cross-coupling also
deforms the defect, weakly near the core and more away from
it, where the solution deviates from the Pad\'e profile
(\cref{fig:comoving-deformation}(a,b) and
\cref{fig:nematicaxis}). The cross-coupling and the motion
of the defect also shift the active force away from
\cref{eq:active-force-defect}
(\cref{fig:comoving-deformation}(c)).


\subsection{Direction of the defect drift}
\label{app:drift}
% Source: claude_experiments/drift_closure_2026_10_09/ (run_lambda.wls: comoving solutions;
% analyze_deformation.wls: core deformation); drift estimate:
% claude_experiments/round_2026_10_26/solvability_accurate.py
In one term of the $\Q$-equation, the anisotropic transport
of nematic order by self-propulsion, we replace the
lowest-order closure by the exact moments of the
maximum-entropy distribution in \cref{eq:exact-moments},
which \cref{app:realizability} already uses for the noise.
With this term the defect moves along $-x$, toward its
head, instead of toward its tail as in \cref{app:comoving}.
This is the direction that the Supplementary Note of
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} reports, in polar coordinates
oriented as ours.

With reversals, self-propulsion makes each rod diffuse
along its own axis, with the diffusivity
$v_0^2/(2\frev)=2\zeta/\xi_0$ of the second line of
\cref{eq:final-langevin}, and each rod carries its
alignment with it. In the $\Q$-equation this diffusion
gives the third line of \cref{eq:Q-full},
$(\zeta/\xi_0)(\nabla^2Q_\ab+\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\langle\hat
Q_\ab\hat Q_\mn\rangle_\varrho)$. The first part is
isotropic and enters $K'$ in \cref{eq:H-active}. The second
part is anisotropic, because the alignment moves along the
axis of the rods and not across it, so it depends on the
angular distribution of the rods. The lowest-order closure
\cref{eq:leading-closure} reduces it to
$(\zeta/\xi_0)(\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta-\tfrac12\delta_\ab\nabla^2)\rho$,
which is smaller than the cross-coupling
$(B/\xi_0)\nabla\cdot(\Q\nabla\rho)$ by $\zeta/B$ and is
dropped (\cref{app:dean-angle}). The exact moments
\cref{eq:exact-moments} instead add $(g_2/2\rho)\,\U(\Q)$
to $\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho$, with the
fourth-harmonic tensor $\U$ of \cref{eq:tensor-basis} and
the coefficient $g_2$ of \cref{eq:g-coefficients}, and so
add to the right-hand side of the $\Q$-equation the term
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-U-term}
  Y_\ab=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\,\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\Bigl[\frac{g_2}{2\rho}\,U_{\ab \mu\nu}(\Q)\Bigr].
\end{equation}
We add $w\Y$ to the right-hand side of the comoving
equation \cref{eq:comoving-equations-Q}, with a weight $w$
that runs from $w=0$, the closure of the main text, to
$w=1$, the exact moments in this term. Everything else,
including the closure of all other terms and the parameters
of \cref{tab:field-params}, stays as it is.

Since $\Y$ contains no density gradient, the large modulus
$B$ does not suppress it. Its size is set instead by the
amplitude $g_2S^2$ of the fourth angular harmonic of the
distribution \cref{eq:von-mises}, which is $0.66$ at
$S=0.9$. Near the core $\Y$ is therefore of order $\zeta
g_2S^2/(\xi_0\elld^2)$, the same order as the
cross-coupling, $\zeta S^2/(\xi_0\elld^2)$, because
$B\nabla\rho\simeq\bfa=-\zeta\nabla\cdot\Q$
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}). The lowest-order closure thus
drops a term as large as the one that moves the defect.

On the defect, $\Y$ takes a simple form. We combine
$Q_1=Q_{xx}$ and $Q_2=Q_{xy}$ of \cref{app:comoving} into
the complex field $\Psi=Q_1+\ii Q_2$ and write
$\bar\partial=\partial_x-\ii\partial_y$. Expanding
\cref{eq:tensor-basis} in components then gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-complex}
  Y_{xx}+\ii Y_{xy}=\frac{\zeta}{2\xi_0}\,\bar\partial^{\,2}\Bigl(\frac{g_2}{\rho}\,\Psi^2\Bigr).
\end{equation}
The Pad\'e profile \cref{eq:pade-approximation} is
$\Psi_0=S(r)\,\ee^{\ii\phi}$. At $\rho\simeq1$ we therefore
have $g_2\Psi_0^2=F(r)\,\ee^{2\ii\phi}$ with $F=g_2S^2$,
and
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-T-profile}
  \bar\partial^{\,2}\bigl(F\,\ee^{2\ii\phi}\bigr)=F''+\frac3r\,F' .
\end{equation}
On the static defect $\Y$ thus has no angular dependence
and adds to $Q_1$ alone. At the core it equals
$2(\zeta/\xi_0)S'(0)^2$, about twice the cross-coupling,
which has the opposite sign. In the tail, where the
director lies along the axis, $Q_1>0$, and in the head,
where the director lies across, $Q_1<0$. A positive source
of $Q_1$ therefore shifts the zero of $\Psi$, the core,
toward $-x$. The cross-coupling acts the other way: it
carries $\Q$ with the velocity $-B\nabla\rho/\xi_0$, which
points along $+x$ at the core (\cref{sec:defect-steady}).

The drift has no closed form (\cref{app:comoving}), but the
competition between the two transports can be estimated at
first order. In the laboratory frame, the $\Q$-equation
with $w\Y$ added, of which \cref{eq:comoving-equations-Q}
is the comoving steady form, splits into the relaxation and
the two transports,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-Q-split}
  \partial_t\Q=\frac4{\xir}\Ha+\mathbf{s},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{s}=\frac B{\xi_0}\nabla\cdot(\Q\nabla\rho)+w\,\Y,
\end{equation}
with $\rho$ of \cref{eq:rho-analytic}. Like $\Q$, the
tensor $\mathbf{s}$ is symmetric and traceless, so its
complex form $s=s_{xx}+\ii s_{xy}$ carries all of it. The
relaxation lowers an effective free energy that does not
change when the defect is displaced, so a displaced profile
$\Psi_0(\r-\mathbf{a})$ is also a steady state of the
relaxation alone. For a small displacement along $x$, it
differs from $\Psi_0$ by a multiple of the translation mode
$\partial_x\Psi_0$, which the linearized relaxation
therefore maps to zero. This linearization is self-adjoint,
because it is the second variation of a free energy. When
$s$ is weak, the defect moves with only a small change of
its shape, $\Psi\simeq\Psi_0(\r-\u t)$, and projecting the
complex form of \cref{eq:drift-Q-split} onto the
translation mode removes this unknown change of shape and
leaves the estimate
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-solvability}
  u_x\simeq-\frac{\la\partial_x\Psi_0,s\ra}{\la\partial_x\Psi_0,\partial_x\Psi_0\ra},
  \qquad
  \la a,b\ra=\mathrm{Re}\!\int\!\d^2r\;a\,b^*,
\end{equation}
with $s$ evaluated on the static profile. Neither transport
is small against the relaxation, since their ratio to it is
$\zeta\xir S_0/(4\xi_0K')=0.45$, so we expect
\cref{eq:drift-solvability} to give the trend of the drift
rather than its value.

The translation mode contains only the angular harmonics
$0$ and $2$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-translation}
  \partial_x\Psi_0=\frac12\Bigl(S'+\frac Sr\Bigr)+\frac12\Bigl(S'-\frac Sr\Bigr)\ee^{2\ii\phi}.
\end{equation}
Of the harmonics of $s=\sum_ms_m(r)\,\ee^{\ii m\phi}$, only
$s_0$ and $s_2$ therefore contribute, and on a disk of
radius $R$ \cref{eq:drift-solvability} reads
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-radial}
  u_x\simeq-\frac{\int_0^R\bigl[(S'+S/r)\,s_0+(S'-S/r)\,s_2\bigr]\,r\,\d r}
  {\int_0^R(S'^2+S^2/r^2)\,r\,\d r}.
\end{equation}
Its denominator grows as $S_0^2\ln(R/\elld)$, the elastic
energy of the far field, so the drift depends on the size
of the boundary and slows down as the boundary moves away.

Since $s$ is linear in $w$, so is the estimate, $u_x\simeq
u_a+w\,u_b$, where $u_a$ comes from the cross-coupling and
$u_b$ from $\Y$. For the cross-coupling,
\cref{eq:rho-analytic} gives $s_0$ and $s_2$ in closed
form, and \cref{eq:drift-radial} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-ua}
  u_a=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0}\,
  \frac{\int_0^R\bigl(S'^2P'+SS'\Delta S+S^2P/r^3\bigr)\,r\,\d r}{\int_0^R(S'^2+S^2/r^2)\,r\,\d r},
\end{equation}
with $P=S-(r/R)\,S(R)$ the radial factor of
\cref{eq:rho-analytic} and $\Delta S=S''+S'/r-S/r^2$. The
term $\Y$ has the harmonic $0$ alone,
$s_0=w(\zeta/2\xi_0)(F''+3F'/r)$, which gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:drift-ub}
  u_b=-\frac{\zeta}{2\xi_0}\,
  \frac{\int_0^R(S'+S/r)(F''+3F'/r)\,r\,\d r}{\int_0^R(S'^2+S^2/r^2)\,r\,\d r}.
\end{equation}
With the parameters of \cref{tab:field-params} and
$R=20~\micron$, $u_a=0.28~\micron/\minute$ and
$u_b=-0.51~\micron/\minute$, so the estimate reverses the
drift before $w$ reaches one (dashed lines in
\cref{fig:drift-closure}). With the small-$S$ value
$g_2=1/2$ of \cref{eq:g-small-S} in place of $g_2(S)$,
$u_b$ is smaller in magnitude, so the strong order of the
defect matters too.

In the solver of \cref{app:comoving}, with its box,
boundary conditions and grid, we follow the steady state
from $w=0$ to $w=1$ in small steps of $w$, starting each
solve from the solution at the previous step. As $w$ grows,
$u_x$ decreases and changes sign, so with the exact moments
in $\Y$ the defect moves toward its head (markers in
\cref{fig:drift-closure}). The estimate overshoots the
numerical drift but captures its trend. With $g_2=1/2$ the
defect also moves toward its head at $w=1$, but only
slowly.

Friction anisotropy does not reverse the drift. Copenhagen
\textit{et al.}~\cite{copenhagen2021} attribute the
asymmetry of the flows around defects to a friction that is
stronger across the cells than along them,
$\xi_0(\I-\varepsilon\hat\Q)$ for a single rod with the
anisotropy $\varepsilon$. In our model it acts only on the
passive force, because self-propulsion sets the velocity of
the rods directly, and to first order in $\varepsilon$ and
in the activity it adds to the $\Q$-equation only the
angular harmonics $\ee^{\pm\ii\phi}$ and
$\ee^{\pm3\ii\phi}$, which do not project onto the
translation mode \cref{eq:drift-translation}. The drift
therefore changes only at higher order.


\section{Density variance with a short-length cutoff}
\label{app:variance}
% dean.tex 904-1211 ("Only density fluctuations"); checks: claude_experiments/appF_check.wls,
% fit_new.py, appF_corr.wls
The layering rate depends exponentially on the equal-point
density variance, so the few percent by which the defect
modulates that variance decide the result of
\cref{sec:nucleation-2}. Here we derive the variance from
\cref{eq:rho-fourier} at $\delta\Q=0$, together with the
correlation time and length that \cref{sec:defect-fluct}
quotes. Two features of the calculation need care: the
variance of a continuum diverges, so it depends on a
short-length cutoff, and the nematic order makes the noise
anisotropic.

With the Fourier conventions $\tilde
f(\k)=\int\!\d^2\r\,\ee^{-\ii\k\cdot\r}f(\r)$ in space and
$\tilde f(\omega)=\int\!\d t\,\ee^{-\ii\omega t}f(t)$ in
time, the noise correlator \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss} transforms
to
\begin{multline}
  \la\tilde \eta^\rho_\alpha(\k,\omega)\tilde \eta^\rho_\beta(\k',\omega')\ra = \frac{4\pi\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}\,\delta(\omega+\omega')\,\times
  \\
  \times
  \left[(2\pi)^2 \delta_\ab \delta(\k+\k')\;+\;\tilde Q^\ss_\ab(\k+\k')\right],
\end{multline}
and \cref{eq:rho-fourier} gives
\begin{multline}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,\omega)\delta\tilde\rho(\k',\omega') \ra= \frac{4\pi\zeta\,\delta(\omega+\omega')}{\xi_0\rho_0\,[\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2]\![-\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k'^2]}\times
  \\
  \times
  \!\left[(2\pi)^2 k^2\,\delta(\k+\k')\;-\;k_\alpha k'_\beta\,\tilde Q^\ss_\ab(\k+\k')\right].
\end{multline}
Transforming back to $(t,t')$, the $\delta(\omega+\omega')$
removes one frequency integral. Partial fractions of the
remaining denominator,
\begin{multline}
    \frac{1}{[\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2]\,[-\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k'^2]} = 
    \\
    =\frac{1}{\gamma_0(k^2+k'^2)}\!\left[\frac{1}{\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2}\;+\;\frac{1}{-\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k'^2}\right],
\end{multline}
and the identity $\int\!\frac{\d\omega}{2\pi}\ee^{\ii\omega
t}/(\ii\omega+a) = \theta(t)\ee^{-at}$ applied to each term
give
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,t)\delta\tilde\rho(\k',t')\ra = \frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\,\gamma_0(k^2+k'^2)}\times
  \\
  \times \left[\theta(t-t')\ee^{-\gamma_0 k^2(t-t')}+\theta(t'-t)\ee^{-\gamma_0 k'^2(t'-t)}\right]
  \\
  \times\!\left[(2\pi)^2 k^2\delta(\k+\k') - k_ik'_j\tilde{Q}^\ss_{ij}(\k+\k')\right].
\end{multline}

At equal times, $t=t'$, the support of $\delta(\k+\k')$
sets $k^2=(k^2+k'^2)/2$ in the first term, and the spectrum
becomes
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-spectrum}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k)\,\delta\tilde\rho(\k')\ra
  = \frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\Bigl[{}&(2\pi)^2\delta(\k+\k')
  \nonumber\\
  &-\frac{2k_ik'_j\,\tilde Q^\ss_{ij}(\k+\k')}{k^2+k'^2}\Bigr].
\end{align}
The nematic field enters only through the second term.

\subsection{Isotropic case}
\label{app:variance-iso}
Without nematic order, $\Q_\ss=0$, only the first term of
\cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln}
remains. On the support of $\delta(\k+\k')$ we have
$k'^2=k^2$, so the two exponentials combine into one, and
\begin{equation}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,t)\,\delta\tilde\rho(\k',t')\ra=(2\pi)^2\delta(\k+\k')\,
  \frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\,\ee^{-\gamma_0k^2|t-t'|}.
\end{equation}
Inverting over space gives a heat kernel,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:rho-correlation-no-cutoff}
  \la\delta\rho(\r,t)\,\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\,
  \frac{\ee^{-|\r-\r'|^2/(4\gamma_0|t-t'|)}}{4\pi\gamma_0|t-t'|},
\end{equation}
and as $t'\to t$ it tends to
$(\zeta/\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0)\,\delta(\r-\r')$. At equal
times the density at two different points is therefore
uncorrelated, and the variance at one point diverges.

\subsection{The case with uniform nematic order}
\label{app:variance-uniform}
Let the nematic order be uniform, with strength $S$ and the
director along $x$, so that $Q^\ss_{xx}=-Q^\ss_{yy}=S$ and
$Q^\ss_{xy}=0$. Its transform is $\tilde\Q_\ss(\bs
p)=(2\pi)^2\delta(\bs p)\,\Q_\ss$, so the second term of
\cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln} sits
on $\k'=-\k$ as well, where it becomes
$(2\pi)^2\delta(\k+\k')\,(k_x^2-k_y^2)S$. Both terms then
sit on $\k'=-\k$, where $k'^2=k^2$, so the two exponentials
combine into $\ee^{-\gamma_0k^2|t-t'|}$ and
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:rho-spectrum-uniform}
  \la\delta\tilde\rho(\k,t)\,\delta\tilde\rho(\k',t')\ra
  =(2\pi)^2\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\,\delta(\k+\k')\times
  \\
  \times\bigl[1+S\cos2\psi\bigr]\,\ee^{-\gamma_0k^2|t-t'|},
\end{multline}
with $\psi$ the angle between $\k$ and the director ($x$
axis). Uniform order thus keeps the spectrum diagonal and
only multiplies it by $1+S\cos2\psi$, which averages to one
over the directions of $\k$. A correlation at a single
point integrates over all directions, so the
autocorrelation in time at a fixed point is exactly the
isotropic
$(\zeta/\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0)/(4\pi\gamma_0|t-t'|)$ for
every $t-t'$, and the equal-point variance is that of the
isotropic case as well.

Two distinct points, however, weight the directions of $\k$
unequally. Let $\r'-\r$ make the angle $\alpha$ with the
director. The angular integral of
$\ee^{\ii\k\cdot(\r'-\r)}\cos2\psi$ gives $-2\pi
J_2(k|\r'-\r|)\cos2\alpha$, with $J_2$ the Bessel function
of the first kind of order two~\cite{olver2010nist}, and
the remaining radial integrals are elementary,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:corr-uniform}
  &\la\delta\rho(\r,t)\,\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\,\frac{1}{4\pi\gamma_0|t-t'|}
  \Bigl[\ee^{-u}-S\cos2\alpha\,g(u)\Bigr],
  \nonumber\\
  &g(u)=\frac{1-(1+u)\,\ee^{-u}}{u},\qquad u=\frac{|\r-\r'|^2}{4\gamma_0|t-t'|}.
\end{align}
The first term is the isotropic heat kernel. The second
vanishes at $\r'=\r$, because $g(u)\simeq u/2$ at small
$u$, but at large separation, where $g(u)\simeq1/u$, it
decays only as $-\zeta
S\cos2\alpha/(\pi\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0|\r-\r'|^2)$. This tail
does not depend on the time difference $|t-t'|$, and it
comes from $k\to0$, where the direction factor is not
analytic and the modes relax arbitrarily slowly. Such
power-law correlations are generic when the noise and the
relaxation of a conserved density have different
anisotropies~\cite{grinstein1990conservation,
garrido1990longrange,mahdisoltani2021longrange}. At equal
times the heat kernel collapses to a $\delta$ function and
only the tail is left at finite separation,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:corr-uniform-equal-time}
  \la\delta\rho(\r)\,\delta\rho(\r+\bs d)\ra
  =\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\Bigl[\delta(\bs d)-\frac{S\cos2\alpha}{\pi d^2}\Bigr].
\end{equation}
Uniform order thus anticorrelates two points along the
director and correlates them across it, whereas without
order they are uncorrelated at equal times.

\subsection{Short-length cutoff}
\label{app:variance-cutoff}

The equal-point, equal-time variance of
\cref{eq:rho-correlation-no-cutoff} is infinite, because
the flat isotropic spectrum integrates to infinity over
$\k$. The anisotropic part, from the second term of
\cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln}, grows
only logarithmically with the largest wavevector
(\cref{app:variance-neighbors}), so without a cutoff the
relative modulation by the defect vanishes. We suppress
large wavevectors with a Gaussian filter,
\begin{equation}
  \delta\tilde\rho(\k,\omega) =
  -\frac{\ii\,\k\cdot\tilde \bEta_\rho(\k,\omega)}{\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2} W(\k),
  \qquad W(\k)=\ee^{-k^2\elln^2/4},
\end{equation}
so that the integrals below stay Gaussian. We choose the
factor $1/4$ in the exponent so that the correlation length
of the density in the isotropic case equals $\elln$.

The correlator
\cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic-without-elln} thus
carries the factor $\ee^{-(k^2+k'^2)\elln^2/4}$, which on
the support of $\delta(\k+\k')$ becomes
$\ee^{-k^2\elln^2/2}$. The isotropic spectrum of
\cref{app:variance-iso} then integrates to a finite
equal-point variance,
\begin{equation}
  \sigmarho=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\int\!\frac{\d^2\k}{(2\pi)^2}\,
  \ee^{-k^2\elln^2/2}=\frac{\zeta}{2\pi\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0\elln^2},
\end{equation}
which with $\xi_0\gamma_0=B$ is the pedestal
\cref{eq:pedestal}. The filter $W$ depends on $|\k|$ alone,
so uniform nematic order still leaves the pedestal and the
autocorrelation in time at a point unchanged
(\cref{app:variance-uniform}).

At unequal times the isotropic spectrum carries the
relaxation factor $\ee^{-\gamma_0k^2|t-t'|}$ besides the
smoothing, and inverting over space gives
\begin{equation}
  \la\delta\rho(\r,t)\,\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra=\frac{\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}
  \int\!\frac{\d^2\k}{(2\pi)^2}\,\ee^{\ii\k\cdot(\r-\r')}\,\ee^{-a k^2},
\end{equation}
with $a=\gamma_0|t-t'|+\elln^2/2$, so relaxation over the
time $|t-t'|$ acts like a wider filter. The integral is
Gaussian,
\begin{equation}
  \int\!\frac{\d^2\k}{(2\pi)^2}\,\ee^{\ii\k\cdot\bs d-ak^2}=\frac{\ee^{-d^2/4a}}{4\pi a},
\end{equation}
and with $\xi_0\gamma_0=B$ it gives
\cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic}: a Gaussian in
$\r-\r'$ of width $\elln$ at equal times, whose
normalization $1/4\pi a$ has halved after the correlation
time $\tau_c$ of \cref{eq:tau-c}.

\subsection{The anisotropic piece}
\label{app:variance-aniso}
With the cutoff, the equal-point variance is the integral
of \cref{eq:rho-spectrum} over $\k$ and $\k'$, weighted by
$\ee^{\ii(\k+\k')\cdot\r}\ee^{-(k^2+k'^2)\elln^2/4}$. Its
second term depends on the sum $\bs p=\k+\k'$, which is
conjugate to the slow variation of $\Q_\ss$, and on the
internal wavevector $\bs q=(\k-\k')/2$. We therefore change
variables to
\begin{equation}
  \k=\tfrac12\bs p+\bs q,\qquad\k'=\tfrac12\bs p-\bs q,
\end{equation}
which has unit Jacobian, $\d^2\k\,\d^2\k'=\d^2\bs
p\,\d^2\bs q$. In these variables
\begin{align}
  k_ik'_j&=\tfrac14p_ip_j-q_iq_j+\tfrac12(q_ip_j-p_iq_j),
  \nonumber\\
  k^2+k'^2&=\tfrac12p^2+2q^2 .
\end{align}
The last term of $k_ik'_j$ is antisymmetric and drops
against the symmetric $\tilde\Q_\ss$, and the smoothing
factor splits into $\ee^{-z}\ee^{-q^2\elln^2/2}$ with
$z=p^2\elln^2/8$. The anisotropic part of the variance thus
reads
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:aniso-pq}
  \sigmarho(\r)-\sigmarho\big|_\text{pedestal}
  =-\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\int\!\frac{\d^2\bs p}{(2\pi)^2}\,\ee^{\ii\bs p\cdot\r}\,
  \ee^{-z}\,\tilde Q^\ss_{ij}(\bs p)
  \\
  \times\int\!\frac{\d^2\bs q}{(2\pi)^2}\,
  \frac{\tfrac14p_ip_j-q_iq_j}{p^2/2+2q^2}\,\ee^{-q^2\elln^2/2}.
\end{multline}
The rest of the inner integrand depends only on $q=|\bs
q|$, so $q_iq_j$ averages over the directions of $\bs q$ to
$\tfrac12q^2\delta_{ij}$, which vanishes against the
traceless $\tilde\Q_\ss$. The $p_ip_j$ part leaves an
elementary integral,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:aniso-inner}
  \int\!\frac{\d^2\bs q}{(2\pi)^2}\,\frac{\ee^{-q^2\elln^2/2}}{p^2/2+2q^2}
  =\frac{\ee^{z}E_1(z)}{8\pi},
\end{equation}
with $E_1$ the exponential integral. Its $\ee^{z}$ cancels
the external $\ee^{-z}$ exactly. Since $p_ip_j\tilde
Q^\ss_{ij}$ is minus the Fourier transform of
$\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}$, the anisotropic variance
is the double divergence of the nematic field passed
through a filter,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:aniso-kernel}
  &\sigmarho(\r)-\sigmarho\big|_\text{pedestal}
  \nonumber\\
  &\quad=\frac{\zeta}{8\pi\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0}\!\int\!\frac{\d^2\bs p}{(2\pi)^2}\,
  \ee^{\ii\bs p\cdot\r}\,\hat\Phi(p)\,\widetilde{\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}}(\bs p),
  \nonumber\\
  &\hat\Phi(p)=\tfrac12E_1\bigl(p^2\elln^2/8\bigr).
\end{align}
No expansion and no fitted scale enter it. 
The filter interpolates between two branches,
\begin{alignat}{2}
  \label{eq:Phi-asymptotics}
  \hat\Phi(p)&\simeq\ln\frac1{p\elln}+\frac{3\ln2-\gamma_\mathrm{E}}2
  \quad &(p\elln\ll1),
  \nonumber\\
  \hat\Phi(p)&\simeq\frac4{p^2\elln^2}\,\ee^{-p^2\elln^2/8}
  \quad &(p\elln\gg1),
\end{alignat}
with $\gamma_\mathrm{E}$ Euler's constant. Variations of
the nematic field on scales larger than $\elln$ are
therefore weighted logarithmically, and those below $\elln$
are cut off exponentially.

The inverse transform of $\hat\Phi$ is the real-space
kernel
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kappa-real}
  \kappa(d) = \frac{1-\ee^{-2d^2/\elln^2}}{2\pi\,d^2},
\end{equation}
a function of the distance $d$ between two points. It is
finite at the origin, $\kappa(0)=1/(\pi\elln^2)$, and
beyond $\elln$ it falls off as $1/(2\pi d^2)$, whose
integral produces the logarithm. With the convolution
$(\kappa\star g)(\r)=\int\!\d^2\bs d\,\kappa(d)\,g(\r+\bs
d)$, dividing by the pedestal gives
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:variance-kernel}
  \frac{\sigmarho(\r)}{\sigmarho|_\text{pedestal}}
  = 1 + \frac{\elln^2}{4}\bigl(\kappa\star\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}\bigr)(\r).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Interpretation: average over neighbors}
\label{app:variance-neighbors}
Two integrations by parts move the derivatives in
\cref{eq:variance-kernel} from the nematic field onto the
kernel and give
\begin{equation}
  (\kappa\star\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij})(\r)
  =\int\!\d^2\bs d\;\partial_i\partial_j\kappa(d)\,Q^\ss_{ij}(\r+\bs d),
\end{equation}
without boundary terms, because $\kappa$ and $\kappa'$
decay as $d^{-2}$ and $d^{-3}$, whereas $\Q_\ss$ and its
gradient stay bounded. For the radial kernel,
$\partial_i\partial_j\kappa=\kappa''\hat d_i\hat
d_j+(\kappa'/d)(\delta_{ij}-\hat d_i\hat d_j)$ with
$\hat{\bs d}=\bs d/d$, and $\delta_{ij}$ drops against the
traceless $\Q_\ss$. \Cref{eq:variance-kernel} thus becomes
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:variance-neighbors}
  \frac{\sigmarho(\r)}{\sigmarho|_\text{pedestal}}-1
  &=\int\!\d^2\bs d\;\Omega(d)\;\hat{\bs d}\cdot\Q_\ss(\r+\bs d)\cdot\hat{\bs d},
  \nonumber\\
  \Omega(d)&=\frac{\elln^2}4\Bigl(\kappa''-\frac{\kappa'}d\Bigr),
\end{align}
and the derivatives of \cref{eq:kappa-real} give the weight
\cref{eq:omega-weight}. For $d\gg\elln$ it falls off as
$\elln^2/(\pi d^4)$. The averaged quantity is $\hat{\bs
d}\cdot\Q_\ss\cdot\hat{\bs d}=S\cos2\alpha$, where
$\alpha=\theta_\text{nb}-\theta_{\bs d}$ is the angle
between $\hat{\bs d}$ and the director at the neighbor
(\cref{fig:neighbors}(a)), as in
\cref{app:variance-uniform}, so
\cref{eq:variance-neighbors} is \cref{eq:total-gauss}.

In a uniform nematic, $\cos2\alpha$ averages to zero over
the directions of $\hat{\bs d}$, so only the change of the
nematic order across the neighborhood of a point survives
the average. This change grows with the distance from the
point, and the neighbors farther than $2\elln$ therefore
supply most of the modulation at its peak, although they
carry only a quarter of the weight $\Omega$. Around a
$+1/2$ defect the change comes from the bending of the
director lines (\cref{fig:aimed-rods}(b,d)).

The dependence of the modulation on the cutoff follows from
the filter $\hat\Phi$ of \cref{eq:aniso-kernel}. When
$\elln$ is much smaller than the core size $\elld$, the
nematic field varies only on scales of $\elld$ and larger,
so its transform is supported at $p\lesssim1/\elld$, where
$\hat\Phi$ takes its logarithmic branch
(\cref{eq:Phi-asymptotics}). The anisotropic term then
reduces to
$(\zeta/8\pi\xi_0\rho_0\gamma_0)\,\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}\ln(\elld/\elln)$
plus a term of order one, and relative to the pedestal the
peak modulation behaves as
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:nu0-asymptote}
  \nu_0\simeq\elln^2\Bigl[\tfrac14\max\bigl|\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}\bigr|
  \ln\frac{\elld}{\elln}+c\Bigr]
\end{equation}
at small $\elln$, where the constant $c$ depends on the
shape of the profile. The modulation thus vanishes as
$\elln\to0$. \Cref{fig:ln_curve} shows
\cref{eq:total-gauss} evaluated in full, with
$\nu_0=2.94\%$ at $\elln=0.5~\micron$. As $\elln$ grows,
the peak of the modulation also moves away from the core,
because the filter smooths the nematic field over a larger
region.

\section{Density variance at a $-1/2$ defect}
\label{app:negative}
% Source: plotgen/negative-defect/negative_defect.wls (numbers.txt)
A $-1/2$ defect feels no net active force and does not
drift, but \cref{eq:total-gauss} holds for any steady
nematic field, so it modulates the density variance too. We
evaluate \cref{eq:total-gauss} at $\delta\Q=0$, on the
profile \cref{eq:pade-accurate} and at the parameters of
\cref{app:parameters}, and compare it with the same
treatment at a $+1/2$ defect, which gives $\nu_0=2.94\%$
(\cref{app:variance-neighbors}).


% Checks: claude_experiments/negdefect_neighbors.py, negdefect_diagnostics.py, negdefect_extra.py
In the polar coordinates of \cref{sec:defect-steady}, a
defect of charge $\pm1/2$ has the director at the angle
$\theta=\pm\phi/2$, and both defects share the radial
profile $S(r)$. The nematic field of the $-1/2$ defect has
threefold rotational symmetry
(\cref{fig:defects-layering}(a)), so the variance repeats
every third of a turn around the core. A rotation by half
as much, $\pi/3$, turns the director by a right angle
everywhere, which reverses the sign of
$S\cos2(\theta_\text{nb}-\theta_{\bs d})$ in
\cref{eq:total-gauss}. At a $-1/2$ defect the variance is
therefore raised in three lobes and lowered in three lobes
between them (\cref{fig:negative-3d}(b)).

At a point on the ray $\phi=0$ the director lies along the
ray. Between the point and the core the rods above and
below the ray tilt toward the line to the point, and beyond
the point they tilt away from it, but less, as at the head
of the $+1/2$ defect (\cref{fig:aimed-rods}(b)). The
variance is therefore raised on this ray at every distance
from the core. The raised lobes thus lie at
$\phi=0,\pm2\pi/3$, where the director lies radially, and
the lowered ones at $\phi=\pi,\pm\pi/3$, where it runs
around the core.

The modulation peaks at $\nu_0^{(-)}=1.35\%$ at
$r=3.3~\micron$, less than half of $\nu_0$ at the $+1/2$
defect and more than twice as far from the core. Both
differences come from the threefold symmetry of the
director and not from the loss of order at the core: with
$S$ held at $S_0$ everywhere, \cref{eq:total-gauss} gives
nearly the same ratios, $0.45$ and $2.2$. At a $+1/2$
defect the whole head side is raised. At a $-1/2$ defect,
in contrast, the neighbors of a point on a raised ray close
to the core reach into the lowered lobes on both sides, and
these nearly cancel the contribution of the raised lobe.
The modulation builds up only farther out, where the
neighbors of the point lie mostly within one lobe, but
there the director bends more slowly, so the peak is lower.
The variance is a smooth function of position, and a smooth
function that reverses its sign under a rotation by $\pi/3$
about the core starts from it with cubic terms, such as
$r^3\cos3\phi=x^3-3xy^2$. The $-1/2$ modulation therefore
rises from the core as $r^3$, whereas the $+1/2$
modulation, which is odd in $x$, rises as $r$. At $r=\elld$
the $-1/2$ modulation is only $0.26\%$, against $2.67\%$ on
the head side of the $+1/2$ defect.

\Cref{fig:negative-layering} turns these variances into the
layering rate of \cref{eq:p-layer}, averaged over circles
about the core (\cref{eq:p-layer-radial} with $\r_\mathrm
c=0$). The model thus raises the layering rate near $-1/2$
defects too, but less than near $+1/2$ defects and farther
from the core. Near $-1/2$ defects, however,
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} observes mainly holes opening,
which need additional modeling
(\cref{sec:overlook-and-future-work}).

\section{Lyapunov solver for the coupled fluctuations}
\label{app:lyapunov}
% dean.tex 1521-2089.
The analytic variance \cref{eq:total-gauss} neglects the
force $-\zeta\nabla\cdot\delta\Q$ of the nematic
fluctuations (\cref{sec:defect-fluct}). To test this
approximation, we solve the linearized equations
\cref{eq:linearized} numerically for the equal-time
covariance of $(\delta\rho,\delta\Q)$ on the steady
background of \cref{app:comoving}.

\Cref{app:lyapunov-method} turns the linearized equations
into a Lyapunov equation for the covariance and states when
it has a valid solution. On the grid, the noise then needs
a covariance built from the exact moments of the
maximum-entropy angular distribution, so that it is
positive semi-definite (\cref{app:realizability}),
difference operators that resolve the same details as those
of the drift (\cref{app:lyapunov-operators}), and a spatial
correlation that imposes the cutoff \cref{eq:cutoff} and is
calibrated to the grid (\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff}).
\Cref{app:lyapunov-walls} gives the boundary conditions,
and \cref{app:lyapunov-results} compares the solution with
the analytic variance and shows the fluctuations of the
active force.

\subsection{Method}
\label{app:lyapunov-method}
% dean.tex 1521-2047; solver: lyapunov_solver/lyapunov_solver.m, lyap_solve.py;
% numbers: claude_experiments/appG_numbers.wls, appG_force.wls; job log jobscripts/logs/1773058_2.out
In the variables $\psi=(\delta\rho,\delta Q_1,\delta Q_2)$
the linearized equations \cref{eq:linearized} are a linear
Ornstein--Uhlenbeck field theory,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:ou-model}
  \partial_t\psi=\A\psi+\bxi,
  \qquad
  \la\bxi(\r,t)\,\bxi(\r',t')^\T\ra=\mathbf N(\r,\r')\,\delta(t-t'),
\end{equation}
where the drift $\A$ and the noise covariance $\mathbf N$
are linear operators on fields. Both contain derivatives,
$\mathbf N$ because the flux noises $\bEta_\rho$ and
$\bEta^\Q$ enter through their divergence. The flux
correlators are
\cref{eq:eta-rho-ss,eq:Q-noise-correlators}, evaluated with
the exact moments of \cref{app:realizability}. Since the
noise is white in time, It\^o's lemma applied to
$\psi\psi^\T$ turns stationarity into an algebraic
condition on the equal-time covariance $\mathbf
C(\r,\r')=\langle\psi(\r,t)\,\psi(\r',t)^\T\rangle$,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:lyapunov}
  \A\,\mathbf C+\mathbf C\,\A^\T+\mathbf N=0,
\end{equation}
with $\A$ acting on the first argument of $\mathbf C$ and
$\A^\T$ on the second. This step needs only the delta
function in time, so the spatial form of $\mathbf N$ is
free, which lets \cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff} give the noise
a finite correlation length. \Cref{eq:lyapunov} is the
Lyapunov equation of control theory
\cite{lyapunov1992general,zhou1996robust}. Its solution,
$\mathbf C=\int_0^\infty\!\d t\,\ee^{\A t}\,\mathbf
N\,\ee^{\A^\T t}$, adds up the noise injected at every
earlier time and carried to the present by the drift
\cite{zhou1996robust,simoncini2016computational}.

On a grid, \cref{eq:lyapunov} is a matrix equation with a
unique positive semi-definite solution if ($i$)~every
eigenvalue of the discretized $\A$ has a negative real
part, so that the steady state is linearly stable, and
($ii$)~the discretized $\mathbf N$ is positive
semi-definite, so that it is a covariance at all.

We use a uniform grid of $N_\mathrm{int}=200$ interior
nodes per direction on $[-20,20]^2~\micron^2$, with spacing
$\Delta x=40/201~\micron=0.199~\micron$, so the state
vector has $3N_\mathrm{int}^2=120\,000$ entries.
Two-dimensional operators are Kronecker products of
one-dimensional ones. The covariance is a dense matrix, and
we solve \cref{eq:lyapunov} directly: a real Schur
decomposition of the discretized $\A$ is followed by a
recursive Bartels--Stewart back-substitution
\cite{bartels1972solution}, which keeps most of the work in
matrix products. The Schur decomposition also yields the
spectrum of $\A$, and in our run every eigenvalue has a
negative real part, so condition ($i$) holds.

\subsection{Realizability of the noise covariance}
\label{app:realizability}
Condition ($ii$) is not automatic. The noise covariance
$\mathbf N$ is assembled from the local covariance of the
noise fluxes at each point (\cref{app:lyapunov-operators}),
so it is positive semi-definite only if that local
covariance is. If it is not, some combination of the noises
has a negative variance. No noise can then have the
correlators \cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators}, and
\cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} are ill defined. The local
covariance comes from the moments that the closure of
\cref{app:closure} assigns to the angular distribution. The
lowest-order closure keeps it valid only for weak order,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:realizability-condition}
  S\le S_\star \simeq 0.618,
\end{equation}
whereas around the defect the nematic order reaches
$S_0=0.9$.

We collect the three flux noises at a point, $\eta^\rho_i$,
$\eta^\Q_{11i}$ and $\eta^\Q_{12i}$, into a vector with six
components $(a,i)$. Each is an angular integral of the
reversal noise,
$\int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,u_{(ai)}(\theta)\,k$,
with the weights
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:u-vector}
  \bs u(\theta)=\bigl(\p,\ \hat Q_{11}\p,\ \hat Q_{12}\p\bigr).
\end{equation}
By \cref{eq:dean-noise-sum}, their covariance is
$\M\Delta'$, where $\Delta'=\delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t')$ as
in \cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators}, with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:gram-matrix}
  \M=\frac{4\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,\bs u\,\bs u^\T .
\end{equation}
This $6\times6$ matrix holds the density correlator
\cref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation} and the first two
correlators of \cref{eq:Q-noise-correlators}. Since the
weight $\varrho_t$ is non-negative, $\M$ is a Gram matrix,
and $\bs v^\T\M\bs
v=(4\zeta/\xi_0\rho_0)\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t\,(\bs
v\cdot\bs u)^2\ge0$ for every $\bs v$. The covariance of
$\f^\Q$ is a Gram matrix in the same way. Every genuine
distribution, and in particular the von Mises distribution
with the exact moments \cref{eq:exact-moments}, therefore
gives a valid covariance.

There is no margin, however. Since $\hat\Q\cdot\p=\p$ for
every $\theta$, the fluxes obey the two exact relations
$\eta^\Q_{\alpha\beta\beta}=\eta^\rho_\alpha$, so $\M$ has
two zero eigenvalues whatever the distribution.
Equivalently, the six components of $\bs u$ span only the
four functions $\cos\theta$, $\sin\theta$, $\cos3\theta$
and $\sin3\theta$. A covariance built from approximate
moments can move these two eigenvalues in either direction,
and a negative eigenvalue makes it invalid.

This is what happens with the lowest-order closure. The
products $u_{(ai)}u_{(bj)}$ contain only the harmonics
$n=0,2,4,6$, so $\M$ depends on the distribution only
through $\rho$ and the complex harmonics
$z_n=\rho^{-1}\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\,\ee^{\ii n\theta}$
with $n=2,4,6$. The closure \cref{eq:leading-closure} keeps
$z_2=S\ee^{2\ii\theta_0}$ ($\theta_0$ is the angle of the
director), which $\Q$ fixes, and sets $z_4=0$ through
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho=\rho\J$ and $z_6=0$
through
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho=\tfrac12\bT$. Its
$\M$ is valid only if some non-negative distribution has
these harmonics, which is a truncated trigonometric moment
problem. Once the phase of $z_2$ is rotated away, such a
distribution exists if and only if the Toeplitz matrix of
the harmonics,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:toeplitz}
  \mathcal T=\begin{pmatrix}1&S&0&0\\S&1&S&0\\0&S&1&S\\0&0&S&1\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
is positive semi-definite \cite{grenander1958toeplitz}. Its
eigenvalues are $1+2S\cos(k\pi/5)$ with $k=1,\dots,4$, and
the smallest, $1-2S\cos(\pi/5)$, turns negative above
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:golden-bound}
  S_\star=\frac1{2\cos(\pi/5)}=\frac{\sqrt5-1}2\simeq0.618 .
\end{equation}
Above $S_\star$, no non-negative distribution has the
moments of the closure, and its $\M$ acquires a negative
eigenvalue. We checked numerically that the smallest
eigenvalue of $\M$ changes sign exactly at $S_\star$, so
the bound is sharp.

The defect steady state lies far above this bound. In the
steady state of \cref{app:comoving}, $S$ rises from $0.10$
next to the core to $0.90$ far from it, and it exceeds
$S_\star$ everywhere except within about $1.5~\micron$ of
the core. Keeping the $\U$ term of \cref{eq:exact-moments},
with $g_2$ at its small-$S$ value $1/2$, raises the bound
only to $S\le0.775$. The analytic calculation of
\cref{sec:defect-fluct} is not affected, because at
$\delta\Q=0$ it needs only the density correlator
\cref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation}, which is exact. The
coupled equations need the full covariance of
$(\bEta_\rho,\bEta^\Q,\f^\Q)$, so the solver builds it from
the exact moments \cref{eq:exact-moments} of the von Mises
distribution, which give a Gram matrix at every $S$,
solving \cref{eq:kappa-S} once per node.

\subsection{Consistent difference operators}
\label{app:lyapunov-operators}
% dean.tex 1556-1910; lyapunov_solver/lyapunov_solver.m (915-960 operators, 1166-1183 drift,
% 1241-1291 noise); checks: claude_experiments/fd_operators/check_operators.py, cutoff_run_resolution.py
On a grid of spacing $\Delta x$
(\cref{app:lyapunov-method}) a field is known only at the
nodes, so it can carry details down to the size $\Delta x$:
the shortest wave it holds alternates from node to node. A
difference operator, in turn, resolves details only down to
a length set by the nodes it combines. The compact second
difference
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fd-compact}
  (Lu)_n=\frac{u_{n+1}-2u_n+u_{n-1}}{\Delta x^2}
\end{equation}
combines a node with its two neighbors and resolves details
down to $\Delta x$. A Laplacian can also be built from the
central first difference $(Du)_n=(u_{n+1}-u_{n-1})/2\Delta
x$, but applied twice it skips the neighbors
(\cref{fig:staggered-grid}(a)),
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fd-central-twice}
  (D^2u)_n=\frac{u_{n+2}-2u_n+u_{n-2}}{4\Delta x^2}\neq(Lu)_n ,
\end{equation}
and so resolves details only down to $2\Delta x$. The wave
that alternates from node to node takes the same value on
every second node, so $D^2$ gives zero for it. Both
operators approximate the Laplacian for waves much longer
than $\Delta x$ and differ only on the scale of the grid.

In the Lyapunov equation \cref{eq:lyapunov} this difference
matters, because the drift and the noise both contain such
operators and balance each other. In the continuum the
spectrum of the density fluctuations is flat, because the
factor $k^2$ of the noise power cancels the factor $k^2$ of
the relaxation rate (\cref{sec:defect-fluct}). Every
wavevector below the cutoff contributes equally, so the
variance is set by the shortest waves that are kept, on the
scale of the grid. If the noise and the drift are
discretized with operators that resolve different details,
they no longer cancel there, and the variance comes out
wrong. All operators of \cref{eq:lyapunov} must therefore
be discretized consistently.

The symbol of an operator makes this precise. It is the
factor by which the operator multiplies a plane wave
$u_n=\ee^{\ii kn\Delta x}$ \cite{leveque2007finite}, just
as the continuum Laplacian multiplies $\ee^{\ii kx}$ by
$-k^2$. For the two discrete Laplacians,
\begin{align}
  \hat L(k)&=-\frac{4}{\Delta x^2}\sin^2\frac{k\Delta x}{2},
  \nonumber\\
  \label{eq:fd-symbols}
  \hat D(k)^2&=-\frac{\sin^2k\Delta x}{\Delta x^2}=\hat L(k)\cos^2\frac{k\Delta x}{2},
\end{align}
with $\hat D(k)=\ii\sin(k\Delta x)/\Delta x$. Both approach
$-k^2$ for $k\Delta x\ll1$. For the shortest wave of the
grid, $k\Delta x=\pi$, the symbol of $L$ is $-4/\Delta
x^2$, whereas that of $D^2$ vanishes. On the grid each
factor $k^2$ of the spectrum becomes the symbol of a
difference operator, so the spectrum stays flat only if the
noise and the drift have equal symbols.

The forward difference $G$ resolves the same details as
$L$. It takes the first derivative between two nodes, on
the face $n+1/2$, $(Gu)_{n+1/2}=(u_{n+1}-u_n)/\Delta x$.
Its negative transpose, $(-G^\T
F)_n=(F_{n+1/2}-F_{n-1/2})/\Delta x$, takes a face flux $F$
back to the nodes as a divergence
(\cref{fig:staggered-grid}(b)), so that whatever leaves one
node enters its neighbor. Applying $G$ and then $-G^\T$
gives the compact Laplacian exactly, $L=-G^\T G$. This is
the staggered grid of computational fluid dynamics, which
avoids the decoupling of neighboring nodes that $D^2$
produces \cite{ferziger2020computational}.

The density noise in \cref{eq:ou-model} is the divergence
of the flux noise $\bEta_\rho$, so its covariance contains
a first difference on each side. If this divergence is
taken with $D$, the noise carries $|\hat D|^2=-\hat
L\cos^2(k\Delta x/2)$ (\cref{eq:fd-symbols}) where the
drift carries $\hat L$, so the noise power falls short of
the drift by the factor $\cos^2(k\Delta x/2)$ at short
wavelengths. We take the divergence with $G$ instead, as is
done in fluctuating hydrodynamics
\cite{donev2010accuracy,balboa2012staggered,
kim2017stochastic}, so that the noise and the drift share
one operator and the spectrum is exactly flat.

In two dimensions, with $G_x$ and $G_y$ taking the nodes to
the faces between horizontal and vertical neighbors,
respectively, the drift of the density is $(B/\xi_0)L$ with
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fd-identity}
  L=-G_x^\T G_x-G_y^\T G_y .
\end{equation}
Without the spatial correlation of
\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff}, the noise flux $\bEta_\rho$
lives on the faces, independent from face to face, with the
local covariance
$M^\rho_\ab=(2\zeta/\xi_0\rho_0)(\delta_\ab+Q^\ss_\ab)$ of
\cref{eq:eta-rho-ss} sampled there, so the diagonal part of
the density noise is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:fd-noise}
  \mathbf N_{\rho\rho}=\frac1{\Delta x^2}\sum_{i=x,y}G_i^\T\,\mathrm{diag}(M^\rho_{ii})\,G_i ,
\end{equation}
where $1/\Delta x^2$ is the grid form of $\delta(\r-\r')$.
For an isotropic noise the noise matrix is then a multiple
of the drift matrix, the spectrum is flat on the grid, and
every node has the same variance.

The other divergence terms of \cref{eq:linearized} follow
the same construction. A drift term
$\nabla\cdot(c\nabla\,\cdot\,)$ with a scalar coefficient
$c$ becomes $-\sum_iG_i^\T\,\mathrm{diag}(c)\,G_i$ with $c$
on the faces. In the equation for $\delta Q_\ab$, the
transport term
$(B/\xi_0)\nabla\cdot(Q^\ss_\ab\nabla\delta\rho)$ has this
form, with $c=(B/\xi_0)Q^\ss_\ab$. The advection
$\nabla\cdot(\delta\Q\,\bs w_\ss)$ is $-G^\T$ applied to
the face flux $\bs w_\ss\,\delta\Q$, with $\delta\Q$
averaged onto the faces. The noise of $\delta\Q$ and its
correlation with $\bEta_\rho$ are assembled in the same
way. Only the mixed noise terms,
$M^\rho_{xy}=M^\rho_{yx}\propto Q^\ss_{xy}$, keep the
central difference at the nodes. Their symbol is
proportional to $\sin(k_x\Delta x)\sin(k_y\Delta x)$, which
is odd in $k_x$ and averages to zero, as in the continuum,
where uniform nematic order leaves the variance unchanged
(\cref{app:variance-uniform}). Derivatives taken on
different faces would instead add a spurious pedestal
proportional to $Q^\ss_{xy}$.

Each diagonal term of \cref{eq:fd-noise} is a congruence,
so it is positive semi-definite wherever the local
covariance is. With the exact moments of
\cref{app:realizability} the local covariance is valid at
every node, and the solver checks it there with a
$6\times6$ eigensolve.

pedestal $\zeta/(B\rho_0\Delta x^2)$, which is
\cref{eq:pedestal} with $\elln=\Delta x/\sqrt{2\pi}$. To
impose the cutoff \cref{eq:cutoff} instead, we use a noise

We construct it from the white noise $\bxi$ of

\begin{equation*}

\end{equation*}
with $W(\r,\r_1)$ the kernel of $W$ and $\mathbf
N(\r_1,\r_2)$ the covariance of \cref{eq:ou-model}. For any
field $\bs v$ the quadratic form of $W\mathbf NW$ equals
that of $\mathbf N$ evaluated on $W\bs v$, so the
correlated covariance is positive semi-definite whenever
$\mathbf N$ is. Moreover, $W$ commutes with the drift
$(B/\xi_0)\nabla^2$ of the density, so at $\delta\Q=0$ the
correlated noise gives the same density covariance as the
density smoothed with $W$, which is what
\cref{app:variance-cutoff} computes.

\end{equation*}

  \chi=2\pi\frac{\elln^2}{\Delta x^2}\,\mathcal I\Bigl(\frac{\elln}{\Delta x}\Bigr)^2

\end{equation}

  \mathcal I(\ell_\mathrm{eff}/\Delta x)=\frac{\Delta x}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\elln}.
\end{equation}
\Cref{fig:cutoff-calibration} shows the ratio

\subsection{Boundary conditions}
\label{app:lyapunov-walls}
The solver needs three boundary conditions: on the steady
state, on the fluctuating fields, and on the noise. The
third is easy to overlook: the flux noises $\bEta_\rho$ and
$\bEta^\Q$ also move density and alignment through the
boundary, on the outer faces of the grid
(\cref{fig:staggered-grid}(b)), and the condition on
$\delta\rho$ and $\delta\Q$ does not decide whether they
may.

The last two conditions must agree, because both decide
whether a cell may cross the boundary. A reservoir pins the
fluctuations and lets the noise flux through, like a window
cut from a larger colony, and a sealed boundary blocks
both, like a container. A mixed choice fails. With pinned
fluctuations and a blocked noise flux, fluctuations relax
through the boundary without being replaced, and the
variance next to it falls below the pedestal. With a sealed
drift and an open noise flux, the number of cells in the
box, which the drift conserves, fluctuates without bound.

We use the reservoir. The solver removes its boundary
nodes, where the fluctuations then vanish, and builds $W$
on the Dirichlet Laplacian, so that the correlated noise
vanishes there as well. The density condition is the one of
the steady state in \cref{app:comoving}. Only the nematic
condition differs: the steady state leaves $\Q_\ss$ free at
the boundary, $\nh\cdot\nabla\Q_\ss=0$, whereas the
fluctuations are anchored there, $\delta\Q=0$.

\subsection{Results}
\label{app:lyapunov-results}
% Numbers: claude_experiments/round_2026_10_26/varrho_run.log, report_full_solution/numbers_fluct.tex;
% force figures: plotgen/force_fluct/force_fluct.wls, run.sh
Away from the core the variance from the solver returns to
the pedestal \cref{eq:pedestal}, as the calibration of
\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff} requires. Near the core it
differs from \cref{eq:total-gauss} evaluated on the same
steady state $\Q_\ss$ (dotted in \cref{fig:variance}) by at
most $0.14\%$ of the pedestal along the axis and $0.05\%$
across it, a small fraction of the modulation itself. The
difference has two sources. The first is the fluctuating
order $\delta\Q$, which \cref{eq:total-gauss} drops. The
density that $\delta\Q$ drives adds about $10^{-4}$ of the
pedestal at the maximum of the variance, and its
correlation with the density at fixed order removes about
as much (computed on the grid $N_\mathrm{int}=180$), so
this source is an order of magnitude below the difference.
The second, and main, source is the lattice correction of
the anisotropic term (\cref{app:lyapunov-cutoff}). It
shrinks as the grid is refined: from $N_\mathrm{int}=180$
to $200$, the peak of the solver rises from $2.39\%$ to
$2.42\%$, toward the $2.56\%$ of \cref{eq:total-gauss} on
$\Q_\ss$.

The solver also gives the fluctuations of the active force
$\bfa=-\zeta\bs f$, $f_\alpha=\partial_\beta Q_\ab$, which
$\delta\Q$ carries as $\delta f_\alpha=\partial_\beta\delta
Q_\ab$ and which the approximation $\delta\Q=0$ of
\cref{sec:defect-fluct} drops. This force is not small. Its
fluctuations exceed the mean force everywhere along the
axis of the defect, a few times near the core and
increasingly away from it, where the mean force decays and
the fluctuations level off at a uniform value
(\cref{fig:force-axis}). Around the core the force
fluctuates mostly along the axis of the defect, the
direction of the mean active force
(\cref{fig:force-axis,fig:force-map}). The traction
measured in Ref.~\cite{han2025local} shows the same
pattern: its fluctuations lie far above the mean around
defects, and they stay strong in aligned regions, where the
mean traction nearly vanishes. The traction also contains
the pressure gradient, however, so this resemblance is
qualitative.

The density variance nevertheless changes little, as shown
above. On the scale of the cutoff the nematic order relaxes
at most at the rate $4K'/(\xir\elln^2)=20~\minute^{-1}$,
nearly four orders of magnitude below the rate
$1/\tau_c\simeq10^5~\minute^{-1}$ at which the density
relaxes there (\cref{eq:tau-c}), so the density responds to
the force of $\delta\Q$ as to a static force
(\cref{sec:defect-fluct}).
