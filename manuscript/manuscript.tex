\title{Fluctuating active nematics account for layer formation in \textit{Myxococcus xanthus} colonies}

\author{Ashot Matevosyan}
\author{Ricard Alert}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
In colonies of \textit{Myxococcus xanthus}, cells leave the monolayer and build a second
layer preferentially at $+1/2$ topological defects of the cell-orientation field
\cite{copenhagen2021}. The mean compression a defect sustains falls three to four orders
of magnitude short of the pressure needed to extrude a cell, so the events cannot be the
mean crossing a threshold; they are rare excursions of a fluctuating stress, and these
monolayers carry traction fluctuations larger than the mean active stress
\cite{han2025local}. An active nematic has no fluctuation-dissipation relation to fix the
amplitude of its noise, so we obtain that amplitude from the microscopic dynamics rather
than supplying it: we coarse-grain $N$ self-propelled rods that reverse at rate $\frev$
into a Dean equation \cite{dean1996}, and integrate out the orientation to reach
stochastic equations for the density and the nematic order parameter. The reversal rate
fixes the mean active stress and the noise amplitude together, with no freedom between
them. Around a $+1/2$ defect these equations give a pressure variance modulated by about
three percent over a few micrometers. Because the extrusion threshold lies nineteen
standard deviations into the tail, that three percent is amplified into a
two-orders-of-magnitude enhancement of the layering probability, peaking off the defect
core, which is what Fig.~3f of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} measures.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% Pages: ~1.5. Source: new. structure.md \S1.
% ============================================================
A monolayer of \textit{Myxococcus xanthus} on a wet surface is a dry active nematic. The
cells are rods a few micrometers long that glide along their own axis, and the substrate
takes up their momentum. The monolayer contains $\pm1/2$ topological defects. Cells
leave the monolayer and form new layers throughout the colony. Unlike in growing
biofilms, layering needs no critical colony size \cite{copenhagen2021}. Layers form
about $200$ times more often near $+1/2$ defects than far from them
(\cref{fig:main-2}(b) and \cref{fig:main-1}(a)), and holes open about $80$ times more often near $-1/2$ defects
\cite{copenhagen2021}.

Forming a layer is a nucleation problem (\cref{fig:main-1}(b)). A patch of cells that
forms a new layer pays to detach from the substrate and from its neighbors, and to open
new water surface along its perimeter. It gains the work of the monolayer pressure over
its area. In classical nucleation the barrier is fixed, and fluctuations change the size
of the nucleus. A bacterial monolayer has no fluctuations that assemble a nucleus of many
cells. Instead, the pressure fluctuates, and the barrier fluctuates with it. No nucleus
can be smaller than one cell, so a layer forms once a pressure fluctuation shrinks the
critical nucleus to one cell. This sets a critical pressure
$\pcrit\simeq35~\kPa\cdot\micron$, fixed by adhesion, surface tension and cell
size.

A $+1/2$ defect carries a polar active force density, which compresses the monolayer on
one side of the core. This compression alone cannot form a layer. The mean pressure at a
defect is of the order of the active stress, three to four orders of magnitude below
$\pcrit$. Layer formation therefore needs a rare pressure fluctuation. These monolayers
fluctuate strongly: the standard deviation of the traction measured in
Ref.~\cite{han2025local} is about ten times its mean. The layering probability is
set by the probability that the local pressure exceeds $\pcrit$.

Computing that probability needs the noise, which a continuum active-nematic theory
\cite{marchetti2013hydrodynamics} does not supply. In equilibrium, the
fluctuation-dissipation theorem ties the noise to the dissipation in the equations. An
active nematic is driven and has no such relation. Noise added at the continuum level
has a free amplitude and tensor structure, and the layering probability depends on both
exponentially. We derive both. We start from $N$ self-propelled rods that reverse their
gliding direction at rate $\frev$, as \textit{M.\ xanthus} does (\cref{fig:main-2}(a)). We
coarse-grain them into a Dean equation \cite{dean1996} for the joint density in position
and orientation, and integrate out the orientation.

The result is a pair of stochastic equations for the density and the nematic order
parameter. The reversals generate an active stress with coefficient
$\zeta=\xi_0v_0^2/(4\frev)$, which grows with the distance $v_0/\frev$ a cell glides
between reversals. The same $\zeta$ sets the amplitude of the density noise, and the
coarse-graining fixes how that noise depends on the nematic order.

Solved around a $+1/2$ defect, these equations give a density variance with two parts.
One is the variance of a uniform monolayer, set by the noise amplitude, the stiffness
of the monolayer and the cell width. The other follows the nematic texture, decays away from the
core, and changes the local variance by about three percent. The critical pressure lies
about nineteen standard deviations out in the tail of the pressure distribution. This
amplifies the small change in the variance into a factor of about $2\times10^2$ in the
layering probability. With the stiffness fitted, the result matches the enhancement measured near
$+1/2$ defects (\cref{fig:main-1}(a)).

\Cref{sec:nucleation-1} describes layer formation as nucleation over a fluctuating
barrier and reduces it to the statistics of the pressure. \Cref{sec:model} derives the
stochastic equations from the microscopic model. \Cref{sec:defect} solves them at a
$+1/2$ defect, first for the steady state and then for the fluctuations.
\Cref{sec:nucleation-2} computes the layering probability and compares it with
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}. \Cref{sec:discussion} collects what is fitted, what is
approximate, and what would test the mechanism.

\section{Layer formation as a nucleation event}
\label{sec:nucleation-1}
% Mechanism and motivation. The calculation resumes in \cref{sec:nucleation-2} once
% \cref{sec:model,sec:defect} supply the pressure variance. Assembled 2026-09-25 from
% Ashot's draft, the energetics and threshold parts of the former sec:nucleation-old
% (dean.tex 1284-1390), and Report 9 (R. Alert, 2020).
% Rewritten 2026-09-28 as one narrative without subsections: defect compression ->
% energy of a layer -> p_crit -> the mean pressure falls short -> fluctuating barrier ->
% p_layer -> why Sections III-IV compute the density fluctuations. The reasoning behind
% the parameter values moved to \cref{app:parameters-extrusion}.
% ============================================================


New layers are formed when it is energetically favorable. We model this from a nucleation theory perspective. First, we calculate the energy cost of a new layer.

We assume that cells adhere both to the substrate and
to other cells, and that water completely wets the colony. Let $\Wc$ and $\Ws$ be the
cell-cell and cell-substrate adhesion energies per unit area, and $\gammaw$ the surface
tension of water. Suppose a patch of cells of area $A$ and perimeter $\ell$ forms a new
layer (\cref{fig:main-1}(b)). The cells of the patch need to (\textit{i}) detach from the
substrate, (\textit{ii}) detach from their neighbors, and (\textit{iii}) work against the surface tension of
water. This costs $\Ws A + \Wc\ell h + \gammaw\ell h$, where $h$ is the height of the
monolayer. At the same time, the bottom layer relaxes to fill the space the patch left.
The energy gain comes from (\textit{iv}) the adhesion of the patch to the cells beneath it and (\textit{v})
the work done by the pressure, $\Wc A + pA$. Here $p$ is the two-dimensional pressure of
the monolayer, a force per unit length. 
The cell-cell adhesion $\Wc$ enters twice with opposite signs: as a cost on
the side faces and as a gain on the bottom face. For a circular patch, $\ell=\sqrt{4\pi A}$, and the energy cost of the new layer is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dE-of-A}
    \Delta E(A) = (\Ws-\Wc-p)\,A + (\Wc+\gammaw)\,h\sqrt{4\pi A}.
\end{equation}
which is a function of the patch area $A$ and monolayer pressure $p$.

The perimeter term is a cost and grows as $\sqrt A$. The area term grows as $A$ and is a
gain once $p>\Ws-\Wc$. At such pressures $\Delta E(A)$ rises at small $A$, reaches a
maximum and then falls. This is the barrier of classical nucleation theory (\cref{fig:main-2}(c)). Its maximum
is the critical nucleus, of area $A^*=\pi h^2(\Wc+\gammaw)^2/(p+\Wc-\Ws)^2$. A larger patch
lowers its energy by growing, a smaller one by shrinking.

In classical nucleation theory, thermal fluctuations or impurities in the system move $A$ along a fixed curve $\Delta E(A)$.
In our system, because of active and passive flctuations, the pressure fluctuates. The barier moves with it together with the critical nucleus area $A^*$.

Thermal energy is
negligible against the adhesion and surface energies of even one cell. Cells glide in the
plane of the substrate, and nothing lifts a group of them off it together. A new layer must therefore start from a single cell, as already observed in the experiments. If the maximum of $\Delta E(A)$ lies below one cell area($A^*<\Acell$), growth from
that cell is downhill of the energy. Single-cell perturbations, such as substrate roughness or a division event, then complete the extrusion. The discreteness of
the system thus sets the nucleation condition,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:nucleation-condition}
    \Delta E'(A)\big|_{A=\Acell} = 0,
\end{equation}
or $A^*=\Acell$. Solved for the pressure, it gives the critical pressure
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pcrit}
    \pcrit = (\Ws-\Wc) + \sqrt{\frac{\pi h^2}{\Acell}}\,(\Wc+\gammaw).
\end{equation}
So layers are formed only if the pressure in the monolayer exceeds $\pcrit$. Alternaticvely, the probability of layer formation at a point $\r$ is 
(\cref{fig:main-2}(c))
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:p-layer-def}
 \calP(\r) \propto \mathbb P\bigl(p(\r)>\pcrit\bigr).
\end{equation}
With the parameter values of \cref{app:parameters-extrusion}, $\pcrit=35~\kPa\cdot\micron$. The surface tension of water dominates it. With $\gammaw=65~\kPa\cdot\micron$, close to the clean air-water value, the second term of \cref{eq:pcrit} contributes $33~\kPa\cdot\micron$. The mean pressure in the monolayer is far below $\pcrit$, so layering is predominantly from the large and rare fluctuations.










\Cref{eq:pcrit} contains the cell size and the adhesion and surface energies. It
contains no property of the fluctuations and does not depend on position. A defect can
therefore favor layers only by changing the distribution of the pressure and its fluctuations around it, which
makes the nucleation heterogeneous. 

In the following sections we set up a microscopic model of the monolayer and calculate the pressure fluctuations around a defect. Then, in \cref{sec:nucleation-2}, when all the components are in place, we compute the spatial profile of the layering probability \eqref{eq:p-layer-def}.

\subsection{The microscopic model}
\label{sec:model-micro}
% dean.tex 109-173 ("Microscopic equations of motion").
We start from Langevin equations for $N$ overdamped, self-propelled rods
\cite{cugliandolo2015,bertin2013}, each with position $\r_i$ and orientation $\theta_i$:
\begin{subequations}
  \label{eq:model}
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:langevin-position}
    \frac{\d\r_i}{\d t} &= \frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F_i + v_0\,\p(\theta_i) k_i,
    \\
    \label{eq:langevin-angle}
    \frac{\d\theta_i}{\d t} &= \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma_i + \lambda_i,
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
with $\p(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ the unit vector along the rod axis. Here $\xi_0/\rho_0$ and $\xir/\rho_0$ are
translational and rotational friction coefficients ($\rho_0$ a reference density
carried along for later convenience), and $\lambda_i$ is Gaussian white
noise, $\la\lambda_i(t)\lambda_j(t')\ra=2\Lambda\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t')$, with $\Lambda$
the rotational diffusion coefficient. The rods
interact through a pairwise potential $V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')$, so that
$V_\mathrm{tot}=\tfrac12\sum_{i,j=1}^N V(\r_i-\r_j,\theta_i,\theta_j)$. The force and
torque on rod $i$ are the values at $(\r_i,\theta_i)$ of the fields
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:force-torque}
    \F(\r,\theta) &= -\pd{}{\r}\sum_{j=1}^N V(\r-\r_j,\theta,\theta_j),
    \nonumber\\
    \Gamma(\r,\theta) &= -\pd{}{\theta}\sum_{j=1}^N V(\r-\r_j,\theta,\theta_j),
\end{align}
The sums run over all $N$ rods, including $j=i$: a pair
potential between identical rods satisfies
$V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')=V(\r'-\r,\theta',\theta)$, and at $\r=\r'$ rotational
invariance leaves it a function of $\theta-\theta'$ alone. Both derivatives in
\cref{eq:force-torque} therefore vanish on the $j=i$ term.

The second term of \cref{eq:langevin-position} is self-propulsion at speed $v_0$ along
the rod axis. Here $k_i$ is a dichotomous noise: $k_i=\pm1$ with
equal probability, switching at rate $\frev$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:k-dichotomous}
    \la k_i(t)k_j(t')\ra = \delta_{ij}\,\ee^{-2\frev|t-t'|}.
\end{equation}
This models the reversals of \textit{Myxococcus xanthus} gliding motility \citeTODO{}. On timescales long compared with $\frev^{-1}$ we approximate $k_i$ by Gaussian white noise,
$\la k_i(t)k_j(t')\ra \approx \frev^{-1}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t')$. The coarse-graining of
\cref{sec:model-coarse} uses It\^o calculus and therefore requires this approximation.
Refs.~\cite{bressloff2024global,spinney2025dean} derive
density equations for particles that switch between discrete states, as our rods do at
each reversal, without taking this limit.


Self-propulsion is a multiplicative noise. The noise is $k_i$, and the amplitude it
multiplies, $v_0\p(\theta_i)$, is set by the orientation of the rod. Coarse-graining a
multiplicative noise with It\^o calculus leaves a deterministic term alongside the
fluctuation, and both carry the same amplitude $v_0^2/\frev$
(\cref{app:dean-approach}). The anisotropic part of $\p\p$ becomes the active force
density of \cref{eq:model-rho}; the isotropic part is a diffusion, negligible against $B$. The fluctuation becomes the
density noise of the same equation.

\subsection{Coarse-graining that preserves fluctuations}
\label{sec:model-coarse}
% dean.tex 174-359 ("Dean's approach").
Dean's equation \cite{dean1996} is an \emph{exact} field-theoretic Langevin equation for
the density of interacting Langevin processes. Our model \eqref{eq:model} adds an
orientation, a self-propulsion and a reversal noise. The same derivation then gives an
exact equation for the joint density over the state space $(\r,\theta)$; the only
approximation is the Gaussian white-noise limit taken in \cref{sec:model-micro}.

Write the single-particle density $\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta) =
\delta(\r-\r_i(t))\,\delta(\theta-\theta_i(t))$, and let
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:joint-density-definition}
  \varrho_t(\r,\theta)=\rho_0^{-1}\sum_i\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta)
\end{align}
be the joint density
over $(\r,\theta)$, made dimensionless by $\rho_0$. The interaction is
pairwise, so the force and torque fields \cref{eq:force-torque} are gradients of a single
functional of $\varrho_t$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pair-free-energy}
    \calF[\varrho] = \frac{\rho_0^2}2\!\int\!\d\r\d\r'\,\d\theta\d\theta'\,
    V(\r\!-\!\r'\!,\theta,\theta')\,\varrho(\r,\theta)\,\varrho(\r'\!,\theta'),
\end{equation}
namely $\F=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\r\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$ and
$\Gamma=-\rho_0^{-1}\partial_\theta\,\delta\calF/\delta\varrho$. Applying It\^o's lemma
to $\varrho^{(i)}_t$ and summing over $i$ then gives a closed equation for $\varrho_t$
alone (\cref{app:dean-approach}),
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:final-langevin}
    \begin{aligned}
    \partial_t\varrho_t(\r,\theta) = {}&
    \frac1{\xi_0}\pd{}{\r}\!\cdot\!\left(\varrho_t\pd{}{\r}\frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho}\right)
    + \frac1{\xir}\pd{}{\theta}\!\left(\varrho_t\pd{}{\theta}\frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho}\right)
    \\
    &+ \frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0}\,p_\alpha(\theta)p_\beta(\theta)\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
    + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{\theta^2}
    \\
    &- \pd{}{\r}\!\cdot\!\left(\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p(\theta)\,k(\r,\theta,t)\right)
    \\
    &- \pd{}{\theta}\!\left(\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda(\r,\theta,t)\right).
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The last line of \cref{eq:final-langevin} carries two noise fields, $k(\r,\theta,t)$ and
$\lambda(\r,\theta,t)$. Both are Gaussian white noises in position, orientation and
time,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:noise-correlators}
    \la k(\r,\theta,t)\,k(\r',\theta',t')\ra &= \frac{v_0^2}{\frev\rho_0}\;\Delta,
    \nonumber\\
    \la \lambda(\r,\theta,t)\,\lambda(\r',\theta',t')\ra &= \frac{2\Lambda}{\rho_0}\;\Delta,
\end{align}
with $\Delta\equiv\delta(t-t')\delta(\r-\r')\delta(\theta-\theta')$, and they are
uncorrelated with each other. Their amplitudes are inherited from the reversal noise
$k_i$ and the rotational noise $\lambda_i$ of \cref{eq:model}.
\Cref{app:dean-approach} derives that relation, together with the multiplicative factor
$\sqrt{\varrho_t}$ that accompanies both fields.


\Cref{eq:final-langevin} is nonlinear even without interactions. The noise is
multiplicative: where there are no rods, $\varrho_t$ cannot fluctuate. The product
$\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,k$ is an It\^o product, because \cref{eq:final-langevin} follows from
It\^o's lemma: $\varrho_t$ is evaluated before each noise increment \cite{dean1996}. Each
realization of $k$ and $\lambda$ gives one random history of $\varrho_t$. The statistics
of the density, and of the pressure in \cref{sec:nucleation-1}, therefore follow from
\cref{eq:final-langevin} directly.

The joint density $\varrho_t$ is a sum of delta functions, the empirical density.
Strictly speaking, \cref{eq:final-langevin} is derived only for distributions, and
rigorous results for it are few
\cite{konarovskyi2019dean,konarovskyi2020dean,cornalba2023dean}. However, it becomes
well-posed if the noise is correlated in space \cite{fehrman2024well}, or if each delta
function is replaced by a kernel of finite width \cite{cornalba2019regularized}. Many
applications still use Dean's equation for smooth densities without a rigorous proof
\cite{kruger2017gaussian,demery2016conductivity,dean2014relaxation,demery2014generalized},
including the fluctuating theories of active particles
\cite{tailleur2008statistical,solon2015active,bertin2013}. From
\cref{sec:model-closure} on we treat $\varrho_t$ as smooth.


\subsection{Free energy, closure, and the stochastic field equations}
\label{sec:model-closure}
The joint density $\varrho_t$ carries far more information than we need. The rest of the
paper works with its lowest angular moments, the fluctuating density and nematic order
parameter,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:density-definition}
    \rho(\r,t) = \int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta),
    \quad
    \Q(\r,t) = \int\!\d\theta\,\varrho_t(\r,\theta)\,\hat\Q(\theta),
\end{align}
with $\hat\Q(\theta)=2\p(\theta)\p(\theta)-\I$ the single-rod nematic tensor.%
\footnote{%
The conventional nematic order parameter is normalized by the local density,
$\Q=\rho^{-1}\!\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\hat\Q$, and is bounded by unity. We use the
unnormalized moment \cref{eq:density-definition} instead; the two coincide in the
incompressible limit $\rho\to1$ taken throughout (\cref{app:dean-normalisation}).}
In this subsection we reduce \cref{eq:final-langevin} to a closed pair of stochastic
equations for these two fields.

We do not know the interbacterial potential
${V(\r-\r',\theta,\theta')}$ in any detail, so we do not attempt to evaluate
\cref{eq:pair-free-energy} from it. Instead we assume that the free energy depends on
$\varrho$ only through the two moments \cref{eq:density-definition},
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:free-energy-parametrisation}
    \calF[\varrho] = F[\rho,\Q] = \int\!\d\r\;\mathfrak f\bigl(\rho(\r),\Q(\r),\partial_\r\Q(\r)\bigr),
\end{align}
which is both a physical assumption and a choice of parametrization. It gives a chain
rule,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:chain-rule}
    \frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)}
    = \frac{\delta F}{\delta\rho(\r)}
    + \frac{\delta F}{\delta\Q(\r)}\!:\!\hat\Q(\theta).
\end{align}
This truncation of the $\theta$ dependence to a single harmonic is what makes
\cref{eq:final-langevin} integrable over $\theta$ (\cref{app:dean-angle}).

For the free energy density $\mathfrak f$ we take the sum of an incompressibility penalty and a Landau-de Gennes
nematic term,
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:free-energy-choice}
    \mathfrak f(\rho,\Q,\partial_\r\Q) = {}& \frac B2(\rho-1)^2 + \frac a2(\Q\!:\!\Q)
    + \frac b4(\Q\!:\!\Q)^2
    \nonumber\\
    &+ \frac K2(\partial_\r\Q)\!:\!(\partial_\r\Q).
\end{align}
The first term enforces near-incompressibility of the monolayer with a large bulk
modulus $B$; the rest is the standard nematic free energy, with $a<0$ favoring order and $K$ penalizing distortion. The single $K$ is the one-constant approximation of the Frank free energy, in which the splay and bend moduli of the two-dimensional nematic are taken equal \citeTODO{}.

The first term also sets the pressure of the monolayer. From
$p=\rho\,\partial_\rho\mathfrak f-\mathfrak f$,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:pressure-definition}
  p=\frac B2\left(\rho^2-1\right)\simeq B(\rho-1),
\end{align}
where the last form is the leading order in $\rho-1$. 
This is the two-dimensional pressure $p$ of \cref{eq:dE-of-A}.

Integrating \cref{eq:final-langevin} over $\theta$ and keeping the leading terms in
$1/B$ (\cref{app:dean-angle}) gives a continuity equation for the density,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:model-rho}
  \partial_t\rho = -\nabla\!\cdot\!\Bigl[
    \frac1{\xi_0}\bigl(-B\nabla\rho + \Q\!:\!\nabla\H + \fa\bigr)
    + \bEta_{\rho}(\r,t)\Bigr].
\end{align}
The first two terms in the bracket are passive, inherited from the pair potential through
the free energy. The term $-B\nabla\rho$ resists compression, and $\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$
carries cells along gradients of the nematic force field
$\H(\r)=-\delta F/\delta\Q= -a\Q - b(\Q\!:\!\Q)\Q + K\nabla^2\Q$ \citeTODO{}. The third term is the active force density
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:active-force}
  f^a_\alpha(\r) = -\zeta\,\partial_\beta Q_\ab(\r)
  \quad\text{with}\quad
  \zeta = \frac{\xi_0v_0^2}{4\frev},
\end{align}
the divergence of the active nematic stress $-\zeta\Q$. Positive $\zeta$ denotes extensile active stress.
The coefficient comes entirely from self-propulsion and reversal, so fast-reversing colonies are less active.

The last term is the noise of the density flux,
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-def}
  \bEta_{\rho}(\r,t) = \int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t(\r,\theta)}\,
  \p(\theta)\,k(\r,\theta,t).
\end{equation}
Its autocorrelation follows from
\cref{eq:noise-correlators},
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation}
  \la \eta^{\rho}_\alpha(\r,t) \,\eta^{\rho}_\beta(\r',t') \ra
  \\
  =
  \frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \left[Q_\ab(\r)+\rho(\r) \delta_\ab\right]
 \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t') ,
\end{multline}
an isotropic piece proportional to $\rho$ and an anisotropic piece proportional to $\Q$.
Both scale with $\zeta$ of \cref{eq:active-force}, so the noise strength is set by the
same reversal rate $\frev$ and self-propulsion speed $v_0$ as the active force. The
rotational noise does not appear: every term it produces is a $\theta$ derivative, and
$\int\!\d\theta\,\partial_\theta(\cdot)=0$. The noise $\bEta_{\rho}$ sits inside the
divergence, so $\int\!\d\r\,\rho$ is constant in every realization of the noise, not only
in the mean.

\Cref{eq:model-rho} is a continuity equation,
$\partial_t\rho=-\nabla\!\cdot\!\bs J$. If we write $\bs J = \rho \v$, where $\v(\r,t)$
is the velocity field, and set $\rho\approx1$ in the incompressible limit, we get a force balance
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:velocity}
  \xi_0 \v = -B\nabla\rho + \Q\!:\!\nabla\H + \fa + \xi_0 \bEta_{\rho},
\end{align}
where the friction force density $\xi_0 \v$ on the cell is balanced by the passive interactions, the active nematic force and the noise. By \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, $B\nabla\rho\simeq\nabla p$ is the pressure gradient.
The elastic force $\Q\!:\!\nabla\H$ redistributes
cells to relieve orientational stress. Only $\fa$ does not derive from the free energy $F$. Since $\fa\propto\nabla\!\cdot\!\Q$, it vanishes in a
uniform texture and is largest where the texture bends fastest, at a defect core
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}).

We also need a dynamical equation for the nematic order parameter $\Q$. Multiplying
\cref{eq:final-langevin} by $\hat\Q(\theta)$ and integrating over $\theta$ does not close
the system: angular averages like $\la\hat\Q\hat\Q\ra_\varrho$ appear, and $\rho$ and $\Q$ do not determine them. We use
the lowest-order maximum-entropy closure,
$\langle\hat\Q\hat\Q\rangle_\varrho \simeq \rho\,\J$
with $\J$ the rank-four identity on symmetric traceless tensors. It is accurate to
relative $O(S^2)$ in the strength of the nematic order $S=\sqrt{(\Q\!:\!\Q)/2}$. \Cref{app:closure} derives the
higher-order terms and the limitations of this closure.

With the closure in place we obtain
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:model-Q}
  \partial_t \Q = {}& -\nabla\!\cdot\!\Bigl[\frac1{\xi_0}
    \bigl(-\Q B \nabla\rho + \nabla \H\bigr) + \bEta^\Q\Bigr]
  \nonumber\\
  &+ \frac4{\xir} \Ha + \f^Q.
\end{align}
The first two terms inside the divergence are the Onsager conjugates of the passive
terms of \cref{eq:model-rho}. $\Ha$ is the
nematic force field with coefficients renormalized by the rotational noise and the activity,
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:H-active}
  \Ha &= -a'\Q - b(\Q\!:\!\Q)\Q + K'\nabla^2\Q,
  \nonumber\\
  a' &= a+\Lambda\xir, \qquad K' = K+{\zeta\xir}/({4\xi_0}).
\end{align}
It is $a'$ and $K'$, not the bare $a$ and $K$, that set the defect profile solved for in
\cref{sec:defect-steady}.

The relaxational and the transport terms of \cref{eq:model-Q} do different jobs. The
relaxational term $\tfrac4{\xir}\Ha$ carries no flux divergence. It relaxes $\Q$ toward a
minimizer of the effective free energy and fixes the shape of the defect. The transport
terms leave that shape almost unchanged, but through $\Q B\nabla\rho$ they couple the
texture to the density field and translate the
defect. 

The two noises of \cref{eq:model-Q} enter differently. $\bEta^\Q$ is a flux, the
rank-three tensor $\eta^Q_{\ab i}=\int\!\d\theta\,\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\hat
Q_\ab(\theta)p_i(\theta)k(\r,\theta,t)$. Both $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$ carry the same
reversal noise $k(\r,\theta,t)$, so the two are correlated. The
second noise $\f^Q$ is local and non-conservative. It carries the rotational noise
$\lambda(\r,\theta,t)$ alone and is uncorrelated with both.
\Cref{app:dean-angle} gives their correlators.

In the rest of the paper we drop the terms proportional to $\nabla\H$ from
\cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q}. They introduce fourth-order spatial derivatives of $\Q$,
which we cannot treat either analytically or numerically. The two terms must
go together, because they are Onsager conjugates: dropping one alone would break
reciprocity. They are also the smaller of the two channels by which orientational order
relaxes. Rods can turn in place, at a rate set by the rotational friction $\xir$, or rods
of different orientation can be exchanged between neighboring regions, at a rate set by
the translational friction $\xi_0$. The second channel is a flux divergence and costs two
extra powers of wavevector, so the ratio of the two rates is $(k\ell_\star)^2/4$ with
$\ell_\star=\sqrt{\xir/\xi_0}=0.14~\micron$ (\cref{app:parameters-choice}). At the defect
core this suppresses the discarded channel by
$(\ell_\star/\elld)^2/4\simeq5\times10^{-3}$.

\Cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} are the fluctuating active nematic equations this paper
solves. Activity enters them in three places: the force $\fa$,
the amplitude of the reversal noises $\bEta_\rho$ and $\bEta^\Q$, and the renormalization of the nematic force field $\Ha$ in \cref{eq:H-active}. The
density and the nematic field are coupled in both directions, through
$\fa\propto\nabla\!\cdot\!\Q$ in one equation and through $\Q B\nabla\rho$ in the other.
That coupling sets a $+1/2$ defect in motion in \cref{sec:defect-steady}.

Everything that follows comes from solving \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} at a $+1/2$
defect, with the parameters collected in \cref{app:parameters}.



The closure truncates a hierarchy of moments over $\theta$ within one realization of the noise.
Continuum theories are usually built by truncating a different hierarchy. Averaging
Dean's equation over the noise gives an equation for the mean density that contains the
pair correlation. The equation for the pair correlation contains the three-point
correlation, and so on \cite{dean1996}. Dynamical density functional theory closes this
hierarchy with an equilibrium free energy \cite{marconi1999dynamic,tevrugt2020classical}.
Kinetic theories of self-propelled rods close the analogous hierarchy for the one-rod
distribution \cite{baskaran2008hydrodynamics}. Both give deterministic equations for the
ensemble-averaged density, because the fluctuations are averaged out before the closure.
A noise term belongs only in the equation for a density coarse-grained in space or time
\cite{archer2004dynamical}. \Cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} are equations for moments of
one realization, so they keep the noise of \cref{eq:final-langevin}.

\section{Fluctuations around a $+1/2$ defect}
\label{sec:defect}
% structure.md restructure, 2026-09-21. dean.tex 681-1266.
% ============================================================
Solving \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} at a $+1/2$ defect takes three steps: find the
 steady state of the noiseless equations
(\cref{sec:defect-steady}); linearize the stochastic equations around it; and solve the
resulting linear field theory for the equal-time covariance of the fluctuations
(\cref{sec:defect-fluct}). \Cref{app:parameters} collects the parameters.

We solve \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} numerically in
\cref{app:comoving}, and their linearization in \cref{app:lyapunov}. The main text gives analytical solutions of simplified
equations, which agree well with the numerics. The analytical solutions also show how the
result depends on the parameters of the model; the numerical solutions do not.

\subsection{The steady state}
\label{sec:defect-steady}
% Pages ~1.5. dean.tex 705-747, 819-894, 2460-2597 (solver moved to appendix, results summarised here).

We first solve a simplified system. The $+1/2$ defect moves because the density and the
nematic order are cross-coupled. Dropping the cross-coupling term in the $\Q$ equation
leaves a static problem:
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:simplified-noiseless}
  0 = \nabla\cdot\left[B\nabla\rho - \fa\right] , \qquad \Ha = 0.
\end{align}
The solution of the second equation is given by a Pad\'{e} approximation \citeTODO{}:
\begin{align}
  \Q(r,\phi) = S(r)\hat\Q(\phi/2)
  \qquad
  S(r) = S_0 f_\pade(r/\elld)
  \nonumber\\
  \label{eq:pade-approximation}
  f_\pade(x) = x \sqrt{\frac{0.34 + 0.07 x^2}{1 + 0.41 x^2+0.07 x^4}}.
\end{align}
Two scales appear. The far-field nematic order $S_0=\sqrt{-a'/(2b)}$ is the value $S(r)$
saturates to, and the core size $\elld=\sqrt{K'/(-a')}$ is the distance over which $S$
recovers from $S(0)=0$ at the defect. Both are built from the renormalized $a'$
and $K'$ of \cref{eq:H-active}, not from the bare coefficients of the free energy
density \eqref{eq:free-energy-choice}. \Cref{app:parameters-choice} gives their values
\cite{copenhagen2021,han2025local} and \cref{app:comoving} the
accuracy of the approximant.

Now, the first equation in \cref{eq:simplified-noiseless} is a Poisson equation for the density
with an exact solution:
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-analytic}
  \rho(r,\phi) = 1 - \frac{\zeta}{B}\left[ S(r) - \frac{r}{R}S(R)\right]\cos \phi
\end{align}
where $(r,\phi)$ are the polar coordinates and we impose $\rho(R,\phi) = 1$ on the boundary $r=R$.
The density therefore departs from uniformity only at order $\zeta/B$.

Restoring the cross-coupling sets the defect in motion. It moves
\emph{opposite} to the active force. The active force does not push the
defect; it pushes the cells. The cells build a density gradient until the diffusive flux
cancels the active one, $B\nabla\rho\simeq\fa$. The nematic order parameter, however,
couples to the density gradient and not to the active force. The nematic flux in
\cref{eq:model-Q} is $\bs J_\Q = -\Q B\nabla\rho/\xi_0 \simeq -\Q\fa/\xi_0$, so $\Q$ is
transported with velocity $-B\nabla\rho/\xi_0$, down the density gradient and therefore
against $\fa$.

For the $+1/2$ defect \cref{eq:pade-approximation} gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:active-force-defect}
  f^\text{a}_x(r, \phi) = -\zeta \left(S'(r) +S(r)/r\right),
  \qquad f^\text{a}_y = 0,
\end{align}
a vector field along the defect axis with no transverse component. It points along $-x$,
the side on which the director bends around the core (\cref{fig:main-2}(a)), so the
defect drifts along $+x$ (\cref{app:comoving}).

A drifting defect has no steady state in the laboratory frame.
\Cref{app:comoving} therefore solves the noiseless \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q}
numerically in a frame comoving with the core, with neither simplification made above. \Cref{fig:rho1d-dirichlet} shows the resulting density along the
defect axis against \cref{eq:rho-analytic}, and \cref{fig:rho2d} the
two-dimensional field.

The cross-coupling also deforms the defect itself, but only slightly. The director
rotates by about one degree out to $r=5~\micron$; the nematic order and the active force
depart as little (\cref{fig:strength,fig:defectangle,fig:activeforce}).

The closed form and the numerics are separate calculations. The closed form fixes $\Q$ to the Pad\'e profile
\cref{eq:pade-approximation} and solves the density equation exactly; the numerics solve
both fields together and let $\Q$ depart from that profile. They give the same density:
enhanced behind the defect, depleted ahead of it, amplitude $O(\zeta/B)$. That checks the
solver and the simplification at once. They differ in one respect.
\Cref{eq:rho-analytic} is exactly antisymmetric under $x\to-x$ and the numerical solution
is not, and that asymmetry is the signature of the drift.

The density profile matters for consistency rather than for the final result. It
enters only the \emph{mean} pressure, which \cref{sec:nucleation-2} shows to be far
below the fluctuations.


\subsection{Linearization and the fluctuation spectrum}
\label{sec:defect-fluct}
% Pages ~2.5. dean.tex 748-818, 895-1266.
Let $\delta\rho(\r,t)=\rho(\r,t)-\rho_\ss(\r)$ and $\delta\Q(\r,t)=\Q(\r,t)-\Q_\ss(\r)$.
Linearizing the comoving-frame form of \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} around the
steady state of \cref{sec:defect-steady} gives
(\cref{app:dean-angle})
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:linearized}
\begin{align}
    &\partial_t\delta\rho =-\nabla\!\cdot\!\Bigl[
    \frac1{\xi_0}\bigl(-B\nabla\delta\rho -\zeta\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\delta\Q \bigr) + \bEta_\rho(\r,t)\Bigr],
    \label{eq:linearized-rho}
    \\
    &\partial_t\delta\Q = \frac B{\xi_0}\nabla\!\cdot\!(\Q_\ss\nabla\delta\rho)
    + \nabla\!\cdot\!(\delta\Q\,\bs w_\ss) + \frac4{\xir}\delta\Ha
    \nonumber\\
    &\qquad\qquad
    {}- \nabla\!\cdot\!\bEta^\Q(\r,t)+ \f^Q(\r,t),
    \label{eq:linearized-Q}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
with $\u$ the velocity of the comoving frame (\cref{app:comoving}), $\bs w_\ss\equiv\u+(B/\xi_0)\nabla\rho_\ss$,
so that $\delta\Q$ is transported with velocity $-\bs w_\ss$, and
$\delta\Ha$ the linearization of \cref{eq:H-active} at $\Q_\ss$. The frame term is
kept only in the $\Q$ equation, through $\bs w_\ss$; in \cref{eq:linearized-rho} it is
smaller than the retained $B\nabla^2\delta\rho$ by $10^{-4}$ (\cref{app:dean-angle}).
The three noises
are those of \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} with their amplitudes evaluated on the steady
state, which is their leading order in the fluctuation. This is a linear
stochastic field theory on the background $(\rho_\ss,\Q_\ss)$. \Cref{app:lyapunov}
obtains the equal-time covariance of $\delta\rho$ and $\delta\Q$ numerically, by solving
a Lyapunov equation.

The main text presents the analytically tractable case, in which only the density
fluctuates and $\delta\Q=0$. 
This is the appropriate limit when the $\zeta\,\nabla\!\cdot\!\delta\Q$ term in
\cref{eq:linearized-rho} is small compared with the noise $\bEta_\rho$. The standard deviation of the traction measured in Ref.~\cite{han2025local} is about
ten times its mean, which is consistent with the reversal noise dominating.

\Cref{eq:linearized-Q} then drops out, and both of its
noises with it: only $\bEta_\rho$ survives.
The steady density departs from unity only at order $\zeta/B$ (\cref{eq:rho-analytic}), so we use a flat density profile in the noise autocorrelation:
\begin{multline}
  \label{eq:eta-rho-ss}
  \la \eta^{\rho}_\alpha(\r,t) \,\eta^{\rho}_\beta(\r',t') \ra
  \\
  =\frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}
  \left[Q^\ss_\ab(\r)+\delta_\ab\right]
 \delta(\r-\r')\delta(t-t').
\end{multline}

The defect then enters \cref{eq:linearized-rho} in exactly one place, through the
$\Q_\ss$ term in \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss}. The defect reaches $\delta\rho$
through the noise amplitude alone, not through the relaxation rate.

Taking the Fourier transform of \cref{eq:linearized-rho} over space and time gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-fourier}
  \delta\tilde\rho(\k,\omega) =
  -\frac{\ii\,\k\cdot\tilde \bEta_\rho(\k,\omega)}{\ii\omega+\gamma_0 k^2},
  \qquad \gamma_0 \equiv B/\xi_0,
\end{align}
with $\k$ the wavevector and $k=|\k|$. The noise is conservative, so its power carries a
factor $k^2$ that cancels the $k^2$ of the relaxation rate $\gamma_0k^2$. The spectrum is
then flat in $\k$, and the equal-time variance
$\la\delta\rho(\r,t)\delta\rho(\r',t)\ra_\ss$ diverges as $\r'\to\r$.

The divergence comes from treating the monolayer as a continuum down to zero length. However, there is a physical cutoff at the width of a cell. Thus we introduce a short length scale cutoff
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cutoff}
    \elln = 0.5~\micron.
\end{equation}
We implement this by suppressing wavevectors above $1/\elln$ with a Gaussian filter, multiplying \cref{eq:rho-fourier} by $\ee^{-k^2\elln^2/4}$.
Everything below carries that factor.

Consider first the isotropic case $\Q_\ss=0$. Inverting the smoothed
\cref{eq:rho-fourier} over space and time gives
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic}
  \la\delta\rho(\r,t)\delta\rho(\r',t')\ra =
  \frac{\zeta}{2\pi B\rho_0 \elln^2}
  \frac{
      \exp\!\!\left(\!-\frac{|\r-\r'|^2}{2 \elln^2+4 \gamma_0 |t-t'|}\right)
      }{1 + 2 \gamma_0 |t-t'| / \elln^2}
\end{align}
so that the equal-point, equal-time variance is the constant pedestal
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:pedestal}
  \sigmarho(\r) = \sigmarho\big|_\text{pedestal}
  = \frac{\zeta}{2\pi B\rho_0\elln^2}.
\end{align}
The fluctuation is much larger than the deterministic modulation. \Cref{eq:pedestal} gives the standard deviation of the density,
$\sigma_\rho \sim B^{-1/2}$, while the scale of the modulation of the average density in \cref{eq:rho-analytic} is $B^{-1}$. In the large-$B$ limit the average density profile is overwhelmed by the fluctuations.

\Cref{eq:rho-autocorrelation-isotropic} also gives the two scales over which a density
fluctuation stays coherent: a correlation length equal to the cutoff $\elln$ and a correlation time
\begin{align}
 \label{eq:tau-c}
 \tau_c = \frac{\elln^2}{2 \gamma_0},
\end{align}
the time a fluctuation of size $\elln$ takes to diffuse away. Neither scale involves the
defect, and we assume the anisotropy does not change them much. \Cref{sec:nucleation-2}
uses $\tau_c$ to turn the layering probability into a rate, where it cancels from the ratio.

Restoring the anisotropic piece of \cref{eq:eta-rho-ss}, the one that
carries $\Q_\ss$, gives the total variance (\cref{app:variance})
\begin{align}
  \label{eq:total-gauss}
  \sigmarho(\r) = \sigmarho\big|_\text{pedestal}
  \left(1 + \frac{\elln^2}{4}\bigl(\kappa\star\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}\bigr)(\r)\right),
\end{align}
a convolution of the double divergence of the steady nematic texture against the
isotropic real-space kernel
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:kappa-real}
  \kappa(w) = \frac{1-\ee^{-2w^2/\elln^2}}{2\pi\,w^2}.
\end{equation}
\Cref{eq:total-gauss} is the central result of \cref{sec:defect}.
The defect modulates the variance around a uniform pedestal. The pedestal is the
variance of a uniform monolayer, fixed by the noise amplitude $\zeta$, the stiffness $B$
and the resolution $\elln$.
\Cref{fig:variance} shows sections of \cref{eq:total-gauss}.
Along the defect axis the
modulation is odd in $x$ and vanishes at the core, because
$\partial_i\partial_jQ^\ss_{ij}=\partial_x[S'(r)+S(r)/r]$ is odd. The variance is raised
on the $-x$ side, where it peaks about $1.5~\micron$ from the core at
$\nu_0\equiv[\sigmarho(\r_\mathrm{max})/\sigmarho(\infty)]-1=3.1\%$, and lowered by as much on the $+x$ side. Along the $y$ axis the
simplified model predicts no modulation at all.
The scale of the modulation $\nu_0$ decreases with decreasing cutoff $\elln$ as shown in \cref{fig:ln_curve} and vanishes at $\elln=0$.

In \cref{fig:variance} we also show the density variance from the full numerical solution of \cref{eq:linearized}, retaining $\delta\Q\ne0$ (see \cref{app:lyapunov} for details). The simplified model captures its main features. \Cref{fig:variance3d} shows the two-dimensional profile of the density variance.

Three percent is the entire position\nobreakdash-dependent signal in the density variance profile. In \cref{sec:nucleation-2} we show that it modulates the layering probability by two orders of magnitude.

  \centering

\section{Layer formation probability from the pressure fluctuations}
\label{sec:nucleation-2}
% The calculation motivated in \cref{sec:nucleation-1}, now that \cref{sec:defect}
% supplies the variance. Pressure statistics from the former sec:nucleation-theory,
% comparison with experiment from the former sec:nucleation-results
% (dean.tex 1392-1487). Former fig:match is panel (a) of fig:main-1.

By \cref{eq:pressure-definition}, a density fluctuation $\delta\rho$ changes the
pressure by $B\,\delta\rho$. Since $\delta\rho$ is Gaussian with the variance of
\cref{eq:total-gauss}, $p$ is Gaussian too, with
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sigma-p}
    \sigma_p^2(\r) = B^2\sigmarho(\r),
    \qquad
    \sigma_{p,\infty}^2 = \frac{B\zeta}{2\pi\rho_0\elln^2},
\end{equation}
the second expression its far-field value, from the isotropic pedestal of
\cref{eq:total-gauss}.
As we argued for the average density, in the large-$B$ limit the mean pressure is $\la p(\r) \ra \ll \sigma_p(\r)$. We therefore set it to zero.
The probability in \cref{eq:p-layer-def} is then the Gaussian
tail
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:p-layer}
    \calP(\r) &\propto \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\,z(\r)}\ee^{-z(\r)^2/2},
    \qquad
    z(\r) &= \frac{\pcrit}{\sigma_p(\r)}.
\end{align}

The comparison with experiment is not to an absolute rate but to a \emph{ratio}:
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} reports the distribution of distances between defects and new
layers, normalized by the same distribution for random points in the monolayer. The attempt rate is $\calP/\tau_c$, and $\tau_c$ of \cref{eq:tau-c} is
essentially position-independent, so that ratio equals the ratio of layering
probabilities up to a slowly varying prefactor. \Cref{eq:p-layer} then gives
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:R-ratio}
    \frac{\calP(\r)}{\calP(\infty)} \simeq
    \exp\!\left[\frac{z_\infty^2}2\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{p,\infty}^2}{\sigma_p^2(\r)}\right)\right],
    \qquad z_\infty \equiv \frac{\pcrit}{\sigma_{p,\infty}}.
\end{equation}
Only the \emph{relative} modulation of the variance matters, and the large prefactor
$z_\infty^2/2$ amplifies it. A modulation of a few percent therefore produces an
enhancement of two orders of magnitude.


\Cref{fig:main-1}(a) shows the layering probability averaged over a circle of radius $r$
about a centre $\r_\mathrm c$,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:p-layer-radial}
    \calP(r) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \oint\limits_{|\r-\r_\mathrm c|=r}\!\d\phi\,\calP(\r),
\end{equation}
relative to its far-field value. Which centre matches the experiment is open.

The
histogram bins the distance from the defect core, but layering is most probable at the
maximum of the pressure variance. The two solid curves correspond to these two choices:
the core, $\r_\mathrm c=0$, and the point of maximum probability,

With $B=2\times10^5~\Pa\cdot\micron$, $\elln=0.5~\micron$ and the parameters of
\cref{app:parameters}, $\sigma_{p,\infty}=1.8\times10^3~\Pa\cdot\micron$ and
$z_\infty=19.1$. The variance modulation of \cref{fig:variance} peaks at $\nu_0=3.1\%$,
about $1.5~\micron$ off the core, and \cref{eq:R-ratio} then gives the peak enhancement
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:R-value}
    \frac{\calP(\r_\mathrm{max})}{\calP(\infty)}
    = \exp\!\left[\frac{z_\infty^2}2\,\frac{\nu_0}{1+\nu_0}\right] \simeq 2.3\times10^2 .
\end{equation}
The curves of \cref{fig:main-1}(a) use the variance of the numerical solution of
\cref{eq:linearized} instead. It peaks at $\nu_0\simeq2.6\%$ and gives about $10^2$.
Both values lie within the error bar of the first bin of Fig.~3f of
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}, which spans $40$--$500$ around $2\times10^2$.

The match is a one-parameter fit. Every quantity in \cref{eq:R-value} is fixed in advance
except the bulk modulus $B$, the stiffness of the incompressibility penalty in
\cref{eq:free-energy-choice}. $B$ is a model parameter rather than a measured constant.
The \emph{shape} of the variance profile in \cref{fig:variance} is not fitted.
\Cref{eq:total-gauss} fixes it once $\Q_\ss$ is known, with no adjustable parameter.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% Pages ~1.5. New. structure.md \S4, four closing moves.
% ============================================================
The fit in \cref{sec:nucleation-2} fixes only the combination
$\pcrit^2\elln^2\nu_0(\elln)/(B\zeta)$. It therefore does not test the nucleation
condition \cref{eq:nucleation-condition}: a different $\pcrit$ would only shift the
fitted $B$. The stricter condition $\Delta E(\Acell)=0$, for example, doubles $\pcrit$,
raises the fitted $B$ about fourfold, and leaves the predicted profile unchanged.

The coarse-graining fixes the tensor structure of the noise, $\Q_\ss+\I$ in
\cref{eq:eta-rho-ss}. A noise added at the continuum level would leave it free. This
structure sets the shape of the variance profile, and the comparison tests that shape.
The peak $\nu_0$ and the noise amplitude enter only through the fitted combination, so
the comparison does not test them.

The reversal rate enters through $\zeta\propto1/\frev$ (\cref{eq:active-force}) and
shifts $\nu_0$ slightly through $K'$ (\cref{eq:H-active}). At fixed $B$ and $\elln$, the
logarithm of the enhancement \cref{eq:R-value} is then nearly proportional to $\frev$.
Slower reversals make layers more frequent everywhere, but less concentrated at the
defect. Reversal-frequency mutants of the \textit{frz} pathway can test this.
Ref.~\cite{han2025local} reports that non-reversing cells show larger traction
fluctuations and more layer formation. This agrees in sign, but non-reversing cells lie
outside the model, since $\zeta$ diverges as $\frev\to0$.
\todo{confirm the non-reversing (frzE) traction and layering observation is in
Ref.~\cite{han2025local} and not in a companion paper before this sentence stands.}

Two inputs of the combination, $B$ and $\elln$, are model parameters rather than
measured constants. We set $\elln$ to the width of a cell and fit $B$. No measurement
exists of the compressibility of a bacterial monolayer at this scale. The velocity fit
of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} constrains only the product of $B$ with an undetermined cell
flux, so it does not fix $B$ either. The fitted $B=2\times10^5~\Pa\cdot\micron$ lies near
the effective cell modulus fitted to the planar-to-bulk transition of growing
\textit{E.\ coli} colonies \cite{grant2014}. We do not count this as support. That
transition is driven by growth, whereas \textit{M.\ xanthus} layers form by migration
\cite{copenhagen2021}. The fitted $B$ also moves with $\elln$, roughly as $\elln^{3.3}$
(\cref{eq:R-value,fig:ln_curve}).

The energetics idealize the nucleus. The patch is taken as circular, although one cell is
a rod with about twice the perimeter of a disc of the same area.
\Cref{eq:dE-of-A} needs the pressure averaged over the patch, while $p(\r)$ is
resolved at $\elln$. Averaged over $\Acell$, its variance is three times smaller. These
choices and the unmeasured energies of \cref{app:parameters-extrusion} all move $\pcrit$ or $\sigma_p$, and so the fitted $B$.
The criterion \cref{eq:nucleation-condition} does not say how the first cell leaves. At
$p=\pcrit$, one cell still faces $\Delta E(\Acell)\simeq10^5~\Pa\cdot\micron^3$
(\cref{fig:main-2}(c)), about $2\times10^7\,\kB T$. The single-cell perturbations of
\cref{sec:nucleation-1}, which \cref{eq:dE-of-A} does not describe, must carry the
cell over this barrier.

We drop both $\nabla\H$ terms of \cref{eq:model-rho,eq:model-Q} (\cref{sec:model-closure}).
The analytic calculation of \cref{sec:defect-fluct} sets $\delta\Q=0$. We have no bound
on the resulting error and check the calculation against the coupled numerical solution.
The maximum-entropy closure is accurate to relative $O(S^2)$, and $S_0=0.9$ is not small.
The closure sets the relaxational term of \cref{eq:model-Q}, and so $\Q_\ss$. Its noise
covariance is positive semi-definite only for $S\le0.62$, and around the defect $S$
exceeds this bound over most of the domain. This constrains the numerical solver but not
the analytic result, which uses the exact density-noise correlator
\cref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation} (\cref{app:realizability,app:lyapunov}). The
Gaussian tail is used nineteen standard deviations out, where the monolayer is
compressed by $\pcrit/B\simeq18\%$. There the exact equation of state
\cref{eq:pressure-definition} lowers the predicted ratio from
$2.3\times10^2$ to $1.0\times10^2$ at the same $B$.

A $-1/2$ defect feels no net active force, so it does not drift. Its active force has
threefold symmetry, and so does the variance modulation that \cref{eq:total-gauss} gives
for it, with three lobes of raised variance. The model therefore predicts more layers
near $-1/2$ defects too. Fig.~3f of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} shows no layer enrichment
within $10~\micron$ of $-1/2$ defects, and Fig.~3g no hole enrichment near $+1/2$
defects. The variance carries no sign: where it rises, both tails of the pressure
distribution rise. The variance alone therefore cannot produce layers at $+1/2$ defects
and holes at $-1/2$ defects.

The variance field is also an observable. Ref.~\cite{han2025local} maps the standard
deviation of the traction around $\pm1/2$ defects and finds it larger at $+1/2$ than at
$-1/2$ defects. The traction is the divergence of the stress, not the pressure. A
comparison needs the traction variance at the resolution and frame time of the
measurement. We have not computed it. Its normalized profile would not depend on $B$ but
would depend on $\elln$.

\section{Elastic energy released by the lower layer}
\label{app:extrusion}
% dean.tex 1293-1337. Supports \cref{sec:nucleation-1} and \cref{eq:pressure-definition}.
The pressure term $-pA$ of \cref{eq:dE-of-A} is the elastic energy released by
the lower monolayer. A region compressed to density
$\rho$ above the relaxed value carries a bulk strain $\epsilon=\rho-1$ in the units of
\cref{eq:free-energy-choice} and stores energy $\tfrac B2\epsilon^2A_d$ over the
deformed area $A_d$, with $B$ the same two-dimensional bulk modulus that sets the
incompressibility penalty in the field theory. The patch removes
$\rho_0(1+\epsilon)A$ cells, and the lower layer spreads back over the whole of $A_d$
(\cref{fig:main-1}(b)), so its strain falls to $\epsilon_f=\epsilon-(1+\epsilon)A/A_d$.
The extruded cells are unconfined in the new layer and relax to zero strain. Hence
$\Delta E_e=\tfrac B2(\epsilon_f^2-\epsilon^2)A_d$, or
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dEe-full}
    \Delta E_e = \frac B2\left[-2\epsilon - 2\epsilon^2
    + (1+\epsilon)^2\frac A{A_d}\right]A,
\end{equation}
which to leading order in the small compression $\epsilon$ and the small area ratio
$A/A_d$ collapses to
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dEe}
    \Delta E_e \simeq -B\epsilon A = -pA,
\end{equation}
with $p$ the pressure \cref{eq:pressure-definition} at leading order in $\epsilon$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item At second order the release is not $pA$. As $A/A_d\to0$, \cref{eq:dEe-full}
    gives $-B\epsilon(1+\epsilon)A=-\rho\,\partial_\rho\mathfrak f\,A=-(p+\mathfrak f)A$.
    Besides the work $pA$, the extruded cells release their own strain energy
    $\mathfrak fA$. At $\epsilon=\pcrit/B\simeq0.18$ the release exceeds $pA$ by $8\%$.
  \item \prompt{I do not see a reason to put the threshold on this. So Do not write about this} The consequence for the threshold. Placing $\pcrit$ on
    $\rho\,\partial_\rho\mathfrak f$ instead of on $p$ lowers the predicted ratio at
    fixed $B$ from $2.3\times10^2$ to about $60$. The exact equation of state alone
    gives $1.0\times10^2$ (\cref{sec:discussion}).
  \item \prompt{skip this} The finite-area correction $(1+\epsilon)^2A/A_d$ of \cref{eq:dEe-full}, and its
    size for a patch of one cell.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The fluctuating active nematic equations}
\label{app:dean-angle}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The chain rule \cref{eq:chain-rule}. It rests on

    $\delta\Q(\r')/\delta\varrho(\r,\theta)=\hat\Q(\theta)\,\delta(\r-\r')$, the second

  \item The density equation. Integrate \cref{eq:final-langevin} over $\theta$ using
    $\int\!\d\theta\,\varrho\,p_\alpha p_\beta=\tfrac12(Q_\ab+\rho\delta_\ab)$. The
    self-propulsion term splits into the active force $-\zeta\nabla\!\cdot\!\Q$ and a

    unless $B$ multiplies it. This removes the diffusion and reduces $\nabla p$ to

  \item The density noise. The same contraction turns \cref{eq:noise-correlators} into
    \cref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation}.
  \item The $\Q$ equation. Multiply \cref{eq:final-langevin} by $\hat\Q(\theta)$ and

\subsection{Construction}
\label{app:closure-maxent}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The moments to close: the angular averages in the $\Q$ equation and in its noise
    correlators, written as integrals over $\theta$.
  \item The maximum-entropy distribution at given $\rho$ and $\Q$: a von Mises
    distribution in $2\theta$, with concentration $\kappa$ fixed by
    $I_1(\kappa)/I_0(\kappa)=S$.
  \item The tensor basis of the moments: $\I$, $\Q$, $\J$ and the higher tensors
    $\U(\Q)$ and $\W(\Q)$.
  \item The exact moments, as ratios of Bessel functions of $\kappa$.
  \item The small-$S$ expansion, $\la\hat\Q\hat\Q\ra_\varrho\simeq\rho\J$ and
    $\la\hat\Q'\hat\Q'\ra_\varrho\simeq4\rho\J$, accurate to relative $O(S^2)$. This is
    the closure used in \cref{eq:model-Q}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Realizability of the noise covariance}
\label{app:realizability}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The covariance of $(\bEta_\rho,\bEta^\Q,\f^Q)$ is a Gram matrix of angular
    functions weighted by $\varrho$. It is positive semi-definite for every genuine
    distribution, and depends on it only through the harmonics $n\le6$.
  \item The truncated closure breaks this. Its covariance is positive semi-definite only
    for $S\le S_\star=(\sqrt5-1)/2\simeq0.618$. The defect steady state, with
    $S_0=0.9$, exceeds $S_\star$ over most of the domain.
  \item What this affects. 
  The analytic result of \cref{sec:defect-fluct} does not use
    the closure, since \cref{eq:eta-rho-autocorrelation} is exact. The Lyapunov solver of
    \cref{app:lyapunov} does.
  \item The fix. The exact moments restore positivity at every $S$, at the cost of one
    scalar root-find per grid point. \st{A table of the Bessel-function ratios at
    $S=0.70$, $0.81$, $0.89$}. Whether the solver of \cref{app:lyapunov} uses the exact
    moments or the truncated closure.
\end{itemize}

This appendix supports \cref{sec:defect-steady}: it is the numerical route to the
deterministic steady state $\rho_\ss$, $\Q_\ss$ and to the drift speed $u$, together
with the closed-form companion solution quoted there.

Dropping the $\nabla\H$ terms as in \cref{sec:model-closure}, regrouping the remaining
local terms of \cref{eq:model-Q} into $\Ha$ of \cref{eq:H-active}, and substituting
$\partial_t\to\partial_t-(\u\cdot\nabla)$, the noiseless steady state solves
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:comoving-equations}
\begin{align}
    0 ={}& \u\!\cdot\!\nabla\rho + \frac1{\xi_0}\nabla\!\cdot\!
    \bigl(B\nabla\rho - \fa\bigr),
    \\
    0 ={}& \nabla\!\cdot\!\Bigl[\Q\Bigl(\u+\frac B{\xi_0}\nabla\rho\Bigr)\Bigr]
    + \frac4{\xir}\Ha .
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The frame velocity $\u$ is not available in closed form. It is fixed implicitly, by
requiring that the defect core stay pinned at the origin, and enters the \prompt{discretized $\rightarrow$ numerical} discretized
problem as the Lagrange multiplier conjugate to that constraint. Both fields are held at
their far-field values on the boundary of the computational domain, a Dirichlet condition
on $\Q$ and on $\rho$ alike, which corresponds to a window cut from a larger colony. The
sealed-wall alternative and what changes under it are given below and in
\cref{app:lyapunov}.

\Cref{eq:comoving-equations} is solved by finite differences on a graded, non-uniform
grid, fourth order in the local spacing and refined toward the defect core.
\begin{itemize}
