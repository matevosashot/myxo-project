\title{Fluctuating active nematics account for layer formation in \textit{Myxococcus xanthus} colonies}

\author{Ashot Matevosyan}
\author{Ricard Alert}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Layer formation in colonies of the bacterium \textit{Myxococcus xanthus} -- cells
leaving an otherwise flat monolayer to form a second layer -- localizes at $+1/2$
topological defects in the cell-orientation field \cite{copenhagen2021}, and the same
monolayers show traction-force fluctuations large compared with the mean active stress
\cite{han2025local}. We propose a mechanism: microscopic fluctuations, in particular
the reversals with which \textit{M.\ xanthus} cells glide, drive layer formation at
these defects, and we carry it from a microscopic model of reversing self-propelled
rods to a quantitative comparison with experiment. Coarse-graining the microscopic
model by Dean's method \cite{dean1996} keeps the noise term that a posited continuum
active-nematic theory, such as \cite{marchetti2013hydrodynamics}, would drop, yielding
fluctuating active-nematic equations in which the reversal rate sets the mean active
stress and the noise amplitude together rather than independently. Solved at a $+1/2$
defect -- analytically for the density fluctuations, numerically for the full field, in
agreement with one another -- these equations give a pressure variance that peaks a few
micrometers off the defect core, turning a fixed nucleation threshold into a
position-dependent crossing probability. The predicted enhancement of the layering
probability near a defect relative to the far field, $\sim2\times10^2$, matches the
first-bin value of Fig.~3f in Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} within its reported error bar.
We state plainly what this match does and does not establish: its amplitude is a fit in
two parameters (the bulk modulus and the noise correlation length), while its spatial
shape -- the decay length, the off-core peak, the core suppression by symmetry -- is
fixed with no free parameters and is what a competing mechanism would also have to
reproduce.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% Pages: ~1.5. Source: new. structure.md \S1.
% ============================================================
Bacterial monolayers growing on a soft or wet substrate behave, at the coarse-grained
level, as dry active nematics: elongated, self-propelled cells pack into a
two-dimensional layer whose orientational order organizes into $+1/2$ and $-1/2$
topological defects. When the colony can no longer expand in the plane -- crowded,
starved, or simply out of substrate -- growth is redirected out of it, and cells are
extruded into a second layer. This escape from two dimensions to three is layer
formation, and in \textit{Myxococcus xanthus} it does not happen uniformly across the
colony.

Two experimental observations fix where to look. First, layer-formation events localize
at $+1/2$ topological defects, not at $-1/2$ defects or in the bulk of the monolayer
\cite{copenhagen2021}. Second, the same monolayers show traction-force fluctuations
large compared with the mean active stress \cite{han2025local}. Together these point
toward a fluctuation-driven mechanism centered on the defect core, where the coupling
between cell orientation and density is strongest.

A deterministic active-nematic theory cannot, on its own, answer the question these two
observations pose. Such a theory returns a stress field, a fixed quantity, while layer
formation is observed as a probability that varies systematically with distance from a
defect; casting the prediction in those terms is not an embellishment added after the
fact, it is what is required to state the comparison with experiment at all. Nor can the
fluctuations be added as a generic afterthought. Coupling noise to a deterministic
active-nematic theory through the fluctuation-dissipation theorem is not available here,
because FDT presupposes a system relaxing toward thermal equilibrium at some
temperature, and an active nematic driven by self-propulsion has no such equilibrium to
relax the noise term to. The noise instead has to be obtained from the microscopic
active process itself -- in this case, from the same cell reversals that generate the
mean active stress -- carried through coarse-graining by Dean's method
(\S\ref{sec:model-coarse}) rather than posited at the continuum level by an equilibrium
argument. This is why the coarse-graining that follows is the paper's method, not an
optional derivation of a result that could have been guessed at the continuum level.

We propose a mechanism for layer formation in \textit{M.\ xanthus}: microscopic
fluctuations, and in particular cell reversals, drive layer formation at $+1/2$ defects.
We carry this mechanism end to end, from a microscopic model of self-propelled
reversing rods (\S\ref{sec:model}) through the fluctuating active-nematic equations that
coarse-graining produces from it, to the steady state and fluctuation spectrum at a
$+1/2$ defect (\S\ref{sec:defect}), to a quantitative comparison with the observed
layering rate (\S\ref{sec:nucleation}). We do not claim this mechanism is the only one
that could account for the data -- the measured decay with distance from the defect is
a simple function, and a simple function does not by itself exclude other explanations.
What we show is sufficiency: that a minimal, explicitly simplified microscopic model,
carried through without ad hoc additions at the continuum level, accounts for the
observed pattern quantitatively, magnitude included.

Section~\ref{sec:model} derives the fluctuating active-nematic equations, ending in a
single boxed pair of coupled stochastic partial differential equations for the density
and nematic order parameter; Fig.~\ref{fig:phenomenon} sketches the phenomenon and the
model. Section~\ref{sec:defect} solves these equations at a $+1/2$ defect, first for the
deterministic steady state and then for the fluctuations around it. Section
\ref{sec:nucleation} turns the resulting fluctuation statistics into a nucleation
threshold and a layering probability, and compares the predicted enhancement near a
defect with the measurement of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}. Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
states what that comparison does and does not establish, and gives a falsifiable
prediction that follows from the same mechanism.

\subsection{The microscopic model}
\label{sec:model-micro}
% dean.tex 109-173 ("Microscopic equations of motion").
We start from Langevin equations for $N$ overdamped, self-propelled rods
\cite{cugliandolo2015,bertin2013}, each with position $\r_i$ and orientation $\theta_i$:
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:langevin-position}
    \frac{\d\r_i}{\d t} &= \frac{\rho_0}{\xi_0}\F_i + v_0\,\p_i k_i,
    \\
    \label{eq:langevin-angle}
    \frac{\d\theta_i}{\d t} &= \frac{\rho_0}{\xir}\Gamma_i + \lambda_i,
\end{align}
with $\p_i=(\cos\theta_i,\sin\theta_i)$. Here $\xi_0/\rho_0$ and $\xir/\rho_0$ are
translational and rotational friction coefficients ($\rho_0$ a reference density
carried along for later convenience), $\F_i$ and $\Gamma_i$ are the force and torque
exerted by the other rods through a pairwise interaction potential, and $\lambda_i$ is
Gaussian white rotational noise, $\la\lambda_i(t)\lambda_j(t')\ra=2\Lambda\delta_{ij}
\delta(t-t')$. The second term of \eqref{eq:langevin-position} is self-propulsion at
speed $v_0$ along the rod axis, and it carries the feature that sets this model apart:
$k_i$ is not a fixed sign but a dichotomous noise, $k_i=\pm1$ with equal probability,
switching at rate $\frev$,
\begin{equation}
    \la k_i(t)k_j(t')\ra = \delta_{ij}\,\ee^{-2\frev|t-t'|}.
\end{equation}
This is how the periodic reversal of \textit{Myxococcus xanthus} gliding motility
enters the model. On timescales long compared with $\frev^{-1}$ the reversal noise is
well approximated as Gaussian white noise, $\la k_i(t)k_j(t')\ra \approx
\frev^{-1}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t')$, the approximation used throughout the
coarse-graining of \S\ref{sec:model-coarse} \prompt{use cref package}.

Reversing at a finite rate does more than randomize the direction of self-propulsion.
Expanding \eqref{eq:langevin-position} to the order at which the fast reversal noise
contributes a finite effect (an It\^o-type term, using $(k_i\,\d t)^2=\frev^{-1}\d t$;
Appendix~A.1 \prompt{use labels for references, in all places}) generates a systematic active-stress contribution with coefficient
\prompt{point to active nematic equation (the density equation) to show the coefficient}
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:zeta}
    \zeta = \frac{\xi_0v_0^2}{4\frev},
\end{equation}
which appears below as the coupling between orientation and density.

$\zeta$ matter for what follows. It depends on $v_0$ and $\xi_0$ only through their
ratio to $\frev$, so a change in reversal rate moves it directly -- doubling $\frev$
halves $\zeta$. And $\frev$ is not done setting scales once $\zeta$ is fixed:}\prompt{do not use trifial explanations, this is clear from the formula}
the same
fast-reversal expansion that produces \eqref{eq:zeta} also fixes the amplitude of the

\subsection{Coarse-graining with Dean's method }
\label{sec:model-coarse}
\prompt{In general, do not use Dean's method. It is not recognized as a method in the field. "Dean's equation" is a better term. We apply similar derivation of the dean's equation to our model.}

% dean.tex 174-359 ("Dean's approach").
Dean's method \cite{dean1996} turns the $N$-particle system of \S\ref{sec:model-micro}
into an \emph{exact} field-theoretic Langevin equation, without approximation beyond
the Gaussian reversal-noise limit already taken. Writing the single-particle density
$\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta)=\delta(\r-\r_i(t))\delta(\theta-\theta_i(t))$ and summing
over particles defines the (dimensionless, $\rho_0$-normalized) angular density
$\varrho_t(\r,\theta)=\rho_0^{-1}\sum_i\varrho^{(i)}_t(\r,\theta)$; applying It\^o's
lemma to $\varrho^{(i)}_t$ and summing over $i$ gives a closed equation for $\varrho_t$
alone (Appendix~A.2),
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:final-langevin}
    \begin{aligned}
    \partial_t\varrho_t(\r,\theta) = {}&
    \frac1{\xi_0}\pd{}{\r}\!\cdot\!\left(\varrho_t\pd{}{\r}\frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho}\right)
    + \frac1{\xir}\pd{}{\theta}\!\left(\varrho_t\pd{}{\theta}\frac{\delta\calF}{\delta\varrho}\right)
    \\
    &+ \frac{2\zeta}{\xi_0}\,p_\alpha(\theta)p_\beta(\theta)\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{r_\alpha\partial r_\beta}
    + \Lambda\,\pd{^2\varrho_t}{\theta^2}
    \\
    &- \pd{}{\r}\!\cdot\!\left(\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\p(\theta)\,k(\r,\theta,t)\right)
    - \pd{}{\theta}\!\left(\sqrt{\varrho_t}\,\lambda(\r,\theta,t)\right),
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\calF[\varrho]$ the pair free energy built from the microscopic interaction
potential, and $k(\r,\theta,t)$, $\lambda(\r,\theta,t)$ delta-correlated noise fields
inherited from $k_i$, $\lambda_i$.

\todo{This paragraph will be improved later, better discussion of dean's equation}Two things distinguish \eqref{eq:final-langevin} from
a hydrodynamic active-nematic theory written down directly at the continuum level, e.g.\
\cite{marchetti2013hydrodynamics}. First, it is a genuine Langevin equation for a
stochastic field, not an equation for an ensemble average: the last line is a noise
term, not a correction to one, and it is multiplicative -- its amplitude is set by
$\varrho_t$ itself. Second, that noise term is not put in by hand. It is what remains,
after coarse-graining, of the same particle-level reversal noise of
\S\ref{sec:model-micro}: nothing is added at the continuum level, and nothing beyond the
Gaussian reversal-noise approximation is thrown away. A naive continuum limit --
replacing the discrete sum over particles by its mean-field average from the start --
would drop this term entirely and return a deterministic equation; positing the
continuum equations of motion directly, as is standard practice for active nematics,
has the same effect, since there is then no microscopic noise source left to carry
through. Either route removes precisely the ingredient \S\ref{sec:intro} argued cannot
be reconstructed afterward by an equilibrium (FDT) argument.

The noise correlator inherited from the particle-level dichotomous reversal noise is
(Appendix~A.2)
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:k-correlator}
    \la k(\r,\theta,t)\,k(\r',\theta',t')\ra = \frac{4\zeta}{\xi_0\rho_0}\,
    \delta(t-t')\,\delta(\r-\r')\,\delta(\theta-\theta'),
\end{equation}
and it carries the same coefficient $\zeta$ that fixed the mean active stress in
\S\ref{sec:model-micro}. \st{This is the payoff of coarse-graining rather than of the
microscopic model alone:} one measured biological parameter, the reversal rate $\frev$,
controls the mean active stress \emph{and} the amplitude of the fluctuations around it.
It can only be stated this way once both halves -- $\zeta$ in \S\ref{sec:model-micro}
and the correlator \eqref{eq:k-correlator} here -- actually exist.

\subsection{Linearization and the fluctuation spectrum}
\label{sec:defect-fluct}

Let $\delta\rho(\r,t)=\rho(\r,t)-\rho_\ss(\r)$ and $\delta\Q(\r,t)=\Q(\r,t)-\Q_\ss(\r)$.
Linearizing the comoving-frame equations \eqref{eq:comoving-equations} around the
steady state of \S\ref{sec:defect-steady}, and restoring the noise dropped there, gives
(Appendix~A.4)
\begin{subequations}
    \label{eq:linearized}
\begin{align}
    \partial_t\delta\rho ={}& \frac B{\xi_0}\nabla^2\delta\rho
    + \frac\zeta{\xi_0}\nabla\nabla\!:\!\delta\Q + f_\rho(\r,t),
    \label{eq:linearized-rho}
    \\
    \partial_t\delta\Q ={}& \frac B{\xi_0}\nabla\!\cdot\!(\Q_\ss\nabla\delta\rho)
    + \nabla\!\cdot\!(\delta\Q\,\bs w_\ss) + \frac4{\xir}\delta\H_\mathrm{active}
    \nonumber\\
    &+ \f^Q(\r,t),
    \label{eq:linearized-Q}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

$\delta\H_\mathrm{active}$ the linearization of \eqref{eq:H-active} at $\Q_\ss$. This is
a linear stochastic field theory on the spatially varying background $(\rho_\ss,\Q_\ss)$:

discretizing $\delta\rho$ and $\delta\Q$ together on the grid of \S\ref{sec:defect-steady}

\begin{align}
    \label{eq:total-gauss}
    \la\delta\rho(\r)^2\ra_\ss = {}& \frac\zeta{2\pi B\rho_0\,\elln^2}
    \nonumber\\
    &+ \frac\zeta{8\pi B\rho_0}\int\!\frac{\d^2\bs p}{(2\pi)^2}\,

\end{align}

term. The first, $\zeta/(2\pi B\rho_0\elln^2)$, is spatially uniform: it is the price of
resolving conserved shot noise at scale $\elln$, and it says nothing about the defect.
The second term is what makes the defect visible in the variance. It is a
\emph{filtered double divergence} of the steady nematic field, with filter
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:filter-asymptotics}
    \hat\Phi_{\elln}(p) = \tfrac12E_1\!\left(\frac{p^2\elln^2}8\right)
    \;\longrightarrow\;
    \begin{cases}
    \ln\dfrac1{p\elln} + \text{const}, & p\elln\ll1,
    \\[4pt]
    \dfrac4{p^2\elln^2}\ee^{-p^2\elln^2/8}, & p\elln\gg1,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

only logarithmically; structure at or finer than the resolution scale is cut off
exponentially -- genuinely invisible rather than merely down-weighted. The filter's
simplicity is not an assumption but a cancellation: writing the anisotropic term as an
integral over an internal wavevector $\bs q$ and an external one $\bs p$ conjugate to
the slow variation of $\Q_\ss$, the part of the $\bs q$-integral quadratic in $\bs q$
vanishes exactly by isotropy against the traceless $\Q_\ss$, and the exponential the
remaining $\bs q$-integral produces is exactly cancelled by the external smoothing
factor, leaving the bare exponential integral of \eqref{eq:filter-asymptotics} with no
further approximation.

\subsection{Energetics of layer formation}
\label{sec:nucleation-energetics}
% dean.tex 1284-1360 (main-text portion only; full ranges to Appendix F.3).

% \prompt{The next paragraph is too short. "section_nucleation/Report 1.pdf" might be useful to improve it}
Forming a second layer of cells, of area $A$ and perimeter $\ell$, costs energy to
detach cells from the substrate, from their neighbors, and to work against the surface
tension of the wetting water film, while it gains energy from adhesion to the layer
below and from relaxing the elastic strain of the compressed monolayer:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:virtual-work}
    \Delta E = \Ws A + \Wc\ell h + \gammaw\ell h - \Wc A + \Delta E_e,
\end{equation}
with $\Ws$, $\Wc$ the cell-substrate and cell-cell adhesion energies per unit area, $h$
the height of a cell layer, $\gammaw$ the surface tension of water, and $\Delta E_e$ the
change in elastic strain energy of the monolayer. 
\st{Only two combinations of these
quantities, $(\gammaw+\Wc)$ and $(\Ws-\Wc)$, enter the results below}
(\S\ref{sec:nucleation-theory}); none of the individual adhesion energies has been
measured for \textit{M.\ xanthus}, and Appendix~F.3 gives the full literature ranges and
a one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis. Table~\ref{tab:used-values} lists the values used
here.

\subsection{Nucleation theory with a fluctuating barrier}
\label{sec:nucleation-theory}
% Merges dean.tex 1361-1390 ("The nucleation threshold") with the pressure-statistics
% opening of dean.tex 1392-1487 ("Layering probability") -- the deterministic threshold
% and the fluctuation statistics it is measured against belong in one subsection, since
% the section title now names the fluctuating barrier explicitly.
Equation~\eqref{eq:virtual-work} has the structure of classical nucleation theory once
written as a function of the nucleus area: a line cost growing as the perimeter and a
bulk gain growing as the area, with $\Delta E_e=-pA$ the work done by a local monolayer
pressure $p$ and $\ell=\sqrt{4\pi A}$ for a circular patch,
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dE-of-A}
    \Delta E(A) = (\Ws-\Wc-p)\,A + (\Wc+\gammaw)\,h\sqrt{4\pi A}.
\end{equation}
This rises as $\sqrt A$ at small $A$, turns over, and falls once the area term
dominates; the turning point is the classical critical nucleus.

\prompt{The following is unncessary}
% The natural-looking condition, that the barrier be abolished outright,
% $\Delta E(\Acell)\le0$, is far too strong: at the surface tensions of
% Table~\ref{tab:used-values} it would require a pressure some three orders of magnitude
% larger than is physically plausible. 
The \st{correct} condition is \st{weaker, and} follows from
the discreteness of the system rather than from its continuum limit. There is no way to
assemble a nucleus smaller than one cell, so \eqref{eq:dE-of-A} is meaningful only down
to $A\simeq\Acell$; once the \emph{maximum} of $\Delta E(A)$ has descended to that size,
growth from the smallest object that can exist is downhill, and ordinary single-cell
perturbations -- thermal motion, substrate roughness, a division event -- complete the
extrusion. This is the essential move of the mechanism: the fluctuating quantity is not
the size of the nucleus but the height and position of the barrier itself. The
nucleation condition is therefore
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:nucleation-condition}
    \Delta E'(A)\big|_{A=\Acell} = 0
    % \qquad\text{rather than}\qquad
    % \Delta E(\Acell) = 0,
\end{equation}
and it is what makes a near-water surface tension compatible with observable extrusion
at all: differentiating \eqref{eq:dE-of-A} and solving gives the critical pressure
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pcrit}
    \pcrit = (\Ws-\Wc) + \sqrt{\frac{\pi h^2}{\Acell}}\,(\Wc+\gammaw),
\end{equation}
which with Table~\ref{tab:used-values} evaluates to $\pcrit=3.5\times10^4~\Pa\cdot
\micron$. Note what $\pcrit$ does \emph{not} contain: no $B$, no $\elln$, no property of
the fluctuations at all. It is fixed entirely by adhesion, surface tension, and cell
geometry; everything from \S\ref{sec:defect-fluct} enters only through the statistics
of $p$, which we turn to now.

The local pressure follows from the free energy density as $p=\rho\mathfrak f'-
\mathfrak f$, which for \eqref{eq:free-energy-choice} and $\rho$ near unity is
$p\simeq B(\rho-1) = B\,\delta\rho$. Since $\delta\rho$ is Gaussian with the variance of
\eqref{eq:total-gauss}, $p$ is Gaussian too, with
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sigma-p}
    \sigma_p^2(\r) = B^2\sigmarho(\r),
    \qquad
    \sigma_{p,\infty}^2 = \frac{B\zeta}{2\pi\rho_0\elln^2},
\end{equation}
the second expression its far-field value, from the isotropic pedestal of
\eqref{eq:total-gauss}. 
\prompt{I need simpler explanation for ignoring mean pressure. What is here now is confusing, you talk about pressure but state density variance etc.}
The mean pressure is negligible against this:
$\rho_\ss-1=O(\zeta/B)$, two orders of magnitude below $\sqrt{\sigmarho}$ at the
parameters used here, so it is the fluctuations, not the mean compression, that carry
the signal below. \emph{This is the sense in which the barrier is fluctuating}: $\pcrit$
of \eqref{eq:pcrit} is a fixed number, set by adhesion and geometry alone, but the
pressure that must exceed it is a stochastic field whose variance is spatially
modulated by the defect through \eqref{eq:total-gauss}. Extrusion is not driven by a
deterministic pressure drift toward $\pcrit$; it happens where and when a fluctuation
crosses a fixed threshold, and the probability of that at a point $\r$ is the Gaussian
tail
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:p-layer}
    p_\text{layer}(\r) &= \mathbb P\bigl(p(\r)>\pcrit\bigr)
    \simeq \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\,u(\r)}\ee^{-u(\r)^2/2},
    \\
    u(\r) &= \frac{\pcrit}{\sigma_p(\r)}.
\end{align}
Evaluating this probability, and comparing it with the measured layering rate, is the
subject of \S\ref{sec:nucleation-results}.

\subsection{Nucleation probability: results and comparison with experiment}
\label{sec:nucleation-results}
% dean.tex 1392-1487 (remainder). Hero figure lives here.
The comparison with experiment is not to an absolute rate but to a \emph{ratio}:
Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021} reports the distribution of distances between defects and new
layers, normalized by the same distribution for random points in the monolayer (their
Fig.~3f). Because the attempt frequency $\lambda\propto p_\text{layer}/\tau_c$, with
$\tau_c$ essentially position-independent, that ratio equals the ratio of layering
probabilities up to a slowly varying prefactor, and \eqref{eq:p-layer} gives
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:R-ratio}
    \frac{p_\text{layer}(\r)}{p_\text{layer}(\infty)} \simeq
    \exp\!\left[\frac{u_\infty^2}2\left(1-\frac{\sigma_{p,\infty}^2}{\sigma_p^2(\r)}\right)\right],
    \qquad u_\infty \equiv \frac{\pcrit}{\sigma_{p,\infty}}.
\end{equation}
Only the \emph{relative} modulation of the variance around the defect matters, and it is
amplified by the large prefactor $u_\infty^2/2$ in the exponent -- the reason a
modulation of only a few percent produces an enhancement of two orders of magnitude, and
the sense in which layer formation is exponentially sensitive to the defect texture.

At the parameters of Table~\ref{tab:used-values}, $\sigma_{p,\infty}=1.8\times10^3~
\Pa\cdot\micron$ and $u_\infty=19.1$. Figure~\ref{fig:hero}a shows the resulting
variance modulation, $\sigmarho(\r)/\sigmarho(\infty)$ from \eqref{eq:total-gauss}: a
dipole that vanishes at the core by symmetry (\S\ref{sec:defect-fluct}) and peaks about
$1.5~\micron$ away, at $\nu_0\equiv[\sigmarho(\r_\mathrm{max})/\sigmarho(\infty)]-1
=3.1\%$ at the operating resolution $\elln=0.5~\micron$. Equation~\eqref{eq:R-ratio}
then gives a predicted enhancement
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:R-value}
    \frac{p_\text{layer}(\r_\mathrm{max})}{p_\text{layer}(\infty)}
    = \exp\!\left[\frac{u_\infty^2}2\,\frac{\nu_0}{1+\nu_0}\right] \simeq 2.3\times10^2,
\end{equation}
against a first-bin value of $\simeq2\times10^2$ in Fig.~3f of Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021},
whose reported error bar spans $40$--$500$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:hero}b). The two agree within
that error bar.

\prompt{B is the only fitting parameter, l_n is set to the lowest lengtscale of the system, width of the bacteria. It is a lower lengthscale cutoff}
\textbf{What this match is, and is not.} It is a fit in \emph{two} parameters, $B$ and
$\elln$, not one: both enter \eqref{eq:R-value} only through the combination
$\pcrit^2\elln^2\nu_0(\elln)/(B\zeta)$ (Appendix~F.3), and because $\nu_0$ is itself
close to linear in $\elln$ near the operating point, the logarithm of the predicted
ratio scales roughly as $\elln^3$ -- halving $\elln$ from its operating value takes the
ratio below $2$, and raising it by $40\%$ takes it past $10^6$. The agreement in
Fig.~\ref{fig:hero}b should be read as a fit of that combination, not as an independent
prediction of its amplitude. What \emph{is} free of fitting is the \emph{shape} of the
curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:hero}: the spatial profile of $\sigmarho(\r)$ is the

parameter once $\Q_\ss$ is known -- the decay over several micrometers, the peak at

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% Pages ~1.5. New. structure.md \S4, four closing moves.
% ============================================================
\textbf{What this does and does not establish.} The measured decay of layer-formation
probability with distance from a $+1/2$ defect is a simple function, and a simple
function does not by itself exclude other mechanisms. What we claim is sufficiency, not
uniqueness: a minimal, explicitly simplified microscopic model of self-propelled
reversing rods, carried through coarse-graining, closure, and linearization without ad
hoc additions at any continuum step, accounts for the observed pattern -- both its shape
and, at a two-parameter fit, its amplitude.

\textbf{A falsifiable prediction.} Because $\zeta\propto1/\frev$
(\S\ref{sec:model-micro}) and the coarse-grained noise amplitude is also
$\propto1/\frev$ (\S\ref{sec:model-coarse}), and because the amplitude fit of
\S\ref{sec:nucleation-results} already fixes $\pcrit$ and the combination
$B\zeta/\elln^2$ appearing in \eqref{eq:R-value}, the mechanism proposed here predicts
how the layer-formation enhancement near a defect should change with the cell reversal
rate, with no further free parameters once $\frev$ is changed. \textit{Myxococcus
xanthus} reversal-frequency mutants (\textit{frz}) are standard tools in the field;
testing this prediction against them is a natural next step, though not one attempted
here.

\textbf{A coincidence, not a claim.} The fitted bulk modulus, $B=2\times10^5~
\Pa\cdot\micron$, lands close to the effective cell modulus independently fitted to the
mono-to-multilayer transition in \textit{E.\ coli} \cite{grant2014} (Appendix~F.3) -- a
different organism, undergoing the same \emph{kind} of transition. It is worth noting;
it is not corroboration, since the two systems have not been connected by any argument
beyond the coincidence itself.

\textbf{Limitations.} Several approximations, each stated plainly where it was
introduced, are collected here rather than left scattered. The comoving-frame velocity
$u$ is not fully converged in box size (\S\ref{sec:defect-steady}). The fourth-order
Onsager-conjugate terms are dropped from both field equations in order to solve for the
steady state (\S\ref{sec:defect-steady}), and the main analytic line of
\S\ref{sec:defect-fluct} sets $\delta\Q=0$, checked instead against the full numerical
solution of Appendix~E. The small-order-parameter closure of \S\ref{sec:model-closure}
loses positive semi-definiteness of the noise covariance above $S_\star\simeq0.618$, a
bound the defect steady state exceeds over most of its domain (Appendix~C); this affects
the full numerical fluctuation solver of Appendix~E more than the $\delta\Q=0$ analytic
result of \S\ref{sec:defect-fluct}, whose density-noise correlator does not depend on
the closure, but it has not been fully resolved by the exact closure alternative
available there. And the amplitude match of \S\ref{sec:nucleation-results} is, again, a
fit in two parameters, exponentially sensitive to both -- worth repeating here, since a
limitations list that omitted the paper's most fitting-dependent result would read as
evasive. Neither $B$ nor the adhesion and tension parameters of
\S\ref{sec:nucleation-energetics} have been measured for \textit{M.\ xanthus}.

A $-1/2$-defect comparison was considered, during the development of this work, as a
second and more stringent test of the mechanism, since $-1/2$ defects do not self-propel
and were not observed to nucleate layers in Ref.~\cite{copenhagen2021}; we set it aside
as outside the scope of this paper, and rely instead on the $\frev$ prediction above for
falsifiability.
